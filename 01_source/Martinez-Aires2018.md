## Abstract ^sec-abstract

Occupational health and safety (OHS) in building construction remains a worldwide problem in workplace injury, illness and fatality statistics. Safety requires care and planning across the project lifecycle, from design through maintenance. Early attempts addressed safety only at the design phase or through manual processes in execution. BIM is now growing rapidly in construction operations, planning, management and safety management. The paper systematically reviews the existing literature on BIM and construction safety to extract useful findings and knowledge gaps. The main result is that the growing implementation of BIM in the AEC industry is changing how safety can be approached. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro

The authors frame construction as one of the most hazardous sectors and argue that safety management must span the full project lifecycle rather than be confined to either design or execution silos. ^p1

They identify two historical strands — design-phase prevention and manual execution-phase processes — and argue that BIM offers an integrative platform that can connect them. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method

1. Systematic literature search on BIM and construction safety. ^p1
2. Screening and selection of contributions concerned with safety management across project phases. ^p2
3. Thematic synthesis distinguishing BIM uses in design-phase prevention, planning, execution and maintenance. ^p3

## Key Findings ^sec-results

- BIM adoption in AEC is changing how construction safety is conceptualised and operationalised. ^f1
- Literature contributions concentrate in recent years, reflecting accelerating BIM-safety integration. ^f2
- Safety care must be planned throughout the project lifecycle, from design to maintenance, not only at single phases. ^f3
- Gaps remain on the integration between BIM-based safety planning and execution-phase realities. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion

The review converts safety from a phase-bounded compliance task into a lifecycle property carried by the BIM model, foreshadowing the role a cognitive DT could play in propagating safety state across phases. ^p1

The identified design-to-execution integration gap is exactly the gap a runtime, sensor-fed DT layer is positioned to close. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion

BIM is shifting AEC safety management toward a model-centred, lifecycle-spanning approach, but execution-phase integration and runtime safety feedback remain open. ^p1
