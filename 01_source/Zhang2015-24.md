## Abstract ^sec-abstract
Safety and productivity in construction are often degraded by congested site conditions, and there is no formalized approach to activity-level planning that proactively avoids workspace congestion. The paper investigates and prototypes a BIM-enabled approach to activity-level site planning that improves construction safety. It establishes automated workspace visualization in BIM using remote sensing and workspace modeling: GPS data loggers were attached to hardhats of a crew constructing cast-in-place concrete columns; novel algorithms extract activity-specific workspace parameters from the recorded location data; workspaces are then visualized on a BIM platform to detect potential workspace conflicts among competing crews or between material-lifting equipment. The method supports engineers, planners, managers, foremen, supervisors, and workers in proactive safety planning. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro
Site congestion and overlapping workspaces are leading causes of struck-by, caught-between, and other contact-mode injuries, yet pre-construction planning rarely models the *dynamic spatial footprint* that each activity actually claims on site. ^p1

The authors argue that ubiquitous positioning (GPS/RTLS) plus BIM can close this gap: real workforce trajectories become data from which activity-level workspaces can be empirically inferred and then reasoned over within the BIM environment. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method
1. **Field instrumentation.** GPS data loggers are mounted on hardhats of a work crew constructing cast-in-place concrete columns, capturing worker trajectories during real activities. ^p1
2. **Workspace extraction algorithms.** Novel algorithms convert raw position data into activity-specific workspace parameters (spatial extent, occupancy patterns, temporal footprint). ^p2
3. **BIM visualization and conflict detection.** Inferred workspaces are visualized on a BIM platform and checked for spatial/temporal conflicts among competing crews and between crews and material-lifting equipment. ^p3
4. **Stakeholder use cases.** The method is positioned to support engineers, planners, construction managers, foremen, site supervisors, and workers in activity-level safety planning. ^p4

## Key Findings ^sec-results
- Activity-specific workspace footprints can be empirically inferred from GPS-tracked workforce trajectories, rather than assumed from rules of thumb. ^f1
- Visualizing inferred workspaces in BIM enables detection of workspace conflicts among competing crews and with material-lifting equipment. ^f2
- The approach yields an activity-level, proactive safety-planning capability that traditional schedule-only planning cannot deliver. ^f3
- Demonstrated on a real cast-in-place column crew, providing field evidence rather than only simulation. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion
The paper is one of the earliest concrete demonstrations that *physical-to-digital* feedback — empirical worker location data flowing back into BIM-based planning — can revise safety planning assumptions. It is therefore an early instance of the backward-causal-update pattern the SLR formalizes in §5. ^p1

It also exemplifies the SLR's "data-centric" maturity: the BIM model is enriched with empirically observed spatial data rather than only design-time assumptions, increasing realism but introducing data-quality and synchronization concerns. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion
GPS-based workforce tracking integrated with BIM workspace visualization provides a practical, activity-level safety-planning capability that addresses workspace congestion before it becomes incidents. ^p1
