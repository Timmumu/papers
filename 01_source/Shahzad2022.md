## Abstract ^sec-abstract
The concept of digital twins is proposed as a new technology-led advancement to support the processes of the design, construction, and operation of built assets. Commonalities between the emerging definitions of digital twins describe them as digital or cyber environments that are bidirectionally-linked to their physical or real-life replica to enable simulation and data-centric decision making. Studies have started to investigate their role in the built environment, but the field lacks a consolidated view of DT characteristics, applications and challenges. This paper provides such an investigation through a systematic review of built-environment DT studies, mapping reported applications to lifecycle stages and identifying recurrent technical and organisational challenges. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro
DT discourse has moved rapidly from manufacturing into the built environment, but published definitions diverge and reported "DTs" range from data-linked BIM viewers to integrated operational platforms. The authors argue that the field needs a structured account of what built-environment DTs *actually are*, what they are *used for*, and what *prevents* their adoption. ^p1

They position the review against the Grieves and Tao lineages and against domain-specific BIM-led work, looking for the gap between aspiration and current deployment. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method
1. Systematic literature search of built-environment DT studies in major databases, filtered by relevance and rigour. ^p1
2. Extract reported DT characteristics (data sources, integration mechanisms, decision support level) and tabulate them. ^p2
3. Classify applications by lifecycle stage (design, construction, operation, decommission) and by functional purpose (monitoring, visualisation, prediction, control). ^p3
4. Surface recurring challenges (interoperability, data quality, organisational adoption, autonomy) and report their frequency in the corpus. ^p4

## Key Findings ^sec-results
- Built-environment DT research is dominated by *monitoring and visualisation* applications; *autonomous control* is rare. ^f1
- Reported DTs typically rely on BIM + sensors with manual or semi-automated integration; closed-loop autonomy is the exception. ^f2
- Interoperability and data-quality are the most frequently reported technical challenges; organisational and skills challenges are equally important but less studied. ^f3
- The operation stage of the lifecycle is the most-studied, while design-time and decommission-time DT applications remain thin. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion
The dominance of monitoring/visualisation, and the rarity of autonomous control, indicates that built-environment DT practice is still at the Digital-Shadow end of the taxonomy. The authors call for explicit attention to the autonomy gap and to the organisational mechanisms (governance, skills) needed to operationalise higher-maturity DTs. ^p1

The lifecycle skew (operation-heavy, decommission-thin) flags an opportunity for design-time and end-of-life DT research, which our SLR also identifies as under-developed in AECO. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion
Built-environment DTs are conceptually rich but operationally shallow: most reported implementations stop at monitoring and visualisation and do not deliver the autonomous, closed-loop control that the DT definition implies. ^p1
