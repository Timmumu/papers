## Abstract ^sec-abstract
Construction projects and cities account for over 50% of carbon emissions and energy consumption. Industry 4.0 and digital transformation may increase productivity and reduce energy consumption. A digital twin (DT) is a key enabler for implementing Industry 4.0 in the areas of construction and smart cities. It is an emerging technology that connects different objects through the advanced Internet of Things (IoT). Previous digital modelling practices — data acquisition tools, human–computer–machine interfaces, programmable cities and infrastructure, and BIM — have provided digital data for construction, monitoring, or controlling physical objects. However, a DT is supposed to offer much more than digital representation: characteristics such as bi-directional, real-time data exchange between physical and virtual entities distinguish it from a digital model or digital shadow. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro
The author observes that the literature increasingly calls any data-linked 3D model a "digital twin", conflating three distinct artefacts: the Digital Model (no automatic data flow), the Digital Shadow (one-way physical → digital flow), and the Digital Twin (bidirectional, automated flow). This terminological drift muddies both research and practice. ^p1

The review situates DT within the wider Industry-4.0 / smart-city agenda and asks specifically what additional capabilities a DT must provide *beyond* BIM, sensor dashboards and digital shadows to merit the name. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method
1. Systematically review DT, digital-shadow and digital-model literature across construction and smart-city domains. ^p1
2. Identify and tabulate distinguishing characteristics (data-flow directionality, automation level, real-time capability, decision authority). ^p2
3. Propose a three-level taxonomy: Digital Model — Digital Shadow — Digital Twin, with explicit criteria for assigning a system to each level. ^p3
4. Map current built-environment implementations onto the taxonomy to expose which "DT" claims are actually digital-shadow systems. ^p4

## Key Findings ^sec-results
- The defining criterion of a DT is *bidirectional, automated, real-time* data exchange between the physical and digital twins, not the richness of the 3D model. ^f1
- Most built-environment systems labelled "DT" in the literature are in fact digital shadows, with no automated feedback into the physical asset. ^f2
- A clear taxonomy reduces semantic confusion and lets reviewers compare systems on an equal basis. ^f3
- Achieving true DT in the built environment requires resolving sustainability, data-quality and bi-directional control challenges that current implementations sidestep. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion
The taxonomy operationalises Grieves' original distinction and gives reviewers and SLR authors a defensible criterion for classifying maturity. It also exposes that the AECO field largely sits at the Digital-Shadow level despite DT branding. ^p1

By tying the discussion to carbon emissions and sustainability, the author frames the DT–digital-shadow distinction as more than a definitional debate: closed-loop feedback is what enables operational interventions that actually reduce energy consumption. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion
The paper supplies the canonical taxonomy that separates DTs from digital shadows and digital models, and shows that built-environment DT research has largely not yet crossed the bidirectional-feedback threshold. ^p1
