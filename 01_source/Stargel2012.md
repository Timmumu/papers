## Abstract ^sec-abstract
Future generations of NASA and U.S. Air Force vehicles will require lighter mass while being subjected to higher loads and more extreme service conditions over longer time periods than the present generation. Current approaches for certification, fleet management and sustainment are largely based on statistical distributions of material properties, heuristic design philosophies, physical testing and assumed similitude between testing and operational conditions — and will likely be unable to address these extreme requirements. To address the shortcomings of conventional approaches, a fundamental paradigm shift is needed: the Digital Twin, which integrates ultra-high-fidelity simulation with the vehicle's on-board Integrated Vehicle Health Management (IVHM) system, maintenance history, and all available historical and fleet data to mirror the life of its flying twin and enable unprecedented levels of safety and reliability. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro
Existing methodologies for vehicle certification, fleet management and sustainment rely on similitude and heuristic understanding of how operational and anomalous conditions affect structural health, safety and performance. A common manifestation is the "factors-of-safety" used during design and certification, which are rooted in a heuristic legacy in which a factor of 1.5 or 2.0 has historically been treated as sufficient. ^p1

The authors argue that this heuristic regime cannot serve next-generation vehicles, which will operate closer to material limits, under more extreme conditions, and for longer service lives. A fundamentally different, model-and-data-driven approach is required — and they name it the Digital Twin. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method
The paper is a *position paper* commissioned for the AIAA Special Session on the Digital Twin, not an empirical study. Its method is conceptual: ^p1
1. Diagnose the limits of similitude- and heuristic-based vehicle certification. ^p2
2. Propose the Digital Twin as an ultra-high-fidelity simulation of a *specific* vehicle, continuously updated with sensor data, maintenance history and fleet-level information. ^p3
3. Identify the technical pre-requisites (multi-physics modelling, IVHM sensing, computing, data fusion) and the institutional implications for NASA and the USAF. ^p4

## Key Findings ^sec-results
- Heuristic factors-of-safety cannot scale to next-generation aerospace performance and lifetime demands. ^f1
- A DT — defined as ultra-high-fidelity, per-vehicle, continuously updated simulation linked to IVHM — is proposed as the replacement paradigm. ^f2
- The DT integrates *historical*, *fleet* and *operational* data, not only real-time telemetry, distinguishing it from earlier model-based engineering. ^f3
- Achieving the paradigm requires advances in multi-physics simulation, sensing, computing and data fusion that exceeded 2012 state-of-the-art. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion
This paper is widely treated as the canonical aerospace articulation of the DT and is one of the most cited definitional sources in the broader DT literature. Its framing — DT as a *response* to the inadequacy of heuristic engineering practice — is precisely the framing AECO needs as it confronts the limits of BIM-as-handover. ^p1

The emphasis on per-asset fidelity, lifecycle data integration and safety-critical reliability sets a high bar that AECO DT implementations rarely meet today. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion
Glaessgen and Stargel articulate the DT as a paradigm shift required to move beyond similitude-and-heuristic vehicle certification, defining the per-asset, lifecycle-integrated, simulation-rich DT that subsequent literature has built upon. ^p1
