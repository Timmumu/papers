## Abstract ^sec-abstract

The paper describes and evaluates alternative approaches for deploying rapid manufacturing (RM) — i.e., on-demand additive production — in the spare parts supply chain. Using conceptual designs and the aircraft industry as illustration, the authors argue that distributed RM is attractive because it can simultaneously improve service and reduce inventory, but trade-offs suggest the most feasible deployment is either centralised by OEMs or close to the point of use by generalist RM service providers. RM-based service supply is, in 2010 technology terms, viable only in particular situations because of the limited part range producible. The original contribution is the identification of two distinct deployment archetypes for RM in spare parts supply. ^p1

## Introduction & Background ^sec-intro

Spare parts supply chains suffer the classic dual penalty of high inventory and poor service for slow-moving items. Rapid manufacturing promises to break this trade-off by producing on demand, but the deployment topology (who owns the printer, where it sits) is unsettled. ^p1

The paper reframes the choice as a *capacity deployment* decision rather than a per-part make-or-buy decision. ^p2

## Method ^sec-method

1. Conceptual design of alternative RM deployment configurations in the spare parts SC. ^p1
2. Illustration in the aircraft industry, where service requirements are stringent and inventory cost is high. ^p2
3. Feasibility discussion grounded in the literature on RM economics and spare-parts logistics. ^p3

## Key Findings ^sec-results

- Distributed RM deployment is attractive because it can simultaneously improve service and reduce inventory. ^f1
- Trade-offs (capability, certification, utilisation) make **centralised deployment by OEMs** or **point-of-use deployment by generalist RM service providers** the most feasible archetypes. ^f2
- The limited part range producible by RM (as of 2010) restricts feasibility to particular situations. ^f3
- OEMs should include RM in long-term service-supply-chain planning even though near-term applicability is narrow. ^f4

## Discussion & Implications ^sec-discussion

The deployment-archetype framing — centralised-OEM vs. point-of-use-generalist — anticipates analogous questions for AECO: where should the "cognitive" computation, model storage, and decision authority of a DT sit (central platform vs. project-local node)? ^p1

The paper is also an early statement that on-demand digital production changes inventory logic in service supply chains, a principle later transferred to construction spare-parts and modular component supply. ^p2

## Conclusion ^sec-conclusion

Two viable deployment archetypes for RM in spare parts supply chains are identified; OEM strategic planning should incorporate RM despite present scope limits. ^p1
