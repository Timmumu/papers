ABSTRACT Urban heatwaves pose an escalating threat to cities, requiring advanced planning strategies.
This paper presents a graph-based digital twin approach to model spatial dependencies in heatwave risk
for urban areas. Using Vienna’s city center as a case study, we integrate (i) high-resolution spatial data
from Google on heat hazard, (ii) spatial population distribution from Vienna Open Data and (iii) META ’s
Vienna population datasets alongside (iv) critical road infrastructure from OpenStreetMaps, into a graph
network model that captures cumulative risk paths that help determine the most critical city blocks needing
intervention and the optimal allocation of resources for routing first responders to reach critical locations of
sensitive populations. Our Spatial Dependency Risk Analysis identifies how the distribution of vulnerable
population needing first aid in one location together with heat data may amplify risks in connected areas
and provide a planning tool to first responders to draft optimal routes to reach all persons in need as fast
as possible. The results show how city center junctions form interdependent high-risk clusters; notably, our
tool managed to identify the most at-risk sub-district reaches and automatically plan ambulance routes from
cooling spots to high risk hotspots. To our knowledge, this is the first automatic tool able to quantify these
network routing effects during heatwave disasters, and enhance heatwave response planning by pinpointing
critical areas where targeted interventions (e.g., cooling centers or outreach) could substantially reduce
cascading impacts

II. RELATED WORK
A. URBAN HEATWAVE IMPACT
In urban and highly populated areas, such as cities and
metropolises, the heat waves are further impacted due to the
urban heat island (UHI) effect [6], which indicates a signif-
icantly larger temperature than the surrounding rural areas.
Typically, the temperature difference is higher at night than
during the day and is most apparent when the wind weakens.
The main contributor to the UHI effect is the modification of
the land areas to large blocks of concrete buildings, with sec-
ondary contributions arising from several factors, such as heat
generated by energy consumption used for air conditioning
purposes [6]. More than half of the Earth’s population lives in
urban areas, which occupy only 0.5% of the land surface [7].
The heat wave magnitude index (HWMI) proposed by [8]
led to the possibility of comparing heat waves (or extreme
heat events) in different regions of the world with different
climates. This approach provides a foundation for urban heat
risk management. In [23] several tools, such as satellite data
and GIS tools, have been used to create a comprehensive
urban heat vulnerability map – in this case for the city of
Vienna, Austria – which integrated factors such as exposure,
sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.
B. ROAD TRANSPORTATION RISK MODELS
Transportation networks play a pivotal role during heatwave
emergencies, particularly in urban areas or city centers where
traffic congestion can severely affect the rapid response of
emergency teams. Several road network simulation models
have been introduced to mathematically model the transporta-
tion systems, which are too complicated for simple analytical
or numerical analysis. These models are based on the concept
of system state, a set of variables that describe the system
state over time [9]. System state can be either discrete or con-
tinuous. The more recent methods utilize either the discrete
event simulation (a discrete series of events over time) or the
continuous-time simulation (a set of continuously changing
variables). We can identify two distinct prediction models:
the short-term, which should be precise in providing the
road network characteristics in the case of an adverse event,
and the long-term, which provides a broader road network
picture over time but with limited accuracy in abnormal or
atypical events. Enhancing critical road segments, especially
in post-disaster recovery actions, can be achieved by using
graph neural networks [28]. Furthermore, network improve-
ments and effective management strategies can be achieved
using several centrality metrics (such as betweenness central-
ity) [29] to identify critical nodes in transportation networks.
In addition, web-based decision support systems, as sug-
gested in [30], can assist in strategically placing emergency
facilities to support accessibility options during disasters or
adverse situations. In heatwave scenarios, optimizing traffic
flow becomes critical, particularly for routes connecting areas
with high-risk populations.
Our work demonstrates how spatial dependency risk anal-
ysis can be used to identify optimized response paths that
account for dependencies among urban zones that host
vulnerable populations during extreme events. To our knowl-
edge, there is currently no solution capable of calculating
cumulative risk paths that help determine the most criti-
cal areas needing intervention and the optimal allocation of
resources for first responders and detect the shortest path to
reach critical locations of sensitive populations, considering
heat exposure and population density.
The proposed digital twin methodology significantly
extends existing GIS-based emergency response models by
integrating real-time solar radiation data, population demo-
graphics, and spatial dependency analyses based on network
centrality metrics. Existing GIS methodologies primarily are
focused on vulnerability mapping.

A. DEPENDENCY ANAL YSIS METHODOLOGY
In discrete mathematics, a graph is a collection of objects
that are represented by abstractions called vertices/nodes,
and each of the related pairs is called an edge in the sense
that some pairs of the objects are related [10]. The num-
ber |V(G)| of its vertices is called the order of the graph.
Graphs can be either undirected or directed. In an undirected
graph, edges have no direction, meaning the relationship
between two vertices is bidirectional. Dependency analy-
sis assesses the risk of nth-order dependencies by applying
results of organization-level risk assessments. We define
‘‘dependency’’ as a ‘‘one-directional reliance of an asset,
system, or collection thereof on an input, interaction or
other requirement from other sources to function properly’’
[11], [12].
In our case, dependency risk is quantified at the node level,
representing the risk associated with urban junctions or city
blocks. The overall risk of a path is then derived as the
cumulative aggregation of the risks of the nodes along that
path. Each node Ni is assigned to an individual riskRNi, which
is calculated based on its likelihood and impact factors as per
the following formula (1):
RNi = LNi · INi (1)
where LNi represents the likelihood of the node experiencing
a risk, and INi quantifies the impact at that node.
For a path P = {N 0, N1, . . .,Nk } with N0 as the
source node, the cumulative dependency risk for path P is
expressed (2) as the summary of all the risks at each node
along the route:

defined as the number of nodes (or city blocks) included

Shorter paths: where rapid response and quick access to

Longer paths: where comprehensive coverage of high-risk

B. CENTRALITY METRICS
Within graph theory, a plethora of measures have been pro-
posed, especially within the context of transportation [27],
to analyze complex networks. We can identify centrality as
a quantitative measure to assess a node’s influence, signif-
icance, or importance within a network (graph). The term
‘‘importance’’ can be defined and measured from different
perspectives, depending on the context and the specific metric
used. These metrics are based on specific criteria, such as the
number of direct connections (degree centrality), closeness to
all other nodes (closeness centrality), etc.
PageRank, Betweenness and Eigenvector metrics were
selected based on their suitability to reflect diverse dimen-
sions of urban networks [4], [27]. Betweenness centrality
identifies critical nodes functioning as vital connectors
essential for maintaining emergency route accessibility [4].
Eigenvector and PageRank metrics were selected to quan-
tify nodes’ cascading influences and their roles network
wide [27]. Computationally, these centrality algorithms scale
polynomially (O(V^2) to O(V^3), where V is the num-
ber of nodes), confirming their suitability for application to
city-scale transportation networks.
The aforementioned centrality metrics effectively capture
network vulnerability and, even though they are widely used
within the domain of transportation network analysis and
thus, alone, do not represent methodological novelty, still to
the best of our knowledge, these metrics are not used before in
determining node importance for urban areas in conjunction
with heat indexes and traffic analysis.

C. RISK ANAL YSIS
In this work, we define the graph G = (N, E), where N is
the set of nodes which represent road junctions (or urban
blocks), and E is the set of directed edges which represent
roads bet-ween urban blocks/junctions. Directed edges also
depict the dependency relationship between them. Each node
is also characterized by a heat risk indexHni, calculated based
on population and real-time temperature data. The disruption
transferred through this dependency can be illustrated by
the combination of Likelihood (Li,j), which represents the
probability of heat-related risk affecting node Nj if node Ni is
impacted, and the Impact (I i,j), which quantifies the severity
of the heat-related risk at Nj due to its dependency on Ni.
IPCC (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) has
given a general definition as ‘‘the propensity or predisposi-
tion to be adversely affected’’. According to IPCC [39], the
majority of researchers define vulnerability as a function of
exposure, sensitivity and adaptive capacity, where exposure
‘‘refers to the direct danger of urban heat that affects the
considered area, and manifests as the prevalence of very
high temperatures’’, strength quantifies ‘‘human’s reaction
to high temperatures, and adaptive capacity ‘‘refers to the
ability to cope with, recover and adjust to the impacts of heat
events’’ [22].
1) THE ‘‘LIKELIHOOD’’ METRIC FOR EXTREME HEAT EVENTS
The likelihood metric is used to quantify the probability of
extreme heat exposure at each node (road junction). The
nodes used in the graph serve as geographical points where
solar radiation and population vulnerability data are to be
analyzed. The roads connecting these junctions – represented
as edges in the graph – capture other relevant factors, such as
road length.
The methodology presented utilizes satellite data provided
by the Google Solar API 6 for real-time heat index calcula-
tions to reveal areas within cities that are more exposed to
extreme temperatures. The solar radiation data, particularly
the highest recorded solar radiation value for each junction,
forms the basis for estimating the likelihood of heat vulner-
ability at each node. Higher solar radiation at a junction (an
accumulation of solar radiation data for the surrounding area)
indicates more significant exposure to heat, thus increasing
the likelihood of heat-related risks. The more intense the
solar radiation is, the more likely that node (junction) will
be exposed to heat extremes.

A ‘heatwave risk score’ is calculated by normalizing, scaling,
and weighting data for likelihood and impact metrics above.
Normalization for both metrics involves dividing observed

We chose to use the ‘‘geometric mean’’ since we needed to
combine different variables (in this case, radiation and pop-
ulation) to give us a balanced view. This approach [33], [34]
is particularly useful when values are normalized between
0 and 1, as it scales down extreme values and prevents them
from dominating the average. Furthermore, the usage of the
geometric mean, allows for the combination of values that are
by default distinct in nature and measured on different scales,
with neither factor dominating the results. Additionally, this
approach allows for the combination of different risk factors
that can summarize each other’s effects, rather than a simple
addition.
We adopt the geometric mean (multiplicative) aggregation
in Eq. 13 to combine the hazard, exposure, and vulnerability
factors. This choice ensures that the overall risk index is non-
compensatory, meaning a deficiency in any one component
strongly diminishes the total risk score. In contrast to a simple
sum, a geometric mean does not allow one extremely high
factor value to fully outweigh a very low value in another
factor [36]. This property aligns with standard risk paradigms
where risk is conceptually the product of hazard, exposure,
and vulnerability [37].
Using a geometric mean means to average the log-
transformed inherent values [33], [34]. This formulation
preserves the interpretation of the index on the original scale
in the context of a city scale analysis and inherently penalizes
imbalanced contributions across factors with multiple crite-
ria – for example, improving all factors modestly yields a
higher risk reduction than dramatically improving just one
factor [37]. Eq. 13 variables are assigned equal weight (i.e.,
equal exponents in the multiplicative formula), reflecting
an assumption that hazard, exposure, and vulnerability are
equally important in the absence of evidence to the contrary.
This equal-weight approach is a common, neutral baseline in
composite indicator design [37].
We acknowledge that different weighting schemes could
be employed if justified; for instance, planners might weight
vulnerability higher if social factors are deemed paramount
in a given city. Our framework is flexible in this regard.
In this study, however, sensitivity tests showed that moderate
weight adjustments did not substantially change the ranked
risk outcomes, so we retain equal weights for clarity and to
minimize bias.
This approach ensures a balanced risk metric where the
overall risk is not dominated or influenced at disproportional
levels by either factor. The results are then incorporated into
the city’s road network data, and each node is associated with
the relevant cartesian coordinates (latitude and longitude).

VI. CONCLUSION
This study presents a novel methodology for enhancing heat-
wave response planning in urban centers using a digital twin
model that integrates solar radiation data, population vulner-
ability metrics, and graph-based network analysis. We aim
to identify high-risk urban blocks by focusing on node-level
risks. In that view, we can use efficient resource allocation
and rapid access to high-risk areas by providing optimized
response paths to first responders.
This study provides tangible benefits from the practical
application of the findings into risk prioritization and route
optimizations for the optimized urban heatwave response
preparedness and planning. First responders (e.g., an ambu-
lance) can utilize the proposed methodology to identify paths
that maximize risk coverage while adhering to operational
or other limitations, such as ensuring that the patrol route
does not exceed a duration of 15 minutes, by – for exam-
ple – focusing on paths with a depth of 4 to 7 nodes.
Furthermore, this methodology allows for strategically plac-
ing appropriate resources at nodes within the critical paths
allowing for rapidly reaching any point within their patrol
area. The dual focus of this methodology ensures that emer-
gency resources are both targeted toward the most vulnerable
areas and readily available for rapid deployment in case of
emergencies.
The results demonstrate the effectiveness of this frame-
work in prioritizing emergency planning. Key findings
include the identification of critical junctions with high
cumulative dependency risks and the generation of optimal
paths tailored to user-defined constraints, such as path
length and coverage of vulnerable populations. For example,
we identified node 683 as a critical ‘‘cold spot.’’ Our model
can guide first responders and city planners to strategically
place the appropriate resources and, at the same time, suggest
appropriate intervention strategies during extreme weather
events, such as heat waves. City planners and public health
authorities can benefit from this framework with the introduc-
tion of targeted interventions and minimizing health impacts
on vulnerable populations.
The proposed approach is transferable to other urban set-
tings, provided it is calibrated to local conditions. We antic-
ipate that with appropriate Solar API data, city’s specific
data availability, infrastructure, and road characteristics, the
framework could be adapted to improve heatwave planning
in other cities.
Incorporating real-time data (such as environmental and
demographic) and additional factors (such as transportation
network disruptions) further expands the predictive capabili-
ties of our model. The extension and expansion of our model
can be achieved through its application to other cities or
by simulating other extreme events (such as flooding). As a
result, this methodology provides a robust foundation for
urban resilience against heat waves and other climate-induced
emergencies.
VII. IMPLICATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Future work should explicitly address the implications of our
findings within the context of European Union regulations
and directives, particularly the EU Strategy on Adaptation to
Climate Change (COM(2021) 82) and the revised Network
and Information Security Directive (NIS2 Directive).
According to NIS2., which mandates comprehensive
risk-based approaches to protecting essential entities, mem-
ber states must ensure resilience of essential services such
as healthcare and emergency infrastructure; our findings
directly support these obligations by pinpointing critical
urban nodes where targeted interventions during heat-
waves would substantially improve emergency response
effectiveness.

solar radiation data for urban areas to minimize process-
