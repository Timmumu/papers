When Roads Go Underwater: AI-Enhanced Digital
Twin–Driven Flood Resilience for Roadway
Serviceability Assessment
Moeid Shariatfar, Ph.D. 1; Yangtian Yin 2; Yong-Cheol Lee, Ph.D., A.M.ASCE 3;

Abstract: Extreme flooding poses escalating risks to roadway infrastructure, threatening structural integrity, operational reliability, and
public safety. Existing flood-monitoring approaches primarily utilize sparse sensor networks, thus providing limited real-time data and
insufficiently capturing indirect flooding impacts on noninundated roadway segments. This gap complicates emergency response, delays
evacuation, and undermines postevent recovery efforts. Addressing this critical knowledge gap, this study proposes an advanced predictive
decision-support framework leveraging an artificial intelligence (AI)-enhanced digital twin integrated with flood simulations and graph neural
network (GNN) modeling. It systematically assesses roadway serviceability during extreme flooding by integrating structural conditions,
operational disruptions, historical maintenance records, inundation severity, and recovery timelines. By consolidating heterogeneous data
sets, including historical traffic volumes, pavement conditions, hydrological data, and weather forecasts, the developed framework provides
accurate, real-time predictive insights for both inundated and indirectly impacted roadway segments. This capability was demonstrated
through an illustrative validation study. Ultimately, this research equips transportation agencies and emergency responders with robust
hands-on tools that can facilitate optimized emergency response, improved infrastructure management, and enhanced resilience of trans-
portation systems under ever-increasing flood risks due to climate change. DOI: 10.1061/JMENEA.MEENG-7046. © 2026 American
Society of Civil Engineers.
Introduction, Gaps in Knowledge, and Significance
Road networks are susceptible to natural disasters such as floods
that impact both the structural integrity of the roadways and their
long-term serviceability. Considering the short-term implications
for road usability is essential because debris from disasters and
flooding can lead to road blockages ( Diakakis et al. 2020 ). During
these disasters, the increased traffic due to mainline lane closures or
people trying to escape the affected area can further affect the road
network’s serviceability unfavorably ( Mitsakis et al. 2014 ). Typi-
cally, following such an event, specific locations may become less
accessible, which is especially critical for first responders in some
cases and for people who are dispatched for rescue operations and
emergent support.
Effective monitoring and assessment of roadway conditions dur-
ing extreme flooding events are essential to safeguarding public
safety, supporting timely emergency response, and enhancing the
resilience of transportation infrastructure. Current flood-monitoring
approaches predominantly utilize physical sensor systems deployed
along rivers and waterways to track rising water levels and potential
overflow events. However, these systems offer valuable insights
regarding local inundation; their sparse geographical distribution re-
sults in incomplete and often fragmented data regarding the broader
impact of flooding across extensive roadway networks. Further-
more, these systems do not provide accurate, real-time flooding sta-
tus updates for individual roadway segments, significantly limiting
their effectiveness in critical decision-making scenarios during
disasters (Y uan et al. 2022).
Urban and infrastructure planning processes traditionally lev-
erage floodplain mapping, historical flooding data, and estab-
lished hydrological models to anticipate and mitigate flooding
risks. Nevertheless, unprecedented climate phenomena, rapid
urbanization, altered land-use patterns, and evolving topographic
conditions have introduced substantial uncertainties into these
conventional flood-risk assessments. Consequently, the reliability
of existing floodplain-based planning and management frame-
works has diminished, highlighting the urgent need for dynamic
monitoring solutions that integrate real-time and historical data
streams to accurately reflect evolving environmental and infra-
structural conditions.
A critical operational challenge arising from this data inad-
equacy is the significant disruption to first responders ’ capabilities
during flood events. The absence of integrated real-time monitoring
and predictive tools results in uncertainty regarding roadway acces-
sibility, closures, and congestion, complicating rescue operations,
delaying response activities, and increasing safety risks for both
responders and affected populations (Helderop and Grubesic 2019).
Furthermore, previous research efforts have rarely explored the iden-
tification of feasible alternative routes or systematically assessed

Current methodologies for evaluating roadway serviceability
have primarily focused on surface conditions and structural integrity,
offering essential yet limited insights into infrastructure performance
and maintenance needs ( Aleadelat et al. 2018 ; Fuentes et al. 2021 ).
Despite recent significant advancements in modeling flooding im-
pacts on transportation networks, most existing studies emphasized
flood propagation modeling, highlighting inundation severity and
spatial extent, but seldom comprehensively addressed the indirect
impacts of flooding on roadway segments not directly submerged
but still adversely influenced by nearby inundation at a network
level (Y uan et al. 2022). As a result, prevailing assessment practices
significantly underestimate the true scope of network disruption
and fail to capture the complexity of roadway serviceability resto-
ration efforts following severe flooding events.
Considering the existing challenges in the current methodolo-
gies, this study is guided by the following research question: How
can a predictive, segment-level assessment of roadway serviceabil-
ity under flood conditions be achieved in near real time by integrat-
ing traffic patterns, flood simulation, and roadway attributes? This
question drives the development and validation of an integrated
framework that goes beyond binary inundation mapping to forecast
both direct and indirect service impacts across a dynamic road
network.
To bridge this critical research gap, a broader and more holistic
analytical approach is required to accurately define and evaluate
roadway serviceability under flooding conditions at a macroscopic
system network level. Specifically, this study defines serviceability
as a multifaceted measure that integrates operational capacity, in-
undation severity, and flood duration. Leveraging extensive histori-
cal data sets, including pavement performance records, traffic flow
data, infrastructure conditions, and historical flooding events, this
research proposes an advanced, network-level evaluation frame-
work. This methodology revolves around the creation and imple-
mentation of a digital twin framework seamlessly integrated with
flood simulation capabilities and graph neural network (GNN) mod-
els. This innovative integration approach facilitates holistic, real-
time simulations, precise visualizations, and predictive analyses
of flooding impacts across entire roadway networks. These analyses
encompass both directly inundated and indirectly affected roadway
segments.
In this study, we adopt the concept of a digital twin (DT) as a
dynamic, virtual replica of a physical system that integrates real-time
data streams, supports predictive analytics, and provides an interac-
tive interface for operational decision-making. Unlike conventional
simulation models, which often operate in isolation or with static
inputs, digital twins are characterized by their ability to receive con-
tinuous updates from physical assets and simulate system behavior
under evolving conditions. Although some digital twin implemen-
tations also include bidirectional control (e.g., sending actions back
to physical systems), our focus lies on enabling real-time monitoring
and forecasting based on live and historical data, within a responsive
GIS-based environment. This qualifies the current system as an op-
erational digital twin designed for flood event response.
By addressing these fundamental limitations and questions
in existing research and operational practice, this study aims to
provide transportation agencies and emergency responders with
robust, actionable insights for improved decision-making during ex-
treme flooding scenarios. Ultimately, the anticipated outcomes in-
clude better-informed evacuation strategies, optimized emergency
response, more effective maintenance prioritization, and significantly
enhanced resilience, safety, and operational reliability of transpor-
tation infrastructure in the face of increasing flooding risks.
Research Objectives and Methods
The overarching goal of this study is to address the substantial
knowledge gap regarding the intricate relationships among road-
way serviceability, historical traffic data, and roadway performance
under extreme flooding scenarios. Specifically, this study seeks to
develop an AI-enhanced digital twin –driven predictive decision-
support framework that systematically and efficiently assesses
roadway conditions during severe flooding by leveraging traffic op-
eration data to establish baseline performance metrics for forecast-
ing future roadway serviceability.
Building upon prior research that successfully simulated flood-
ing impacts on directly inundated road segments and their acces-
sibility to critical locations, this study expands the analytical scope
to holistically evaluate broader network-level implications, includ-
ing roadway segments indirectly affected by flooding events. To
achieve this goal, the research developed and implemented an in-
tegrated methodology capable of assessing roadway serviceability
from a short-term perspective, focusing on immediate impacts such
as accessibility, operational disruption, and traffic-flow disruptions.
A key objective of this research involves the construction and
validation of an advanced digital twin framework. This innovative
approach integrates flood simulation and state-of-the-art GNN mod-
eling techniques to dynamically simulate, visualize, and analyze the
impact of flooding across entire transportation networks. The digital
twin developed for this study assessed inundation severity, flood du-
ration, traffic conditions, structural conditions, and historical pave-
ment performance data, providing accurate predictive diagnostics
into both inundated and indirectly impacted roadway segments.
Furthermore, this study also aims to create a robust visualization
and predictive decision-support tool capable of consolidating diverse
and heterogeneous data sets, including weather forecasts, hydrolog-
ical information, historical traffic volumes, flood event records, and
pavement condition metrics. This integrated platform can deliver
real-time situational awareness and predictive analytics to facilitate
more effective evacuation planning, optimize emergency response
operations, and improve strategic decision-making processes related
to disaster resilience and infrastructure management.
In summary, the objective of this study is to develop a real-time
decision-support framework that enhances flood resilience in road-
way networks. Specifically, this study aims to achieve the following
objectives:
 Define and quantify roadway serviceability under flood conditions
as a function of inundation status, and operational performance.
 Develop a predictive model using a graph attention network
(GA T) that incorporates flood simulation data and historical traf-
fic patterns to estimate short-term speed and accessibility across
the network.
 Integrate the predictive model within a digital twin visualization
environment for interactive, GIS-based decision support during
flood events.
 V alidate the proposed framework using historical flood and traf-
fic data from Hurricane Harvey to demonstrate its capability in
capturing both direct and indirect roadway impacts.

product or a service to satisfy the potential or actual needs of the users
or, more in general, of the beneficiaries of the product/service itself
(Colonna et al. 2004). There is a vulnerability factor for assessing the

that measures how sensitive a roadway is to specific risks, like
floods. It is used to assess the potential for an event with consequen-
ces caused by hazards. For roads, flood vulnerability is influenced by
location, structure, elevation, and drainage. There are two aspects of
flooding vulnerability for a roadway section (Tao and Mallick 2020):

tion being flooded during a storm. It is determined by the road ’s
location, elevation, and hydrological conditions.
 Vulnerability factor 2 (VF2) indicates how likely a flooded road-
way section’s structural capacity will deteriorate due to flooding.
It depends on factors like water infiltration, structural condition,
subgrade sensitivity, and drainage ( Shariatfar et al. 2022 ; Lee
et al. 2020 ; Shariatfar and Lee 2024 ).
 The overall vulnerability factor (VF) is the product of VF1
and VF2.
This study aims to provide a holistic roadway serviceability as-
sessment framework that considers short-term roadway service in
areas susceptible to flooding. The introduced framework incorpo-
rates VF1 to analyze and predict short-term flood-induced vulner-
ability, enabling first responders to identify the most serviceable
routes for reaching critical locations during emergency events. It
predicts accessible and inaccessible roadways in case of flooding
and their effect on the entire roadway network. Carey and Irick
(1960) described relative performance as how much pavement
can serve its customer during a service period, although it could
differ from everyone’s perspective. Serviceability of a specific sec-
tion of a pavement is defined as the ability to serve the existing
condition in high-speed, high-volume, and mixed traffic (truck and
automobile) situations. The definition is applied to primary high-
ways and road tests and should be modified for use on city streets,
farm roads, and so on.
Vulnerability defines how much transportation assets are suscep-
tive to harsh weather and climate effects that are exposed to them
(Filosa et al. 2017). On the other hand, the capability of coping with
the existing climate variability or future climate situations is defined
as adaptive capacity, which is essential to know the weather varia-
bles to evaluate the level of risk in different climate conditions. Also,
the Federal Transit Administration considers vulnerability assessment
an evaluation process to understand how natural hazards may endan-
ger public transport systems. Using six resilience phases of Assess,
Plan, Design and Construct, Manage, Maintain, and Monitor, the
Guidebook provides a resilience planning framework to address vul-
nerabilities and build resilience to extreme weather conditions.
Short-Term Effect on Serviceability
Many researchers suggest that there is no single definition suitable
for vulnerability, but it must be defined in the context of an event. A
definition that describes vulnerability as “the susceptibility to inci-
dents that can result in considerable reductions in road network serv-
iceability” has also been widely used since then (Berdica 2002). The
literature on vulnerability indices can be broadly grouped into the
distance-based and the cost-based approaches. Indices based on
travel distance are relevant to sparse regional networks where driv-
ers may need longer detours to reach their destinations if a link is
blocked. On the contrary, several alternative routes are usually
available in dense urban networks. Moreover, it is well known that
drivers often prefer quicker routes that need not necessarily be
shorter in distance terms. Hence, indices such as Hansen ’sI n d e x
(Taylor et al. 2006) and the efficiency measure (Latora and Marchiori
2001), which are based purely on distance, are considered unsuitable
for measuring the vulnerability of dense urban road networks
(Balijepalli and Oppong 2014 ).
The topic of network vulnerabilities goes beyond transportation
planning and has its roots in graph theory and complex network re-
search. The most common measure used to find vulnerabilities is the
betweenness centrality of nodes or edges of the network, which
measures the tendency of paths in a given network to pass through
the element. This parameter implies that nodes or edges with high
betweenness centrality tend to concentrate on more trips and be
more critical in a network ( Barthélemy 2004 ).
Spatiotemporal graph convolutional networks (STGCNs) are
widely used in traffic prediction tasks because they model both tem-
poral patterns and spatial dependencies across road networks. In
more recent approaches, the STGCN models aim to make predic-
tions of road segments ’ flooding status based on their own and ad-
jacent road segments’ current flooding status. These studies utilized
high-resolution traffic data to infer the inundation status of road seg-
ments (road segments with no traffic speed were shown to be inun-
dated). Hence, traffic speed data during floods can be a reliable
indicator of road network status during floods, and using models for
traffic flow predictions can be adjusted to derive foresight about road
network flooding status ( Y u et al. 2018).
Another study has emphasized the cascading effects of flooding
on urban accessibility and emergency response. Yin et al. ( 2017)
evaluated how sea-level rise and coastal flooding reduce emergency
response access in Lower Manhattan using high-resolution flood
maps and accessibility modeling. Their study highlighted the need
for spatially detailed and temporally aware models in emergency
planning. Similarly, Tahvildari et al. ( 2021) analyzed compound
flooding scenarios and their impact on emergency response oper-
ations, incorporating hydrologic and hydraulic modeling to support
planning decisions.
Pregnolato et al. ( 2017) developed a widely cited “depth-disrup-
tion function” that models the relationship between floodwater depth
and vehicle speed, providing a critical improvement over binary open/
closed road assumptions commonly used in flood impact models.
Their empirically derived function enabled integration into transport
simulations to estimate travel delays more accurately under varying
flood depths. Fan et al. (2020) proposed a network percolation–based
contagion model that captures the spatial and temporal evolution of
flooding in urban road networks by treating flood spread analogously
to epidemic transmission using an s usceptible-exposed-infected-re-
covered (SEIR)-based mathematical framework. The model predicts
the likelihood of road segments transitioning from functional to
flooded states based on the status of neighboring segments and mac-
roscopic contagion parameters.
From recent studies that evaluated roadway vulnerability,
Marian et al. ( 2024) proposed a simulation-based framework to
evaluate the vulnerability and robustness of urban road networks
during flood-induced closures by analyzing both controlled inter-
sections and uncontrolled freeway segments. Redzuan et al. ( 2022)
developed a GIS-based methodology to evaluate the vulnerability
of road segments based on the availability and efficiency of diver-
sion routes. Their approach focused on constructing two disjointed
alternative paths for each disrupted link, emphasizing connectivity
recovery and accessibility under disruption scenarios.
Although these studies provided valuable insight in traffic flow
estimates (Pregnolato et al. 2017) and an efficient way of forecasting
flood propagation (Yin et al. 2017; Fan et al. 2020; Y uan et al. 2022),
they do not support predictive, network-scale assessment nor do they
incorporate indirect disruptions from noninundated segments such
as increased congestion or speed reductions resulting from rerouted
traffic. Also, some recent studies, like Marian et al. (2024), relied on
predefined closure scenarios and mesoscopic simulation, which can
be computationally intensive and lack predictive analytics.

Digital Twinning Visualization of Flood Impacts
Early research by Kuser Olsen et al. ( 2018) explored the use of DTs
for flood risk communication through realistic interactive visualiza-
tion techniques. Their study developed an integrative flood-risk map
superimposed on a satellite base map via systems based on GIS, aim-
ing to enhance public awareness and motivate proactive flood mit-
igation efforts. A comparative analysis with Hazard-US (HAZUS)
system, published by FEMA ( 2011), demonstrated that although
both approaches effectively conveyed flood risk knowledge, the
DT-based system was superior in fostering engagement and moti-
vating action. Despite lacking a full virtual model of physical infra-
structure, this study illustrated the potential of DT visualization for
emotionally impactful flood risk communication.
Ghaith et al. ( 2021) proposed a city-scale DT framework incor-
porating real-time simulations of Calgary, Canada ’sp o w e r ,t r a n s -
portation, water distribution, and river systems. By continuously
integrating sensory, Internet of Things (IoT), global positioning sys-
tems (GPS), and satellite data, the DT model dynamically adjusted its
flood impact assessments. Although their implementation effectively
visualized flood risks, it remained constrained by a limited simula-
tion boundary, highlighting the need for broader-scale validation.
The Earth System Digital Twin (ESDT), a collaborative effort
between National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)
and Centre National d'Etudes Spatiales (CNES), further exempli-
fies large-scale DT flood modeling (Huang et al. 2022). Suquet et al.
(2023) expanded upon ESDT by integrating hydrologic models and
AI-enhanced remote sensing data to generate rapid flood predic-
tions. Their four-step DT pipeline incorporated real-time water level
data, synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imaging, numerical modeling,
and geo-located social media analytics to assess flood risks compre-
hensively. Their system prioritized interoperability and large-scale
modeling, and future advancements could focus on enhanced visu-
alization techniques for policymakers and the public.
The digital twin framework developed in this study enables the
integration of flood simulation results, real-time and historical traffic
data, and vulnerability metrics into a unified virtual representation of
the roadway network. Unlike conventional GIS-based dashboards,
this platform incorporates predictive AI models (via a graph attention
network) to dynamically estimate roadway serviceability as conditions
evolve. The digital twin environment facilitates real-time visualization
of accessible and inaccessible segments and supports scenario-based
decision-making during flood events. Although the current implemen-
tation does not yet include automated feedback or control actions back
to field sensors (i.e., bidirectional flow), it supports forward simulation
with near-real-time data feeds, aligning with operational definitions of
digital twins in transportation resilience applications.
Methodology
This study proposes a way to define roadway serviceability and
develop a new prediction method for the serviceability of flood-
vulnerable roadways.
Definition of Roadway Serviceability for Disaster
Resilience
In this study, roadway serviceability is defined as the ability of a
roadway to consistently maintain minimum quality and perfor-
mance of its structure and pavement and effectively and safely sup-
port its operations as its intended purpose. The ability encompasses
the road ’s structural integrity, surface condition, and overall func-
tionality, ensuring vehicles can travel without significant risk or
difficulty. In extreme weather events such as flooding, roadway
serviceability also involves assessing whether a road is passable,
free from hazardous conditions, and suitable for emergency and
regular traffic.
Although roadway serviceability can be defined broadly to in-
clude structural integrity, user comfort, and safety, in the context of
short-term flood response, it is operationalized in this study using
traffic speed as a practical and measurable proxy. Traffic speed re-
flects both direct disruptions (e.g., inundated or closed roadways)
and indirect network effects (e.g., rerouting and congestion), mak-
ing it a meaningful indicator of performance during emergencies.
Although structural vulnerability is not modeled, flood exposure
and roadway connectivity characteristics are included as node and
edge features, enabling the model to predict segment-level service-
ability under evolving flood conditions.
With this scope of the definition, the authors identified the rel-
evant factors of roadway serviceability and established a funda-
mental baseline to calculate and predict roadway serviceability
under catastrophic flooding events.
Prediction Method for the Serviceability of
Flood-Vulnerable Roadways
Diverse data sources and streams can be used to assess the impact
and damage of flooding on roadway systems. Among them, this
research project utilizes historical traffic data recorded in Houston
city to identify underlying patterns of traffic changes under flood-
ing impacts and reveal the impacted sections of roadways. The pro-
posed framework uses spatial data, i.e., sensor locations and their
position compared with flood zones, and temporal data, that is, time
intervals of every 5 min for speed data and every 6 h for flood data.
Based on these data, the authors built a serviceability assessment
and prediction method. In addition, the proposed method acts as a
digital twin during flood events with real-time traffic amount data
that first simulates the flood and traffic speed in a city by identify-
ing the flooded zones and blocked roadways and second, predicts
the near future condition of the flood and traffic based on a given
time window. Fig. 1 shows the overall process and data flow of
the study.
The first step of this project involves extracting and utilizing data
obtained from previous research efforts, including flood simulations,
identification of inundated roadways, and inundation durations.
These data provide a foundation for subsequent serviceability and
accurate traffic predictions based on the new conditions.
We selected a GA T over other GNN variants such as graph con-
volutional network (GCN) and GraphSAGE due to its ability to as-
sign dynamic, learnable attention weights to neighboring nodes.
This is especially valuable in flood-impacted road networks, where
the influence of surrounding roadway segments on a given node can
vary significantly depending on inundation levels and network top-
ology. Unlike GCN, which treats all neighbors equally, GA T enables
the model to learn which connections are more informative in de-
graded traffic conditions. Additionally, GA T has shown improved
performance in prior work on spatiotemporal traffic forecasting
due to its capacity to capture heterogeneous spatial dependencies.

The GA T model developed by Zhang et al. ( 2019), which is a
type of the GNN model, is used for predicting the traffic given the
speed data every 5 min ( Zhang et al. 2019 ). This research team
attained the accumulated traffic data from INRIX, a database of
vehicles’ average speed recorded every 5 min in Houston city dur-
ing Hurricane Harvey from August 25 to August 30, 2017.
The proposed method also incorporates flood data into the
model and the distances between the nodes. In this GA T model,
each node represents a sensor that contains information about the
average speed of the vehicles every 5 min. The edges represent the
connection between the nodes that are based on two factors: (1) the
distance between the nodes attained from the formula used in the
Zhang et al. (2019) study, and (2) the existence of a road inundation
between every two consecutive nodes based on the flood data
extracted from the previously developed flood model ( Yin et al.
2024). The model predicts the speed of the vehicles 45 min in the
future based on the node and edge features ’ data for every 6-h time
interval that the flood model updates.
The time intervals of the flood prediction model are every 6 h
starting from August 25 at midnight (00:00); for example, the first
interval is August 25 from 00:00 to 06:00, and the next is from
06:00. to 12:00 the same day. Node features are speed data for
every sensor (node), and the edge features are (1) the distance be-
tween the nodes, and (2) the flood data for each time interval.
For every time interval, an edge feature of flood data from that
interval was added to the nodes, and the traffic prediction model
was executed separately. The edge feature shows the properties of
the connections between two nodes. For instance, the distance edge
feature helps identify the neighboring sensors, and the flood edge
feature helps the GA T model identify the changes in connection
between the nodes due to flood inundation.
As an example, at the first-time interval (August 25 from 00:00
to 06:00), the node features are speed data, and the edge features are
the distance between the nodes and the flood data about the first
interval, i.e., the roadways that were flooded from 00:00 to 06:00.
For the second interval, the node feature is again the speed data, and
the edge features are distances between the nodes, flood data at the
first interval, and flood data at the second interval. This process
continues for every step until all intervals are added to the predic-
tion model. In summary, for every time window, an edge feature is
added to the model, along with the speed data added as node fea-
tures. Table 1 presents the node features for every time interval, and
Table 2 provides a part of the edge features for each time interval.

each time interval with specific depth. The points in Fig.2 represent the

Digital Twin –Based Visualization Framework
The digital twin –based visualization system is designed as a com-
prehensive framework for visualizing roadway serviceability pre-
diction results in conjunction with flood-affected areas. This
system enables experienced engineers to visually validate predic-
tions, detect potential anomalies, and refine the assessment of road-
way functionality under flood scenarios. Leveraging pre-existing
flood simulation results, the system incorporates roadway inunda-
tion data as input to the GNN model to predict roadway service-
ability. Operating through a multilayered architecture, this system
ensures seamless data processing, model interoperability, and visu-
alization for decision-making support (Fig. 7).

The digital twin –based visualization framework consists of
three primary components that work in concert to provide accurate
and real-time assessments of flood-related roadway serviceability.
The first component is the integrated GA T model prediction
layer, which processes roadway inundation data derived from prior
flood simulations. This layer employs the GA T model to predict
roadway serviceability using roadway inundation data as input. Un-
like traditional simulations, this layer focuses solely on predicting
roadway serviceability based on the GA T model developed in this
study, which is trained by leveraging historical INRIX traffic data
and flood simulation results from studies conducted by Yin et al.
(2024).
The second component is ArcGIS (version 11) Online, which
functions as a middleware for publishing the GA T model predic-
tion layer results online. This enables end-users across the internet
to access and visualize prediction results on their devices. ArcGIS
Online integrates predictive results from the GA T model with
geographic information, all owing for seamless overlaying of
flood maps, road networks, and serviceability predictions. This
cloud-based platform facilitates data sharing, collaboration, and
real-time access to visualization outputs for engineers and deci-
sion-makers.
The third component, the digital twin visualization layer, re-
trieves data from the cloud-stored maps in the second layer. This
layer processes and interprets the stored spatial-temporal prediction
data, transforming it into an interactive and real-time representation
of roadway inundation and serviceability conditions. This layer
converts the GA T model outputs stored in ArcGIS Online into an
intuitive, immersive, and dynamic visualization environment where
stakeholders can gain further insights based on human expertise.
Therefore, the digital twin visualization enhances decision-making
by offering a comprehensive view of affected areas within their ur-
ban context, enabling engineers to validate model predictions and
refine assessments through interactive analysis.
The visualization framework integrated multiple data layers, in-
cluding satellite-based base maps, three-dimensional (3D) terrain
elevation data, and 3D building models obtained from public sour-
ces. INRIX roadway data and simulated flood extents ( Yin et al.
2024) were incorporated as vector tile layers to represent dynamic
traffic and flooding conditions. Finally, flood –roadway speed pre-
dictions developed in this study were integrated as time-series point
data. Together, these layers provided the spatial and temporal foun-
dation for the digital twin environment.
At this stage of development, the system functions as a digital
twin prototype. Although the platform integrates precomputed flood
simulation outputs and historical traffic data for predictive modeling,
real-time ingestion of live traffic sensor data is not yet automated.
The flood scenarios used in this version are not dynamically gen-
erated on demand but selected from a library of historical events.
However, the input information of the model can be fed from sensors
in real-time. Furthermore, the computational time can be reduced to
a level that supports near-real-time simulation output. These limita-
tions are acknowledged as current constraints of the operational im-
plementation. Nevertheless, the system architecture supports future
expansion to include live sensor feeds, automated model updates,
and potential two-way feedback mechanisms to qualify as a fully
operational digital twin.
Validation with a Case Study
To demonstrate the applicability of the proposed framework, a case
study was conducted using the Hurricane Harvey flooding sce-
nario. Although a real-time sensor data acquisition system was
not established, the study employed reliable mock-up data sets
derived from the simulation and historical flood conditions. These
data sets were used to emulate real-time inputs, enabling the testing
and illustration of the model ’s performance in predicting roadway
serviceability during large-scale flood events.
Case Study
The objective of this case study is to evaluate the model ’s ability to
predict roadway speed data and illustrate its practical utility in iden-
tifying road segments likely to remain serviceable in the immediate
future, specifically, over the next 45 min. The model provides real-
time speed data for each roadway section and predicts future speed
conditions by incorporating information on roadway inundation,
which is extracted from a flood model.
Unlike conventional approaches that focus only on flooded road
sections, this model also predicts speed changes on noninundated
roadways. These roadways may experience congestion due to traf-
fic rerouted from blocked or submerged sections, impacting their
serviceability. By considering both direct and indirect flood effects,
the model provides a more comprehensive assessment of roadway
conditions during extreme weather events.
For instance, during disasters like Hurricane Harvey, first
responders can leverage the model ’s speed predictions to identify
the fastest available routes to reach critical destinations efficiently.
This forward-looking functionality enhances emergency response
efforts by allowing responders to proactively navigate dynamic
and often unpredictable roadway conditions with improved situa-
tional awareness and confidence.

Each road segment is represented as a node in the graph, with
edges weighted based on physical proximity. We used a distance-
based exponential weighting function with σ2 ¼ 0.1 and ε ¼ 0.5
to promote sparsity. The “USE_GA T_WEIGHTS” flag was set to
“True”, which binarizes the adjacency matrix and includes self-
loops, following the original GA T formulation. Edge attributes in-
clude a 12-dimensional time series of flood exposure levels across
multiple time stamps.
The data set was constructed using a sliding time window where
each graph snapshot included 12 historical time steps ( x) and 9 pre-
diction time steps ( y), covering the 45-min future speed prediction.
Data were standardized using z-score normalization. We split the
data set using Temporal_Signal_Split with an 80∶20 time-based ra-
tio, ensuring temporal continuity between training and testing sets.
Model training was conducted over 15 epochs using minibatches
of 2,000 graph snapshots per epoch. Training was performed on a
CPU device using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01.
The model optimized MSE between predicted and observed traffic
speeds. Evaluation was conducted on a held-out 20% time-based test
split, where the lowest test MSE was 0.2602. This demonstrates the
model’s ability to generalize to new conditions and accurately esti-
mate short-term traffic performance during flood events. In the next
section, we further discuss variations in MES scores attained from
the model in different intervals.
Methodological Contributions and Novelty
This study presents several key methodological contributions that ex-
tend the current state of the art in flood-related serviceability modeling:
 Coupling of hydrodynamic simulation with GA T-based predic-
tion: Unlike prior models that treat flood effects in isolation or
use fixed closure scenarios, this framework dynamically fuses
flood simulation outputs with real-time and historical traffic data
using a GA T. This coupling enables predictive modeling of both
direct and indirect roadway disruptions.
 Serviceability estimation at segment level: Whereas most prior
works predicted binary status (flooded versus unflooded), the mo-
del proposed in this study forecasts a continuous serviceability
score—capturing varying degrees of speed degradation and acces-
sibility at the road segment level, including for segments not directly
inundated but indirectly impacted by rerouting and congestion.
 Use of GA T architecture for indirect impact modeling: Our imple-
mentation leverages the GA T’s attention mechanism to dynamically
weigh the influence of neighboring segments, allowing the model
to capture localized traffic propagation effects during floods —
something traditional GNNs or statistical methods cannot do.
 Integration with a GIS-based digital twin: The predicted outputs
are embedded within an operational digital twin platform that
supports real-time visualization, scenario exploration, and emer-
gency decision support, bridging the gap between analytical
models and end-user deployment.
These innovations enable more accurate, spatially aware, and op-
erationally actionable insights compared with prior flood disruption
models that focused only on inundation status or used rigid traffic
closure assumptions.
Results and Discussion
The proposed method is developed based on real traffic and flood-
ing simulation data in conjunction with graph neural network mod-
els to assess roadway serviceability from a short-term perspective.
It considers two critical factors: roadway inundation and the result-
ing traffic.
The accuracy of the prediction model is influenced by the fol-
lowing key elements:
 The predictive accuracy of the flood model, which directly af-
fects the overall accuracy of the prediction for the serviceability
of the roadways.
 The volume and granularity of traffic data used for training and
testing the machine learning algorithm impacting the model ’s
learning and generalization capabilities.
These interdependent components collectively shape the effec-
tiveness of the proposed method in forecasting roadway accessibil-
ity during and after extreme flooding events.
To evaluate the performance of the GA T model, this study used
the MSE metric, which assesses the average squared difference be-
tween the actual and predicted values. The results of the MSE show
that the inclusion of flood model data as edge data in the GA T model
contributed to improved predictive accuracy of the model. Fig. 10

A primary factor contributing to this decline is that this study
focuses on predicting flooded areas and inundated roadways,
whereas the duration of inundation remains a subject for future study.
Although the flood model successfully identified the locations of
flooding, the actual recession of floodwaters occurred sooner than
predicted. This discrepancy may be attributed to mitigation efforts
by authorities, such as drainage operations and other flood manage-
ment measures, which were not explicitly accounted for in the model
due to the lack of accurate and detailed input data sets. In addition, to
accurately evaluate the impact of flooding, dynamic modeling and
simulation are required, representing changing parts and portions of
roadways affected by inundation over time. This study, however,
employed static flooding data that illustrates inundated areas of
Houston city at specific time intervals (6 h).
Despite the promising results, the study encountered some lim-
itations related to the volume and scope of traffic data in Houston
for model training and testing. Due to computational constraints
and data handling capacity, only two zones in the Houston area —
selected based on zip codes —were analyzed. Expanding the model
to incorporate broader geographic coverage and more comprehen-
sive data sets is expected to significantly enhance prediction accu-
racy. In addition, the model does not account for other factors, such
as a temporary event, including a car accident, that can make traffic
changes that are not affected by a flooding disaster. These factors
introduce uncertainty into the relationship between flooding and
traffic behavior, limiting the granularity of analysis.
Another important factor influencing the accuracy of the predic-
tion model is the spatial and temporal resolution of the input data.
Higher resolution flood and traffic data can provide more precise
insights into roadway conditions, allowing for finer-grained predic-
tions of serviceability. Additionally, integrating real-time sensor data,
such as traffic cameras and GPS-based vehicle tracking, could en-
hance the model ’s responsiveness to rapidly changing conditions.
Even though the current case study was limited to two zones in
Houston due to computational constraints and data-handling complex-
ity, the framework is designed to be generalizable to other regions. Its
modular structure allows for the integration of location-specific data
such as flood exposure, roadway topology, and historical speed ob-
servations. To apply this approach in different contexts (e.g., coastal
cities with storm surge, or mountainous areas prone to flash flood-
ing), appropriate adjustments to the flood modeling inputs and
network features would be required. As long as adequate traffic,
roadway-segment, and flood data are available, the underlying GNN-
based architecture and inference pipeline can be retrained to accom-
modate regional characteristics. Future work will explore scalability
and domain adaptation to enable broader geographic application.
The digital twin visualization offers a more intuitive representa-
tion of roadway vulnerabilities, facilitating proactive disaster re-
sponse and infrastructure planning. The ability to fully visualize
multiple flood scenarios in an interactive, three-dimensional, and ge-
ospatially accurate way aids transportation agencies in better assess-
ing flood impacts in real time. This enhanced situational awareness
facilitates the optimization of evacuation routes, prioritization of in-
frastructure repairs, and mitigation of long-term roadway degrada-
tion. By offering timely, actionable insights, the digital twin serves
as a critical tool for improving urban resilience in flood-prone
regions.
Although this framework adopts key principles of a digital twin—
data integration, predictive modeling, and interactive visualization—
it is currently limited in terms of automated real-time data acquisition
and dynamic model execution. Live traffic feeds were used for
training and validation, but are not yet ingested in real time. Simi-
larly, flood scenarios were simulated offline and selected during
analysis rather than being generated dynamically within the inter-
face. As future work, we plan to establish automated data pipelines
that can ingest traffic and flood sensor data in real time, and to ex-
pand the model ’s feedback capabilities to allow real-time reconfig-
uration and alert generation. These enhancements will strengthen the
system’s alignment with full digital twin functionality.
Although the physical sensor network (i.e., node positions and
distances) is fixed, the graph structure in this study is dynamic be-
cause edges are defined based on the presence of flood inundation
between sensor nodes. Specifically, an edge exists between two no-
des only if the flood model indicates active inundation along the
path connecting them. As floodwaters rise or recede, these connec-
tions are dynamically updated —edges are created when inundation
appears and removed when it subsides —effectively reconfiguring
the graph to reflect evolving roadway accessibility. This data-driven
structure allows the model to capture both the direct effects of
flooding (blocked or inaccessible roadways) and indirect conse-
quences (e.g., rerouting or congestion) through predicted speed
data. Although the model does not simulate vehicle-level trajecto-
ries, its dynamic edge representation provides a robust foundation
for short-term, networkwide impact assessment during flood events.
This study does not include a calibrated long-term pavement
deterioration model. The VF2 factor (mentioned in the “Literature
Review” section) was used as a static input derived from prior knowl-
edge and infrastructure records. Future work may expand the frame-
work to simulate multievent pavement degradation and recovery
processes.
Conclusion
Securing operational and serviceable roadways under diverse disas-
ter scenarios is always imperative to maintain public safety, evacu-
ation routes, and resiliency. In addition, its prediction and continuing
maintenance are equally critical for the timely delivery of emergency
supplies, disaster relief, and long-term recovery operations. As such,
assessing roadway serviceability during flood disasters is essential
to enhance the safety of our neighborhood and improve long-term
disaster resilience. Although serviceability plays a pivotal role in
managing disaster preparedness and response, it will be critical in
promoting roadway structure and pavement design and proposing
necessary changes in codes and materials. In this regard, this study
proposes a new perspective regarding graph neural networks –based
serviceability evaluation and prediction.
Moreover, this research established a critical baseline for creat-
ing a digital twin capable of modeling and evaluating the multifac-
eted impact of flooding on roadway serviceability and vulnerability
at a network level. Because the digital twin entails a robust, com-
prehensive data hub that can connect multiple sensor-based data
streams and real-time captured information from the physical twin,
serviceability-based data analyses and disaster-related prediction
simulations will be more promoted with the diverse data layers.
This study leverages variations in traffic speed and offers a re-
liable method for forecasting road serviceability during flooding
and the availability of road segments shortly, within the next 2 –6h ,
during flood events. The study provided the following key research
contributions:
 Introduction of a formal definition of short-term roadway serv-
iceability under flood conditions that incorporates both physical
vulnerability and functional operability (including traffic rerout-
ing effects).

To accurately assess the serviceability of roadways under di-

underlying interrelationships between roadway inundation and traf-
fic behavior, particularly the effect of flood events on traffic volume
and flow. Given the availability of historical data sets from previous
disasters, applying the approach to the Houston area yielded reli-
able insights into serviceability prediction and analysis.
However, to enhance the model ’s applicability to broader and
more vulnerable regions, future efforts must incorporate additional
serviceability-related variables into the proposed methodology. With
the identified relationships between traffic data and roadway inun-
dation, we can evaluate flood vulnerability and pavement deteriora-
tion, including surface distress. In addition, the outcomes of this
study will be vital for assessing future traffic flow and access dis-
ruptions, such as road closures and detour plans during flooding
events. In addition, the knowledge can be employed to determine
roadway resilience and adaptation strategies according to their
damage levels and long-term durability. By incorporating flooding
considerations into roadway serviceability analysis, engineers and
planners can better design, maintain, and improve roads to ensure
they remain functional and safe even in flood-prone regions.
Looking forward, the study opens multiple avenues for advanc-
ing resilience strategies in transportation. Incorporating higher-
resolution flood modeling, refining serviceability definitions using
real-time pavement condition monitoring, and expanding the geo-
graphic applicability of the framework will further enhance its utility.
Furthermore, coupling this model with climate adaptation planning
and equity-focused mobility assessments could ensure more inclu-
sive and forward-looking urban resilience initiatives. As climate-
induced extreme weather events continue to escalate, the integration
of AI, digital twins, and dynamic infrastructure modeling will be-
come indispensable tools for safeguarding transportation systems
and the communities they serve.
