ABSTRACT
The increasing frequency of extreme climate events poses signi ﬁcant risks to slope infrastructure, while traditional inspection
methods are often inefﬁ cient and unsafe. Although unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) combined with structure-from-motion
(SfM) provide high- ﬁdelity 3D models, they lack the semantic understanding necessary for automated damage assessment. This
study addresses this gap by developing and validating an anomaly-enhanced digital twin (AEDT) framework. The proposed
system integrates multiview UAV imagery, SfM-based 3D reconstruction, and a convolutional neural network (CNN) for auto-
mated anomaly classiﬁcation. This information is then fused into an interactive, geographic information system (GIS)-compatible
DT platform for lifecycle management. A case study on a soil and water conservation (SWC) structure in central Taiwan was
conducted for veri ﬁcation. The deep learning module achieved a macroaverage F1-score of 0.81, demonstrating balanced perfor-
mance across erosion, spalling, siltation, and collapse classes. This was validated on a held-out test set derived from a total of 2000
annotated images spanning four anomaly types with three severity levels. Furthermore, the AEDT-based work ﬂow reduced on-
site inspection time by ~63 % compared to conventional manual methods. The resulting AEDT model provides a dynamic,
semantically enriched 3D representation of the infrastructure, linking geometric data with damage attributes and historical
maintenance records. This research demonstrates a feasible and scalable solution for intelligent infrastructure monitoring,
offering a robust tool for enhancing climate resilience and enabling proactive asset management.
1 | Introduction
The frequency and intensity of extreme climate events have
increased signiﬁcantly in recent decades, posing escalating risks
to soil engineering and civil infrastructure systems. Extreme pre-
cipitation, prolonged heatwaves, and intensiﬁed typhoons not only
accelerate the physical degradation of infrastructure components—
such as surface erosion, cracking, and material fatigue —but also
destabilize geotechnical systems through hydrologicalﬂuctuations,
increased pore water pressure, and slope saturation.
Soil and water conservation (SWC) structures, which play a critical
role in mitigating sediment transport, maintaining slope stability,
and protecting downstream communities, are particularly vulnera-
ble. This vulnerability is compounded in mountainous or remote
terrains where SWC installations are often located. In such areas,
routine inspection and maintenance are hindered by inaccessibility,
complex topography, and unpredictable weather patterns. As a
result, latent damage may go undetected, increasing the likelihood
of progressive deterioration and sudden failure.

These growing challenges necessitate a paradigm shift in monitor-
ing practices—moving from periodic, manual inspection methods
toward intelligent, automated, and spatially contextualized solu-
tions capable of operating in dynamic and data-scarce environ-
ments. To address this need, researchers are increasingly using
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped with high-resolution
sensors to conduct infrastructure inspections. This technology has
become an important alternative to traditional ground surveys due
to its high ef ﬁciency, safety in accessing hard-to-reach areas, and
ability to obtain high-precision data.
However, simply collecting large amounts of image data is not
enough in itself; the potential of the raw data needs to be fully
unleashed through advanced processing techniques. To this end,
UAV photogrammetry combined with structure-from-motion
(SfM) technology can quickly and noncontact produce dense 3D
point clouds that accurately represent the surface morphology. The
advantage of such reconstructions is that they can provide high-
ﬁdelity geometric models, which are particularly useful for quanti-
tatively recording erosion, deformation, and geometric anomalies.
However, the main limitation of this method is that its output is
purely geometric in nature and lacks semantic understanding. In
other words, although the point cloud accurately depicts the
“shape” of the structure, it cannot automatically identify whether
a feature is a “crack,”“ spalling,” or a harmless shadow. This need
for semantic annotation of massive visual data provides an entry
point for the next stage of technology application.
Simultaneously, advances in computer vision, particularly the
application of deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs)—have
greatly improved the accuracy and automation of structural defect
detection. CNN –based models have demonstrated signi ﬁcant
potential and high precision in detecting surface cracks and other
forms of damage. Emerging methods further combine object detec-
tion with generative networks, improving robustness and speed
even under varying lighting and texture conditions.
Despite these advances in technology, current inspection systems
often lack semantic integration and temporal continuity.
UAV–CNN pipelines typically operate in a 2D image space and
are not embedded in a spatially contextualized framework that
supports lifecycle monitoring or predictive analytics. Digital twin
(DT) technology offers a promising solution by creating a dynamic
3D representation of infrastructure linked to sensor data and main-
tenance records. In geotechnical applications, DTs are increasingly
being used for real-time risk prediction and slope deformation mon-
itoring through data fusion and arti ﬁcial intelligence (AI)-driven
analytics. However, implementations tailored for SWC structures
remain limited and often lack the integration of semantic defect
recognition and geometry-aware modeling.
This study addresses this gap by proposing an integrated anomaly-
enhanced DT (AEDT) framework that combines UAV-SfM-based
3D modeling, CNN–based semantic anomaly detection, and rule-
based degradation classiﬁcation in a geographic information system
(GIS)-compatible environment. The goal is to build a scalable smart
system for time-series inspection, condition assessment, and pre-
ventive maintenance of slope infrastructure to enhance its resilience
under changing climate conditions. By connecting geometricﬁdel-
ity and semantic intelligence into a dynamic, inspectable, and upda-
table DT, this study advances theﬁeld of smart SWC infrastructure
monitoring and climate-adaptive resilience.
2 | Literature Review
The increasing demand for automation in infrastructure inspection
has fueled growing research interest in integrating remote sensing,
AI, and digital modeling technologies. For SWC structures—often
located in remote and rugged areas —manual inspection presents
signiﬁcant limitations. As a result, there is a growing need for efﬁ-
cient, objective, and scalable alternatives to traditional practices.
Recent developments emphasize a multidisciplinary convergence,
combining UAV photogrammetry, deep learning algorithms, and
DT frameworks to address these challenges in a more intelligent,
adaptive, and holistic manner.
2.1 | Deep Learning for Structural Anomaly
Detection
With the rapid advancement of computer vision, deep learning has
emerged as a pivotal tool for defect detection. Foundational research
by Cha et al. [1] ﬁrst demonstrated the efﬁcacy of CNNs for robust
crack detection, signiﬁcantly outperforming traditional edge detec-
tion methods. Building on this, Cha et al. [2] further advanced the
ﬁeld by implementing faster region-based CNNs (Faster R-CNN) for
the simultaneous detection of multiple damage types, establishing a
benchmark for automated visual inspection.
In surface defect inspection, Yang et al. [ 3] proposed a deep
learning-based system that utilizes multiscale and channel-
compressed features, combining multiple convolutional layers
with varied kernel sizes to better accommodate cluttered back-
grounds and variably sized defects. For cement concrete bridge
surfaces, Zhu and Song [4 ] developed an intelligent classi ﬁcation
model by modifying the classic VGG-16 network, which achieved
superior accuracy in distinguishing seven distinct defect categories
compared to shallower networks. Similarly, for underground park-
ing structures, Shin et al. [ 5] utilized a Faster R-CNN model to
objectively locate and classify common defects such as paint peeling
and leakage from a large image dataset.
The application of deep learning extends to specialized infrastruc-
ture monitoring. For railway tracks, Dang et al. [ 6] introduced an
efﬁcient image classiﬁcation model that leverages a CNN to analyze
RGB spectral images of ultrasonic signals, enabling high-accuracy
condition identiﬁcation for large-scale monitoring. In water distri-
bution systems, Shukla and Piratla [ 7] developed a deep learning
algorithm using a modi ﬁed AlexNet CNN to detect pipeline lea-
kages from vibration signal images, reaching up to 98 % accuracy.
For internal defects in reinforced concrete, Kuchipudi and Ghosh
[8] presented a region-based CNN model that employs RoIAlign on
ultrasonic images to automatically detect and segmentﬂaws, attain-
ing a mean average precision (mAP) as high as 0.98.
In manufacturing, Meister and Wermes [ 9] evaluated the perfor-
mance of CNN – and RNN –based algorithms for analyzing laser
scanner data, demonstrating that a combined CNN–RNN architec-
ture is highly effective for real-time defect classi ﬁcation in auto-
mated ﬁber placement processes. To provide a broader
perspective, review articles have also been pivotal. Ming et al.
[10] conducted a literature review of deep CNN applications for
defect detection in 3C products, summarizing various frameworks
and analyzing their respective challenges. Likewise, Ali et al. [ 11]
published a comprehensive review on the implementation of CNNs
for crack detection in civil structures, detailing all procedural

2.2 | Application of UAVs in Structural Damage
Assessment and Monitoring
UAVs have become indispensable tools in inspecting dif ﬁcult-to-
access civil structures such as slopes, bridges, and postdisaster
zones. The operational health inspection and postdisaster damage
assessment of civil structures have long been critical challenges in
both research and practice. Traditional visual inspection methods
suffer from high variability, subjectivity, and the substantial cost
and time required for large-scale infrastructure, leading to insufﬁ-
cient temporal resolution for effective decision-making. In recent
years, the rapid development and maturation of UAV technology
has presented new opportunities to address these challenges. With
their advantages of high spatial resolution, capacity to generate
stereoscopic imagery and its derivatives, and platform ﬂexibility,
UAVs have opened numerous new possibilities in theﬁeld of dam-
age mapping. This review aims to synthesize recent key research on
the use of UAVs for structural damage detection, quanti ﬁcation,
and monitoring, providing a comprehensive perspective on the
progress and potential within this ﬁeld.
A critical evolution in this domain is the shift from manual piloting
to fully autonomous systems. Kang and Cha [ 12] developed an
autonomous UAV system utilizing ultrasonic beacons for structural
health monitoring (SHM) in GPS-denied environments, overcom-
ing major navigation limitations in conﬁned spaces. Furthermore,
Ali et al. [ 13] introduced a real-time multiple damage mapping
framework using autonomous UAVs, while recent innovations
have integrated obstacle-avoidance capabilities with ﬁducial
marker-based localization to ensure safe operations in complex
structural geometries [14]. These advancements highlight the grow-
ing feasibility of automated data acquisition in complex structural
environments. Pan et al. [ 15] integrated UAV photogrammetry
with panoramic imaging and fuzzy semantic evaluation, enhancing
both spatial coverage and automated condition assessment for SWC
infrastructure. Kerle et al. [ 16] reviewed the evolution of UAV
applications in structural disaster damage mapping, charting the
progression from providing simple descriptive overviews of disaster
scenes to employing more complex texture and segmentation-based
methods, and ultimately to utilizing advanced deep learning
approaches with multitemporal and multiview imagery for compre-
hensive damage characterization. Their review also explored
emerging developments such as the practicality of UAV mapping
strategies for ﬁrst responders, improvements in machine learning,
and the application of UAVs in GPS-denied environments and
infrastructure maintenance.
For speciﬁc damage assessment of infrastructure like bridges, sev-
eral studies have demonstrated the potential of integrating UAVs
with advanced image processing and AI. Ellenberg et al. [17] dem-
onstrated the use of computational algorithms with UAV-acquired
imagery for quantitative damage assessment, employing a postpro-
cessing framework involving homography and lens distortion
correction to extract precise measurements of deformation, change
detection, and crack patterns. Peng et al. [ 18] developed a UAV-
based machine vision method using hybrid feature learning for
bridge crack identi ﬁcation and width quanti ﬁcation, which inte-
grated an R-FCN network with Haar-AdaBoost to achieve over
90% accuracy in real-world crack measurements. Kao et al. [ 19]
combined the YOLOv4 deep learning model with UAV image pro-
cessing techniques to extract and quantify bridge cracks, achieving
92% accuracy and a width measurement precision of up to 0.22 mm
by converting identiﬁed crack images to binary maps for precise
edge detection.
Beyond static damage assessment, UAVs are also being utilized for
dynamic response monitoring. Wang et al. [ 20]p r o p o s e daU A V -
based video analysis methodology to track earthquake-induced
building displacements. By correcting sensor motion and tracking
natural features at the pixel level, their method extracts dynamic
responses with engineering-grade accuracy (RMSE<2 cm), as vali-
dated by shake-table tests.
The application of UAVs has further expanded to more specialized
and efﬁ cient infrastructure inspections. Benelmostafa and
Medromi [ 21] introduced a multitask YOLO model, named
PowerLine-MTYOLO, for the simultaneous segmentation of power
cables and detection of broken strands from UAV imagery. By
integrating novel modules into a YOLO v8-based architecture, their
model signiﬁcantly improved detection and segmentation perfor-
mance, offering a promising solution for autonomous aerial inspec-
tions. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that UAVs serve as a
powerful tool, signiﬁcantly enhancing the efﬁciency and precision
of structural damage assessment and infrastructure maintenance,
evolving capabilities from qualitative descriptions to precise quan-
titative analysis, dynamic monitoring, and complex multitask
processing.
2.3 | DTs for Infrastructure Maintenance

simulation, has been increasingly applied in civil infrastructure,
particularly since 2020. As three-dimensional digital models
assume an increasingly vital role in domains such as smart cities,
DTs, autonomous driving, and industrial production, the associated
data processing, model construction, and applications have become
central to current research. Recent literature reveals numerous
innovative ﬁndings, from the precise semantic segmentation of
3D point cloud data to the application of DTs that integrate AI
and extended reality (XR) technologies.

cloud segmentation network that leverages a multiscale feature
fusion and transformer architecture to enhance performance by
globally modeling overall shape while embedding local details.
For detailed urban modeling, M ünzinger et al. [ 24] proposed a
GIS work ﬂow to generate detailed 3D tree models from LiDAR
point clouds by classifying the urban forest and parametrically

In summary, although UAVs and AI have proven effective for
enhancing infrastructure inspection, and DTs provide a robust
framework for lifecycle asset management, current applications
often operate in silos. Few existing systems fully integrate UAV-
acquired imagery, AI-based anomaly detection, and semantically
enriched 3D DT models into a uniﬁed, scalable platform. This lack
of convergence creates a signi ﬁcant research gap in critical areas
such as multimodal data integration (e.g., 2D imagery, 3D geomet-
ric reconstructions, and semantic annotations), automated anom-
aly interpretation, and the embedding of inspection results into an
interactive digital environment that facilitates decision-making.
This fragmentation results in missed opportunities for temporal
tracking, contextual interpretation, and predictive analytics, all of
which are essential for proactive infrastructure management. These
limitations are particularly acute for SWC infrastructure, where
challenging topographies and di spersed locations exacerbate
inspection constraints.
This study addresses these gaps through the development of the
AEDT framework, which uniquely integrates: (1) multiview UAV
photogrammetry for comprehensive geometric coverage; (2) deep
learning-based semantic classi ﬁcation of surface anomalies for
enhanced diagnostic accuracy; and (3) level of detail 3 (LOD3)
DT modeling for spatially and semantically enriched representa-
tion. The proposed AEDT model not only links heterogeneous data
sources into a coherent model but also supports spatiotemporal
inspection analysis and dynamic anomaly tracking, enabling intel-
ligent, context-aware decision-making tailored to geotechnical
infrastructure in subtropical, hazard-prone regions. In doing so,
this research provides a novel, end-to-end solution that advances
the ﬁeld beyond existing fragmented approaches and lays a foun-
dation for resilient infrastructure monitoring.
3 | Research Methods
This study constructs an AI-assisted inspection framework for arti-
ﬁcial slope structures. This framework deeply integrates several
advanced technologies to form a complete work ﬂow from data
acquisition to decision support. The core technical path is as fol-
lows: First, multiview image acquisition from UAVs is used to
overcome the limitations of traditional manual inspections in acces-
sibility, safety, and efﬁciency. Next, SfM algorithms are employed
for 3D reconstruction, transforming 2D image sequences into a 3D
geometric model with real-world scale. Subsequently, deep learning
is introduced for anomaly recognition on the structure ’s appear-
ance, automating the process of identifying potential deterioration
or damage from a vast number of images. Finally, through semantic
DT modeling, the geometric model is fused with the identi ﬁed
anomaly information and engineering attribute data. Theﬁnal out-
put of this process is a semantically annotated AEDT model.
The entire AEDT framework can be conceptualized as a four-layer
modular architecture, each corresponding to the technical imple-
mentation detailed in Sections 3.1–3.3:
1. The data acquisition layer focuses on collecting multiangle
UAV imagery and supplemental on-site data to ensure
comprehensive spatial coverage. This step provides the
raw data input for subsequent reconstruction work ﬂows
(see Section 3.1).
2. The model construction layer processes the captured images
using SfM and multiview stereo (MVS) techniques to gener-
ate dense 3D point clouds and photorealistic mesh models.
These geometric models represent the slope infrastructure

3.1 | SfM-Based 3D Modeling
The 3D modeling process in this study begins with high-quality
data acquisition. We utilize a UAV equipped with a high-resolution
RGB camera for aerial photography. To ensure the spatial consis-
tency and georeferencing accuracy of the reconstructed model, the
ﬂight mission employs automated ﬂight path planning with GPS
positioning enabled throughout. During ﬂight planning, we set a
70% forward overlap and a 60% side overlap. Prior to model input,
all raw images underwent quality control. Images with signiﬁcant
motion blur (ﬂagged by a perceptual blur detector) or severe over/
underexposure were excluded to ensure stable SfM reconstruction
and downstream anomaly detection. Flight parameters targeted an
average ground sampling distance (GSD) of ~1.5 cm/pixel. This
high overlap ratio is crucial for ensuring that the SfM algorithm
can ﬁnd enough tie points to robustly solve for camera poses and
scene structure. Theﬂight altitude is maintained at a relative height
of ~25–30 m, and shooting angles and intervals areﬁne-tuned based
on the site’s topography. In addition to automated ﬂights, we sup-
plement with a handheld camera to capture more detailed textures
of key structures or areas obscured by vegetation. After the image
data is imported, it is processed using SfM and MVS techniques to
generate sparse and dense point clouds. Finally, the point cloud
data undergoes meshing and texturing procedures to generate a
photorealistic triangular mesh model, which is then converted to
the LOD3 format. This preserves the structure’sg e o m e t r ya n ds u r -
face information while ensuring a lightweight model. This step
involves feature alignment, camera intrinsic parameter estimation,
dense reconstruction, and mesh generation.
Compared to other 3D reconstruction methods such as LiDAR-only
or SLAM-based approaches, SfM offers several distinct advantages
for this study. LiDAR systems, while highly accurate, are cost-
prohibitive and less accessible in complex terrain or remote areas.
SLAM-based methods are primarily suitable for indoor or limited-
range environments and may struggle with scale estimation in large
outdoor areas. SfM, on the other hand, allows for detailed and
scalable reconstruction using only passive optical sensors, making
it a cost-effective and practical choice for large-scale slope monitor-
ing. Additionally, the integration with UAVs enables coverage of
difﬁ cult-to-access terrain with minimal risk and logistical
complexity.

levels (Grade A/B/C) to align with government maintenance

efﬁcient and robust solution without the need for labor-intensive
pixel-level annotation. Although YOLOv8 has demonstrated effec-
tiveness in rapid object localization tasks in previous literature, its
primary advantage lies in real-time detection speed rather than the
pixel-level accuracy critical for subtle surface anomaly identi ﬁca-
tion. Given this study’s focus on precise anomaly characterization
and relatively limited training data, CNN architecture offers a more
suitable balance between accuracy and computational ef ﬁciency,
making it preferable over YOLOv8 for this speciﬁc application sce-
nario. While transformers offer superior performance on large-scale
datasets and complex semantic tasks, they require signi ﬁcantly
more data and computational resources. YOLOv8, optimized for
real-time object detection, emphasizes speed over pixel-level
granularity, which is less ideal for identifying subtle surface anom-
alies in slope structures. EfﬁcientNet achieves strong performance
with fewer parameters but involves a more complex architecture
not directly supported by the Teachable Machine platform. CNNs,
with their relative simplicity and strong capability in image classi-
ﬁcation, provided a pragmatic and effective solution for this study’s
dataset and application scope.
The model was trained with batch size 16, learning rate 0.001, and
20 epochs, using the Adam optimizer and categorical cross-entropy
loss, with data augmentation (horizontal/vertical ﬂip, /C615° rota-
tion, brightness/contrast jitter, and mild zoom) to improve robust-
ness. Data augmentation strategies included horizontal and vertical
ﬂipping, rotation (/C615°), brightness and contrast adjustments, and
slight zoom transformations. These augmentations were essential
for improving the model ’s robustness to different environmental
conditions and imaging angles. Training was conducted on a work-
station equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU (12 GB
V
RAM). The total training time was ~2 h.

To establish data credibility and enable reproducibility, we report
the dataset composition and labeling protocol. Images were split
at the structure level to prevent leakage, ensuring that imagery
from the same location did not appear in both the training and

experts, including SWC technicians and engineers with over
10 years of ﬁeld experience, following the classiﬁcation standards
in Section 4.4. Interannotator agreement was assessed, achieving a
Cohen’s κ of 0.87. Discrepancies were adjudicated by a third senior
expert (Table 2).

4.2 | Case Management Work ﬂow
This study adopts aﬁve-phase case management process combining
deep learning, AI, and spatial mapping to improve inspection
efﬁciency:
 Phase 1: Upload Test Images: Users begin by accessing the
new form screen to upload site inspection images. These
images are processed using a pretrained deep learning model
that classiﬁes visible defects and recommends maintenance
actions based on recognized patterns.
 Phase 2: Pattern Interpretation and Form Generation: The sys-
tem displays AI-detected pattern results alongside a schematic
of the DT. This helps users understand each case in spatial and
contextual terms. A digital inspection form is automatically
generated and archived in the case management system.
 Phase 3: Location Calibration: Users enter the “Add New ”
interface and spatially locate the relevant inspection zone
using the 3D DT. Accurate spatial tagging is achieved through
user interaction and knowledge of the site.
 Phase 4: Inspection Review: The platform enables users to
visually conﬁrm that each new case is accurately registered.
Semantic links are used to validate all information, and any
missing or inaccurate data can be identi ﬁed and corrected.
Figure 5 shows the user interface during this step.
 Phase 5: Backend Management: Administrators can edit or
delete recorded ﬁles through the backend system. The inter-
face ensures streamlined access, batch management of inspec-
tion records, and ﬂexible data updates.

4.4 | Deep Learning Model Performance
Evaluation
To evaluate the performance of the image classi ﬁcation model
developed for the automated identiﬁcation of structural anomalies,
this paper summarizes and analyzes three validation metrics: con-
fusion matrix, learning curves for training and testing accuracy, and
accuracy per class. First, the learning curves show that the model
converges quickly early in training. After approximately the ﬁfth
epoch, the training accuracy reaches nearly 100 %, while the test
accuracy stabilizes at ~81%. This result demonstrates the model ’s
good learning ability and basic generalization. However, a signiﬁ-
cant gap exists between the training and test curves, which may
reﬂect a slight over ﬁtting phenomenon. To address this and
enhance generalization, we implemented optimization strategies
such as extensive data augmentation and dropout layers, as shown
in the resulting learning curves (Figure 8). After these
optimizations, the model achieved a stable overall test accuracy
of 81%, demonstrating its potential for practical application.
Analyzing the classiﬁcation accuracy of each category, the model
performed exceptionally well in the erosion-B, spalling-C, and
collapse-C categories, achieving accuracy exceeding 0.94. The con-
fusion matrix also showed complete accuracy in the predictions,
indicating that these anomaly types possess distinct visual charac-
teristics that the model has learned effectively. While there were a
few misclassiﬁcations in the medium-to-high accuracy categories,
such as erosion-A (0.88), spalling-A (0.83), spalling-B (0.88), and
siltation-A (0.83), the model maintained high discrimination capa-
bilities overall. These misclassiﬁcations mostly occurred between
different levels of the same type, representing an expected level of
confusion. However, siltation-B and collapse-B performed rela-
tively poorly, with accuracies of only 0.60. The confusion matrix
showed a wide range of predictions, with misclassi ﬁcations into
erosion-A, spalling-B, and collapse-C, potentially due to ambiguous
pattern characteristics, high sample heterogeneity, or data imbal-
ance. To address this, ambiguous samples are removed, the number
of samples is increased to strengthen the clarity of this category, and
a category weight adjustment strategy is introduced into the model

The classiﬁcation standards for each anomaly type are based on
national guidelines such as Taiwan’s “Technical Speciﬁcations for

siﬁed into levels A –C based on depth and area coverage: Level A
(<5 cm depth), Level B (5 –15 cm), and Level C ( >15 cm or severe
surface retreat). Siltation is judged based on the thickness and
spread of sediment coverage, while spalling and collapse follow
criteria including extent, shape, and risk to structural integrity.
Classiﬁcation decisions are primarily based on visual inspection
supplemented by UAV-derived dimensions. Severity levels are
determined by using thresholds on physical parameters such as
anomaly area, shape complexity, and impact proximity to key struc-
tural zones.
A comprehensive analysis shows that this model achieved an
overall test accuracy of 81 %, with good recognition capabilities
for most common anomaly categories, demonstrating its potential
for practical application. To address the dif ﬁculty in identifying
low-accuracy categories and model over ﬁtting, subsequent
improvements were made through data augmentation, model
architecture optimization, and training strategy adjustments, ulti-
mately improving system stability and reliability. Beyond overall
accuracy (81 %), we report class-balanced metrics: the model
achieved a macroaveraged F1-score of 0.79 and a microaveraged
F1-score of 0.81. For the challenging siltation-B class, precision
was 0.55 and recall 0.60, re ﬂecting visual similarity with adjacent
severity levels. To quantify uncertainty, we performed bootstrap
resampling (1000 replicates) on the test set; the 95 % conﬁdence
interval for the macro-F1 was 0.76 –0.82, indicating a stable per-
formance estimate.

5 | Discussion
5.1 | Broader Implications and Bene ﬁts
Application results show that retaining structures—often rendered
as surface elements—present visualization challenges due to low-
texture realism. In contrast, river channel structures allow for more
precise texture reproduction and were chosen for demonstrative
modeling. The key beneﬁts of the DT system in water conservation
include:
 Design and planning: Enables early-stage simulation for
enhanced design decision-making.
 Real-time monitoring: Uses IoT sensors for continuous facility
condition tracking.
 Predictive maintenance: Anticipates maintenance needs
based on degradation trends.
 Collaborative integration: Provides a shared interface for
stakeholder communication.
 Disaster response: Supports early warnings and structured
emergency workﬂows.
 Sustainability modeling: Simulates environmental impacts to
inform long-term strategies.
In summary, the 3D DT developed in this project enhances water
conservation inspection through semantic modeling, AI-driven
defect detection, and immersive visualization. It reduces inspection
time and costs, increases accuracy, and offers a scalable framework
for future public infrastructure monitoring. Theseﬁndings not only
reinforce the technical feasibility of AI-enabled slope inspection but
also contribute toward broader climate resilience strategies. By
embedding DT technologies into environmental infrastructure sys-
tems, this research aligns with the goals of sustainable infrastruc-
ture (SDG 9) and climate adaptation (SDG 13), offering actionable
insights for policy makers and ﬁeld engineers managing landslide-
prone regions.
Moreover, ﬁeld interviews with maintenance engineers indicated
that the AEDT system signi ﬁcantly streamlined documentation
workﬂows, reducing the time spent on compiling visual inspection
reports by over 40 %. Additionally, the spatial tagging feature
enabled direct correlation between observed defects and mainte-
nance logs, facilitating cross-departmental coordination and audit
compliance.
Speciﬁc use-case evaluations demonstrated that predictive mainte-
nance was achievable through early identiﬁcation of minor erosion
patterns that historically preceded larger slope failures—allowing
preemptive intervention weeks before visual symptoms intensiﬁed.
In disaster response scenarios, the system’s auto-generated reports
and spatially calibrated inspection summaries could be exported
within minutes in standardized formats, signiﬁcantly accelerating
emergency response coordination and reducing reliance on poste-
vent manual surveys. In demonstration ﬁeld sites, management
authorities reported that the system effectively reduced on-site
inspection labor requirements and assisted in prioritizing mainte-
nance scheduling decisions based on spatial anomaly patterns and
severity tags.
5.2 | Limitations of the Current Study
Despite its promising performance, the current system is con-
strained by reliance on RGB imagery, which limits detection of
subsurface or visually occluded defects, such as those hidden by
vegetation. Environmental disturbances like lighting variation or
UAV motion artifacts also affect detection robustness. Future
improvements may include the integration of multispectral or ther-
mal imaging sensors to enhance the identi ﬁcation of partially
obscured or concealed damage. Additionally, incorporating UAV
stabilization modules and low-light enhancement algorithms could
signiﬁcantly improve image quality and detection reliability under
challenging environmental conditions.
6 | Conclusion and Future Directions
This study developed an integrated inspection framework tailored
for artiﬁcial slopes and SWC structures, combining UAV imaging,
CNN, and semantic-enhanced SfM 3D reconstruction. The result-
ing system successfully achieved intelligent ﬁeld-level inspections
that signiﬁcantly reduced manual labor demands while enhancing
consistency and visualization of structural anomalies.
The proposed system consists of ﬁve modular components: (1)
multiview UAV data acquisition; (2) SfM-based 3D model genera-
tion; (3) CNN –based anomaly pattern classi ﬁcation; (4) semantic
embedding into a DT model; and (5) an interactive visualization
and maintenance reporting interface. Field deployment at a water
conservation infrastructure site demonstrated a classiﬁcation accu-
racy of 81% for ﬁve distinct anomaly categories and reduced inspec-
tion time by over 60% compared to traditional methods.
A key contribution of this research is the introduction of the AEDT
model, which integrates both geometric and semantic attributes—
including damage type, severity, spatial distribution, and temporal
change. This enriched digital representation enables enhanced
maintenance scheduling, long-term monitoring, and risk-based pri-
oritization, laying the foundation for intelligent asset management
in hydrological engineering contexts.
Future work should focus on integrating LiDAR or multispectral
imaging for structural penetration, as well as edge AI inference
modules to facilitate real-time, in situ decision-making under
diverse terrain and weather conditions. Ultimately, this research
demonstrates the feasibility and value of embedding deep learning
and DT technologies into slope infrastructure inspection work-
ﬂows. It offers a scalable foundation for the broader adoption of
smart monitoring systems in environmental risk management,
infrastructure resilience, and climate adaptation planning.
