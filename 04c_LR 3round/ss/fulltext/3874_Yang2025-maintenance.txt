ABSTRACT
The primary function of healthcare facility environments is to support the recovery of its occupants. Numerous 
studies have examined how indoor environmental quality (IEQ) and physical design factors in healthcare fa -
cilities affect occupants and the effectiveness of control methods, respectively. However, results from indepen -
dent studies on influential elements may conflict, lack transferability, and recoverability. Therefore, a holistic 
review on indoor environments and related control methods for healthcare facilities from the restorative 
perspective is needed. This study analyzes 249 articles to examine the indoor environment ’ s impact on residents ’ 
restorative perceptions and related control behaviors. It also explores automation modeling methods across 
various healthcare settings. The results show that the setting of different environments and occupant roles in 
healthcare buildings can have an impact on the evaluation of the indoor environment. Indoor environmental 
factors are diverse but lack generalization, and existing standards and models fail to adequately address practical 
needs, highlighting the need for expanded cross-disciplinary research. Although qualitative evaluation and re -
sults on IEQ and physical design factors could be applied to satisfy some groups ’ needs, there is a lack of 
quantitative, personalized and dynamic evaluation frameworks in current healthcare environments. Existing 
control methods are still predominantly manual, relying on personal literacy and perception, and lack integration 
and application with automated control technologies. To bridge this gap, this paper outlines future directions and 
designs a conceptual framework, offering insights for stakeholders on design standards and advanced technology 
applications to better align healthcare interiors with occupants ’ needs.
1. Introduction
Healthcare facilities, among the most complex buildings in modern 
society, aim to improve patient safety, enhance operational efficiency, 
and reduce environmental impact [ 49 , 330 ]. The primary mission of 
these facilities extends beyond medical treatment to creating environ -
ments that actively support healing, recovery, and the well-being of all 
occupants — patients, healthcare staff, and visitors [ 141 , 152 ]. With ris -
ing physician burnout attributed to workplace stressors and chaotic 
work environments [ 218 ], there is an urgent need to optimize health -
care indoor environments through occupant-centric approaches.
The concept of "healing environments" has gained significant mo -
mentum in recent years [ 141 , 190 ], emphasizing spaces that psycho -
logically comfort and empower both patients and healthcare providers 
[ 31 ]. Research has consistently demonstrated that indoor environ -
mental quality (IEQ) profoundly impacts occupant well-being [ 18 ], 
patient recovery rates, and staff performance [ 152 , 324 ]. Current 
research hotspots include smart healthcare facilities leveraging adaptive 
environmental control technologies [ 13 , 258 ] and investigations into the 
psychological impacts of environmental factors [ 191 ].
Despite these advances, several critical gaps persist in the field. First, 
there is no universal standard for IEQ in healthcare settings that 
adequately considers individual needs and perceptions [ 298 , 305 ]. Sec -
ond, the impact of key environmental factors — lighting [ 43 , 174 ], 
acoustics [ 187 , 286 ], thermal conditions [ 324 ], and indoor air quality 
(IAQ) [ 137 , 252 ] — remain underexplored, particularly their interactive 
effects [ 210 ]. Third, existing frameworks lack comprehensive integra -
tion of environmental assessment and control strategies specifically 
tailored for healthcare contexts [ 69 , 209 ].
Current theoretical frameworks, while valuable, present significant 
limitations. The environment – behavior (E – B) model focuses primarily 
on Alzheimer ’ s patients [ 328 ], while the environ -
ment – human-performance framework has been validated mainly in 
educational settings [ 40 – 42 ]. The environment – occupant – health

1.1. Research objectives
The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive, 
occupant-centric framework for assessing and controlling indoor envi -
ronments in healthcare facilities. Specific objectives include: 
1. Systematically review literature on indoor environmental assessment 
and control in healthcare facilities
2. Evaluate current research status across different subjects, scenarios, 
and contexts
3. Comprehensively assess the impact of healthcare indoor environ -
ments on occupants’ restorative perceptions
4. Synthesize existing occupant control behaviors and automation 
models
5. Identify occupant-centric operational challenges and future devel -
opment directions
6. Develop an individual-based environmental assessment system and 
comprehensive control framework
1.2. Research methodology and structure
This study employs a systematic literature review methodology 
combined with scientometric analysis to examine peer-reviewed publi -
cations from multiple databases. The review follows established pro -
tocols for healthcare environment research, incorporating both 
quantitative analysis of research trends and qualitative assessment of 
theoretical frameworks.
The paper is structured into seven sections: Section 1 introduces the 
research context and objectives; Section 2 presents the methodology and 
review procedures; Section 3 provides quantitative analysis results; 
Section 4 assesses environmental factors influencing restorative per -
ceptions; Section 5 examines control strategies and automation models; 
Section 6 discusses findings and proposes the new framework; and 
Section 7 concludes with implications and future directions.
1.3. Strengths and limitations
As getting a clear understanding of healthcare environments is a 
multidisciplinary field, analyzing previous reviews is necessary. Based 
on the literature review method and related results in the second section, 
30 review papers related to this research topic were identified in the 
Appendix, covering physical design, IEQ, and control methods (e.g., 
behavioral adjustments, HVAC controls) [ 149 ]. Recent reviews, like 
Shen et al. [ 254 ] and Eijkelenboom and Bluyssen [ 88 ], have a 
comprehensive understanding but fail to integrate physical design as 
well as occupant behavior with environmental factors and control 
methods, revealing a gap in understanding their interactions in health -
care facilities.
Based on the analysis above, strengths of this study include: (1) 
Comprehensive scope covering multiple environmental factors and 
occupant types; (2) Integration of assessment and control methodolo -
gies; (3) Focus on healthcare-specific requirements; (4) Development of 
individual evaluation framework; (5) Synthesis of interdisciplinary 
research findings.
Limitations include: (1) Reliance on existing literature may limit 
findings to published research; (2) Framework validation requires future 
empirical testing; (3) Geographic and cultural variations in healthcare 
practices may affect generalizability; (4) Rapid technological advances 
may require framework updates.
The findings of this research will provide valuable insights for 
healthcare facility designers, facility managers, and policymakers 
seeking to create optimal healing environments that support both pa -
tient recovery and staff well-being.
2. Methodology and procedures
This study employs a systematic literature review (SLR) com -
plemented by critical literature review (CLR) elements to synthesize 
literature. This current article used SLR method [ 54 ] first, which fol -
lowed by Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and 
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) criteria [ 219 ], to identify, assess, and syn -
thesize all the studies relevant to the research scopes and then applied 
CLR method to further analyze the findings. SLR is a scientific approach 
that involves systematically evaluating and interpreting literature in a 
specific field, aiming to identify, analyze, and synthesize key findings, 
methodologies, and gaps in existing research [ 280 ]. CLR method em -
phasizes critical thinking and objective assessment to understand the 
current state of knowledge and contributes to ongoing scholarly 
discourse. This dual approach aligns with prior IEQ reviews that 
combine systematic synthesis with critical evaluation to address both 
technical and interpretive dimensions (E.g., [ 210 ]). This review adopted 
the SLR methodology based on previous research [ 126 , 333 ] and 
comprised four consecutive steps: 
Step 1: Define the conceptual boundaries of the research.
Step 2: Identify key questions on the research.
Step 3: Literature search and selection.
Step 4: Scientometric analysis of collected bibliographies.
2.1. Description of perceptions of restorative on occupants
Identifying the specific research area is critical before doing the 
literature review as the definitions of perceptions of restorative on oc -
cupants in healthcare facilities are different in the multidisciplinary 
presented in current research [ 3 , 22 , 80 , 190 , 205 , 294 ]. Current discourse 
on how occupants react to indoor environments encompasses various 
concepts, such as comfort [ 233 ], well-being [ 122 , 303 ], satisfaction 
[ 19 ], and attention [ 263 ]. Some studies have examined these aspects 
from multiple angles to evaluate how occupants perceive their envi -
ronments, highlighting the intersections among these concepts [ 17 ,
152 ]. Within the healthcare architecture, the design of indoor envi -
ronments is pivotal to promoting healing [ 221 ]. For instance, Mahmood 
and Tayib [ 191 ] identify four critical design factors—interior appear -
ance, privacy, comfort and control—as essential in creating a healing 
environment. This notion is expanded by incorporating factors like 
natural settings, IAQ, lighting, noise, thermal comfort, and aesthetics 
[ 66 , 80 ], which are recognized for their contribution to enhancing re -
covery, health, and work efficiency in healthcare settings [ 85 , 207 , 303 ].
Environmental psychology introduces the term “restorative percep -
tion” to assess the degree to which an environment supports an occu -
pant’s recovery, health, and stress alleviation [ 3 , 216 ]. Such 
environments are expected to promote positive health outcomes, 
improve mood, reduce anxiety, and empower individuals with a sense of 
control, covering both psychological and physiological aspects [ 97 , 190 ,
193 ]. Traditionally, the quality of therapeutic environments was eval -
uated using satisfaction and acceptance as key metrics [ 216 ], with as -
sessments incorporating both subjective and objective measurements 
[ 109 ].
Considering the diverse needs of healthcare facility occu -
pants—patients focusing on recovery [ 141 ], visitors on comfort [ 233 ], 
and staffs on efficiency [ 116 , 288 ] — this paper proposes using the 
concept of restorative perception to elucidate the specific requirements 
of different individuals. This strategy enables a nuanced assessment of 
healthcare indoor environmental quality and the development of

2.2. Identifying key questions
Based on the above analyses, three key questions could be proposed 
for the research: 
RQ 1: What is the current state of research on the indoor environ -
ment of hospitals?
RQ 2: What are the indoor environmental and design factors that 
influence an individual ’ s sense of restorative perception?
RQ 3: What are the existing regulatory behavior and related control 
models that meet the regulatory requirements of restorative 
perception?
2.3. Literature search and selection criteria
This review process is followed by PRISMA criteria [ 219 ]. This sec -
tion defines the search criteria and screening procedure in the literature 
review process. As shown in Fig. 1 , from the main scientific databases, 
the Scopus, and Web of Science (WOS) databases were selected for the 
peer-reviewed literature search on the restorative perceptions of occu -
pants in healthcare facilities and their relationships by searching in title, 
abstract, and keywords. The PubMed database was not chosen because it 
mainly collects research aimed at the life sciences and biomedical sci -
ences, which is not compatible with the theme of this paper. The final 
search was conducted on March 3, 2024, and a search for updated 
literature was conducted on August 11, 2024.
The first step is to define the scope of the provisions. According to the 
objective of this review, the search keywords should cover people, 
environment, perception, IEQ or design, and control five areas. The 
types and terms used by the authors searched for the titles, abstracts, and 
keywords of articles published in English between 1977 and 2024 are 
listed in Table 1 .
In the second step, we filtered the types of articles, and due to the 
incompleteness of some conference papers, “ articles ” and “ reviews ” of 
journal literature were selected, while other types of literature were 
excluded. The main restriction applied to three primary categories of 
“ environmental science ” , “ engineering ” and “ energy ” , where the sub -
categories in the WOS included (Engineering Environmental or 
Psychology or Engineering Civil or Construction Building Technology or 
Management or Environmental Sciences or Multidisciplinary Sciences or 
Computer Science Information Systems or Ergonomics or Environmental 
Studies or Green Sustainable Science Technology or Architecture).
In the final phase, the relevance of the articles was evaluated. Two 
reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of articles independently. The 
first reviewer extracted the data into an Excel spreadsheet and the data 
were verified by second reviewer. For discrepancies that occurred dur -
ing data extraction, a third reviewer was consulted to reach consensus. 
In detail, an abstract-based screening was conducted to filter out liter -
ature primarily focused on nursing services. After this initial assessment, 
the remaining articles were reviewed to determine their alignment with 
the review ’ s research objectives and questions; those that did not align 
were excluded. Ultimately, this process resulted in a curated selection of 
249 articles that were included in the final classification for this review 
( Tables A and B ).

Publication trends for the eight hottest research sites over the last

and initial occupant experience [ 283 ]. In contrast, IEQ, as defined in 
environmental engineering, includes dynamic, measurable conditions 
like air quality, lighting, acoustics, and thermal comfort, which directly 
influence occupant health and well-being [ 32 ]. Psychologically, phys -
ical design facilitates restorative settings through features like natural 
views, while IEQ supports immediate sensory comfort, as per Attention

To answer RQ2, a whole analysis and assessments of the indoor 
environment for healthcare facilities are shown in this section, which

4.1. Assessment of design factors
Ulrich ’ s theory [ 283 ] of supportive design conceptualizes how the 
healthcare physical-social environment affects patients ’ well-being and 
was tested by Andrade and Devlin [ 30 ] showing that design features had 
a positive effect on human perceptions. Proper layout design decreases 
healthcare facilities ’ risks and increases customer satisfaction and ser -
vice quality [ 74 , 170 , 221 ]. In healthcare, designing is complex [ 133 ] 
and design factors have a great impact on occupants, apart from the 
efficiency and safety requirements [ 245 ], identifying a satisfactory 
environment is a second priority for designers, especially since the 
actual impact of each design factor has not been clarified. So, deter -
mining interior design is related to two key stakeholders: the designer 
and the user. In this part, we would like to evaluate the interior design 
from two perspectives.
4.1.1. Perspective of occupants
From the perspective of users, identifying key design factors that 
influence human perception is of paramount importance. Research has 
utilized various methods, including questionnaires like the H-PTPS [ 17 ], 
to isolate significant design elements.
However, the classification methods are not identified in published 
research, some researchers have proposed frameworks to evaluate 
interior design or rank the design factors. Han et al. [ 130 ] proposed a 
framework for Intelligent Healthscope Quality (IHQ), identifying five 
dimensions and 17 factors as reliable and valid measurements of health 
scope evaluation to cater to diverse needs and preferences. Another 
study found that furniture and spatial connections are crucial di -
mensions affecting the restorative quality of the ward environment, 
identifying four principal components (the space, furniture, safety 
feature, and privacy) and seven sub-dimensions [ 19 ]. Mahmood and 
Tayib [ 190 ] demonstrated a strong relationship between the quality of 
the healing environment and patient satisfaction, identifying four key 
factors: Interior appearance, Comfort and control, Views, and Privacy, 
which was similar to the results by Mahmood and Tayib [ 191 ]. In the 
research by Zhao and Mourshed [ 341 ], design factors ranked in terms of 
importance, with cleanliness, environment, and safety design emerging 
as top priorities while entertainment facilities and pleasant exterior 
views were deemed less critical.
Despite IEQ-related factors and control behaviors in literature, based 
on previous experience and literature collection results, the design fac -
tors in this paper are set into three parts: Decoration [ 104 , 114 , 134 , 205 ], 
nature [ 46 , 65 , 66 , 80 , 143 , 154 , 189 , 205 , 215 , 294 , 316 , 321 ], and spatiality 
[ 71 , 113 , 116 , 138 , 343 ].
For the decoration of healthcare facilities ’ interior environment, the 
decoration concluded with artwork and furniture layout. Environmental 
factors affecting patient outcomes include form, unit layout, floor ma -
terial, room features, medical equipment visibility, nature, lighting, and 
music [ 257 ]. A study by Gao and Zhang [ 114 ] identified 19 design 
characteristics that significantly correlate with the restrictiveness of 
ward environments, including nature, artwork, doorway position, 
furniture layout, room size, and wall color. Ferrante and Villani [ 104 ] 
introduced the photometric characteristics of finishing materials and 
furnishings that would affect the daylight. The wall color may influence 
users ’ emotions [ 259 ]. Song et al. [ 264 ] showed that the color percep -
tion by eye tracking and the color preference by survey did not tend to 
match, as pink attracted a high level of attention, regardless of personal 
preference. Based on virtual reality, Amirbeiki Tafti et al. [ 27 ] demon -
strated that incorporating natural blue elements in PET/CT scan room 
design elicits positive physiological responses in patients, enhancing the 
vitality of medical imaging environments. Facility design features can 
also promote healthy work environments to motivate nurses to continue 
working [ 266 ]. As to artworks, Nejati et al. [ 205 ] compared many 
photos with different elements and identified landscape artworks that 
had greater restorative value than indoor plants. It is similar to Hill et al. 
[ 134 ], who determined that the addition of music and natural landscape 
images could release patients undergoing pelvic reconstructive surgery.
Nature attracted many researchers ’ attention and they used biophilia 
design [ 3 , 86 , 129 , 143 , 321 ] to improve the positive affect on occupants. 
Patients specify a strong need for connection with nature [ 66 ]. Incor -
porating plant walls and digital elements into healthcare environments 
can synergistically enhance emotional well-being. This combination not 
only diminishes negative emotions but also fosters positive emotional 
changes, thereby having a complementary effect on the overall mood of 
individuals [ 154 ]. Berg et al. [ 45 ] discovered that the greening of a 
geriatric ward may support patient well-being and reduce functional 
decline. Nejati et al. [ 205 ] illustrated that physical access to the out -
doors had greater value than window views. People often incorporate

touch, may be helpful to the understanding of gardens [ 65 ].
Spatiality is the traditional design factor which designers consider. It 
impacts patient satisfaction like the centralized unit transformed 
decentralized unit [ 138 ]. In general, the focus of spatiality is to protect 
occupant privacy [ 64 ] and safety [ 113 ]. For special groups such as the 
visually impaired, they can understand the principles of spatial organi -
zation normally, but vision and the length of time spent in the work 
environment can affect the spatial cognition of the visually impaired, so 
it is necessary to enhance the safety of the space to enhance their fa -
miliarity with the specific space [ 343 ]. Cho [ 71 ] revealed spatial qual -
ity, social program, and space for the successful delivery of therapeutic 
healthcare design. A study by Gao and Zhang [ 114 ] identified that 
doorway position and room size would influence patients ’ recovery. 
Relatively spacious rooms make patients feel free by providing enough 
space for their families. For doorway locations, the different relative 
positions between the door and the bed may lead to different percep -
tions of acoustic and visual privacy, as the door has the function of 
partitioning and connecting. Faghiholislam et al. [ 97 ] suggested that 
safe spaces, such as suitable positions of furniture, could help reduce 
depression in therapeutic spaces. Moreover, patients with high sensory 
and hygienic design requirements prefer single rooms [ 341 ].
4.1.2. Perspective of designers
Healthcare facilities are one of the most complicated buildings [ 49 ]. 
The complex spatial environment may impact cognitive mapping [ 203 ] 
and the layout design assures the quality of healthcare facilities services 
[ 221 ]. As to the division methods, based on previous research, it could 
be divided into three categories: Materials, layout, and circulation.
Selecting suitable materials is crucial for designers. In interior 
design, materials often affect 1) flooring, 2) walls, 3) ceilings, and 4) 
furniture. Flooring is often related to sound characteristics [ 223 , 284 ], as 
the materials affect the vibration. Walls are not only responsible for 
carrying artwork but also affect human cognition, especially the wall 
color is seen as a main factor in restorative perception [ 114 ]. As warm 
colors and cold colors are opposites of the color temperature, they have 
arousal-inducing and calming properties, respectively, which should be 
considered in suitable scenarios [ 264 ]. Ceilings are similar to floorings 
as they also affect the sound [ 223 ], heating [ 105 , 262 ] and pollutants 
[ 292 ]. Furniture is a broad concept, as it could be related to green, 
artwork, and decorations. Incorporating natural materials or 
nature-inspired elements can significantly boost patient recovery and 
reduce stress [ 104 , 244 ]. Using safe and easily cleaned surface materials 
[ 244 ] is also significant for furniture usage.
Layout design is a crucial perspective for physical environment 
design. For example, visual factors, physical environmental factors, and 
human – environment interactive factors significantly impact the spatial 
cognition of visually impaired people [ 343 ]. The layout of nursing units 
significantly impacts healthcare delivery and patient experience [ 88 ]. 
Decentralized nursing units are considered best practice, as centralized 
layouts restrict visual access to patient rooms [ 244 ], but Hua et al. [ 138 ] 
considered centralized or decentralized unit designs to analyze effective 
team member communication and illustrated decentralized unit design 
may decrease communications. Besides, inpatient unit design and pa -
tient experience are also connected, which reflects that the hybrid 
decentralized design supplies a more optimal experience than central -
ized design [ 58 ]. Single-bed rooms enhance infection control, privacy, 
and confidentiality, benefiting patients but decreasing the satisfaction of 
healthcare staff [ 88 ]. Additionally, incorporating a family zone or 
waiting zone within these settings provides a dedicated space for pa -
tients, their families, and visitors to gather, supporting a more inclusive 
and comforting environment [ 228 ].
Circulation refers to the way people move through and interact with 
a building, and it is often related to spatiality. Evaluating the circulation 
in healthcare facilities should involve many perspectives [ 172 ], 
including efficiency, restoration, connectivity [ 117 ] and so on. A study 
proposed combining design factors in therapeutic spaces, which could 
extract effective cycles to reduce patients ’ depression [ 97 ]. Geng et al. 
[ 117 ] used space syntax to test spatial arrangement, including way -
finding, spatial integration, visibility from the nursing station, connec -
tivity for public space and visual integration, suggesting the nursing 
station should be set to enhance visibility and operational efficiency. As 
to the room design, designers should consider the room size and 
doorway position [ 113 ]. Cai et al. [ 58 ] also concluded that room design 
should align with and support care flow, operational model, and call 
button communication technology, as well as improve visibility and 
proximity between patient rooms and nurse stations, to improve patient 
experience. Cho [ 71 ] emphasized that safety is one of the most impor -
tant parameters in healthcare facilities, which needs to be assured in the 
entry layout design, airflow circulation and flow of occupants. As to the 
waiting area, Qi et al. [ 228 ] proposed the distance from the waiting area 
to the consulting room should be < 20 m, with no more than one turning 
point, which could help to keep a suitable flow organization and 
improve satisfaction. For the visually impaired [ 343 ], keeping safe, 
colorful especially green spaces and stylized traditional architectural 
forms could increase their behaviors in space recognition.
4.2. Assessment of IEQ factors
In recent years, some reviews summarized the various factors influ -
encing IEQ within healthcare facilities. However, a critical gap in the 
existing literature is the infrequent integration of these factors into 
possible assessment metrics that can be correlated with individual 
feedback. This omission limits the ability to provide a comprehensive 
and nuanced understanding of how specific IEQ components affect oc -
cupants ’ restorative perceptions. Besides, previous research focused on 
single factor individual assessment, the whole assessment of IEQ is 
lacking.
4.2.1. Assessment of visual environment
Firstly, the visual environment is summarized. As interior design 
factors are collected in the 4.1 section, the lighting environment is the 
main research object in this section. The primary research on lighting 
environments can be categorized into two types: natural and artificial 
light, and their impacts on occupants [ 196 ].
In lighting assessments, brightness, illuminance, and color temper -
ature are the main components for evaluating light sources [ 24 ]. Natural 
light, which offers higher brightness and better color rendering, is more 
susceptible to variations due to time and weather conditions. In practice, 
natural light is often associated with views and windows, and most of 
the time, both patients and staff showed a preference for natural light or 
conditions near windows, despite potential glare related to the reflec -
tivity of interior materials [ 104 ]. Choi, Beltran, and Kim [ 73 ] showed 
that exposure to natural light can reduce stress, increase satisfaction, 
and work efficiency for staff, and alleviate pain and depression for pa -
tients, thereby enhancing patient satisfaction. Overall, ample cumula -
tive photic flux leads to higher satisfaction, with employees exposed to 
longer periods of daylight reporting greater job satisfaction [ 50 ]. 
Additionally, the impact of lighting, natural and artificial, on humans is 
influenced by location [ 93 ]. The influence of natural light necessitates 
considering factors like bed position, curtains, and windows, combined 
with patients ’ actual needs [ 104 ], while the impact of artificial light 
sources also requires assessment of illuminance, spectral composition, 
control strategies, and actual environmental needs.
The illuminance and spectrum of light sources are associated with 
staff satisfaction, performance, and visual fatigue [ 145 ], as well as pa -
tients ’ sleep and perception. Environmental deficiencies such as thermal 
conditions and light intensity, along with the lack of proper manage -
ment plans for night shift nurses, are detrimental and negative factors

for the health and well-being of this workforce [ 36 ]. Regarding lighting 
requirements in various hospital settings, many respondents reported no 
significant differences between professions. Concerning control strate -
gies, these relate to the type of fixtures and reflectivity [ 232 ], with glare, 
flicker, and shadows to be avoided as they can affect performance, 
alertness, and sleep patterns [ 250 ]. Meanwhile, hospital lighting control

avoiding blue wavelengths at night is necessary to prevent disrupting

Long-term occupants, or workers (healthcare staff), have higher and 
more diverse demands for indoor lighting, potentially conflicting with 
the needs of short-term occupants (patients and visitors). However, 
overall, their needs are similar, desiring higher daylight flux and more 
color during the day, with satisfaction increasing under sufficient con -
trol [ 196 ].
4.2.2. Assessment of acoustic environment
When it comes to the acoustic environment, the noisy environment 
in hospitals is often problematic [ 44 , 185 , 226 ]. Regardless in general or 
ICU wards, noise often exceeds the standard value [ 29 ]. However, the 
sources of noise are different, and its effects are different. Previous 
studies have often categorized sounds into two categories: noise and 
music [ 254 ]. Given the differences in individual perceptions, music is 
often thought to help heal patients [ 70 , 134 , 281 ] but it may also be 
considered noise at times [ 175 ]. In this research, we discussed acoustic 
environment from the source, the complex composition and whether it is 
conscious.
Firstly, the sources of indoor sound in healthcare facilities were 
analyzed, and the influencing factors mainly included four parts: inter -
nal sound of buildings (ventilation and air conditioning equipment 
sound, cleaning equipment sound, cart, door, etc. [ 25 , 251 , 284 ]); 
building exterior sounds (external sounds transmitted through building 
openings, envelopes, pipes, etc.); human activity sounds (staff conver -
sations, visitor voices, snoring, footsteps, etc. [ 153 , 251 ]; actively 
creating a sound environment (Music [ 70 , 231 ], Television, Alarm 
[ 217 ]).
The second is the composition of sounds, i.e. the proportion of 
different sounds, which is often expressed in terms of the acoustic 
complexity index [ 102 ]. Overall, complex sound environments can 
contribute to cardiovascular disease, sleep disturbances, and hearing 
loss among residents. For patients who are asleep, noise impacts brain 
and cardiovascular functions by influencing heart rate and blood pres -
sure, subsequently deteriorating their sleep quality [ 29 ]. For patients in 
certain settings, such as the ICU, the complex composition of sound can 
cause fear and distress [ 29 ]. At the same time, healthcare workers suffer 
and even respond more strongly than patients [ 168 , 273 ]. An irregular 
and loud sound environment can reduce the satisfaction of medical staff, 
and even the sound of alarms has a similar effect to overall noise [ 217 ]. 
In certain situations, such as the operating room, even regular music 
designed to create a relaxing environment can be considered by some 
medical staff (anesthesiologists) to be a factor affecting their work 
[ 175 ]. However, music can also be considered helpful for ICU patients 
requiring treatment [ 231 ]. For patients, different sound interventions 
(natural sound, steady-state sound and written sound source) may 
improve people ’ s satisfaction [ 186 ]. People tend to have a lower toler -
ance for these background noises. In certain scenarios or under specific 
needs that drive activities, the sounds actively produced by individuals 
become the dominant composite sound, like playing music with head -
phones [ 70 ], overshadowing other background noises. This can enhance 
occupants ’ satisfaction, although this point remains contentious.
Acoustic elements can influence additional domains as well. For 
instance, Dalirnaghadeh et al. [ 76 ] demonstrated that sound-related 
landmarks could offset the absence of visual cues, potentially aiding in 
the development of navigational systems for visually impaired users. 
Meanwhile, in a randomized trial, Hill et al. [ 134 ] discovered that 
although music and landscape imagery did not significantly alter post -
operative pain scores, they did enhance the overall postoperative 
experience. Besides, thermal stimulation can create a comfortable 
thermal environment, which can improve the patient ’ s assessment of 
acoustics, while negative stimulation has the opposite effect [ 307 ].
4.2.3. Assessment of thermal environment
The thermal environment is generally considered to be the most 
important factor affecting people ’ s comfort [ 161 , 324 ], and it is also the 
factor with the most research content. The PMV model is one of the basic 
models established by many standards [ 35 ]. However, it is often found 
to be inconsistent with the actual human comfort in the actual envi -
ronment [ 22 , 227 , 249 , 285 , 318 ]. Standards including ASHRAE 55 and 
ISO 7730 are present, but they are difficult to respond to the real-time 
needs of different individuals, and it is difficult to bring the entire hos -
pital environment into compliance [ 293 ].
Thermal comfort emerges as a subjective perception influenced by 
various factors, including gender [ 61 , 227 ], age [ 62 ], participant type 
[ 249 ] and room orientation [ 62 ], yet consensus remains elusive.
Gender differences in thermal perception and preference are noted 
[ 287 ], potentially linked to health status [ 156 ]. Women are more sen -
sitive to extremes of heat and cold [ 79 ] and prefer warmer room tem -
peratures [ 307 ], generally expressing greater satisfaction with thermal 
conditions and air quality than men [ 227 , 268 , 335 ]. In contrast, men 
report more comfort with healthcare facility temperatures [ 310 ].
Age influences thermal comfort sensitivity, with older individuals 
being more sensitive than younger ones [ 62 ], showing less acceptance 
toward hot and dry conditions [ 307 ]. Young people ( < 25 years) as well 
as those with a normal BMI demonstrate better environmental adapta -
tion [ 236 , 237 ]. Interestingly, older people rate indoor air quality lower 
than younger counterparts [ 336 ], and weight gain is associated with 
increased thermal sensation in patients [ 237 ].
Evaluations of thermal conditions and preferences among patients 
and visitors have been conducted [ 158 ], emphasizing the need for 
focused thermal comfort considerations for healthcare workers and the 
development of adaptive models [ 184 ]. These workers prefer cooler 
conditions, indicative of an adaptation to less warm environments. 
Moreover, a patient ’ s thermal needs are further modulated by physical 
health [ 144 , 156 ], highlighting the complexity of achieving optimal 
thermal comfort in healthcare settings that accommodate the varied 
needs of all occupants.
In general, six main factors directly affect thermal perception [ 35 ] 
including four environmental factors: air temperature, radiation tem -
perature, air velocity, and humidity [ 62 ] as well as two individual fac -
tors: the thermal insulation of the clothing and metabolic rate [ 233 ,
331 ].
Temperature generally has the highest correlation with thermal 
sensation [ 75 ] and low temperature may be an important risk factor for 
hospitalization [ 247 ]. Existing thermoregulation strategies do not take 
into account the patient ’ s temperature needs often affect the patient ’ s 
perceived level of attention [ 21 ]. Air velocity and humidity not only 
affect the sensation of heat but also cause other sensations, such as the 
sensation of blowing wind or stuffiness. Therefore, even if the combi -
nation of these six parameters reaches a neutral state of thermal 
perception, it may still cause discomfort due to the presence of a feeling 
of wind, etc. Managing the microclimate through behavioral controls of 
evenly distributed air temperature, relative humidity, and airflow is 
essential, as they are the three most important thermal environmental 
aspects in healthcare facilities [ 265 ]. These may also be influenced by 
the season and geographic location on thermal perception [ 236 , 260 ,
318 ]. Rahman et al. [ 2 ] determined that information on the climatic 
characteristics of the building ’ s surroundings and the current state of the 
indoor thermal environment will help the building owner to develop an 
appropriate bioclimatic or passive design approach.
Relative humidity directly or indirectly affects the comfort of

Ventilation, or airflow, is also pivotal for thermal comfort within

4.2.4. Assessment of indoor air quality
Farhadi et al. [ 101 ] concluded several standards and the acceptable 
limit for IAQ parameters were proposed. However, the application in 
studies remains diverse and controversial.
For the study of IAQ, there are two main parts: the first part is the 
control of particulate, pollutants and aerosol, including PM2.5, PM10, 
microbial counts, dust particles and the concentrations of carbon diox -
ide (CO
2
), carbon monoxide (CO), formaldehyde (HCHO) and total 
volatile organic compounds (TVOC) [ 33 , 68 , 188 , 252 ] and so on. Those 
pollutants affect the mental health of occupants [ 125 ]. At the same time, 
they also affect the health of the occupants and lead to the occurrence of 
diseases. For example, sick building syndrome (SBS) [ 12 , 212 , 270 ] is a 
common disease closely related to the contaminants of IAQ, even asthma 
[ 188 ] and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) [ 99 ] may 
be caused. Sanna et al. [ 246 ] mentioned that Rhodotorula spp could 
increase the risk to hospitalized patients, highlighting the importance of 
microbiological monitoring of hospital air and surfaces, especially in 
high-risk wards.
Besides, the COVID-19 pandemic is related to IAQ [ 90 , 147 , 180 ]. 
Hospital work during the COVID-19 pandemic has had important per -
sonal, professional, and psychological impacts on healthcare workers 
[ 136 ]. Respondents identified opportunities to better address informa -
tion, training, and support needs. Personal behaviors, such as smoking 
[ 72 , 334 ], or speaking [ 177 ], may affect air quality by producing pol -
lutants and altering aerosol distribution, respectively. Liu et al. [ 177 ] 
studied the effects of different respiratory behaviors such as normal 
breathing, prolonged speech, phrases, and alternating speech on the 
spatiotemporal distribution of aerosols in negative pressure wards, 
which had implications for both the risk of infection and IAQ in 
healthcare workers.
The other research part is to identify residents ’ satisfaction with IAQ. 
Zhang et al. [ 336 ] investigated perceptions of the air environment in 
hospital waiting areas, emphasizing the critical role of IAQ in shaping 
patient and visitor experiences. U ´scinowicz et al. [ 287 ] focused on pa -
tient perceptions of the indoor environment in Polish hospitals, identi -
fying a connection between demographic characteristics such as gender 
and age, and their assessments of the indoor environment ’ s impact on 
recovery processes. This underscores the personalized nature of envi -
ronmental sensitivity impacts on patient care. Yau and Phuah [ 320 ] 
revealed that many hospital areas exceeded recommended IAQ thresh -
olds, potentially leading to SBS. This discrepancy points to the complex 
relationship between different aspects of IAQ and the subjective nature 
of environmental satisfaction. Kagan et al. [ 155 ] linked healthcare 
staff ’ s job satisfaction with their awareness and experiences related to 
air quality issues, particularly concerning carbapenemase-producing 
Klebsiella pneumoniae in isolation rooms. This association between 
knowledge, environmental conditions, and job satisfaction highlights 
the importance of air quality not only for patient care but also for the 
health and satisfaction of healthcare providers. Besides, there are dif -
ferences in the negative impacts of air pollution on occupants, with 
women and low-income groups feeling it more acutely [ 125 ].
4.2.5. Assessment of multiple perspectives
For the evaluation and research of the whole IEQ in healthcare fa -
cilities, the first controversial topic to be solved is whether the existing 
standards and personnel satisfaction can be harmonized. In the U.S., the 
LEED rating system is the most widely recognized certification system 
for green buildings [ 122 ]. Thiel et al. [ 277 ] and Xuan [ 309 ] showed that 
LEED standards were useful. Kim et al. [ 163 ] proved that staff in 
controlled IEQ scenarios would get higher satisfaction than 
non-LEED-certified hospitals. Xuan [ 310 ] did further research in the 
quantitative comparison of LEED-certified and non-LEED-certified hos -
pitals and found detailed differences in IEQ factors. Liu et al. [ 176 ] 
investigated a one-year post-occupant evaluation and compared a 3A 
and a 2A hospital in different climate zones, they found that the insig -
nificant correlation between the objective environment and subjective 
satisfaction because of their self-perceived health and individual dif -
ferences, and seasons would affect occupants ’ perceptions of different 
factors in IEQ. Liu et al. [ 176 ] suggested that hospital types cannot be 
the same as the occupant satisfaction as the over-crowdedness was the 
major impact factor.
Standards still need to be improved to satisfy the needs of occupants 
and evaluating methods is a point for researchers. For the evaluation of 
IEQ in healthcare facilities, Nimlyat [ 207 ] collected data in three hos -
pitals and built a structural regression model to represent occupants ’ 
satisfaction. It gave a standardized protocol for IEQ assessment and 
supplied the building index rating standards. Teoh et al. [ 275 ] used 
multilevel structural equation models to draw on the job 
demands-resource model to test the associations between hospital-level 
care with hospital-level demands and self-reported doctors ’ job de -
mands, job resources and wellbeing.
IEQ have impacts on different occupants ’ restorative perceptions, 
and individuals show different feedback on it. Huang et al. [ 140 ] did a 
questionnaire on occupants in long-term care facilities and got a 
comprehensive understanding of the characteristics (profession, edu -
cation level, age, illness) of building users in LTC facilities were asso -
ciated with differences in the importance given to indoor environmental 
indicators.
In Madagascar, Nematchoua et al. [ 206 ] carried out a complex sur -
vey of 5 hospitals showing that there were not very great differences 
between males and females in their thermal sensations, humidity sen -
sations, and air movement sensations. Ma et al. [ 183 ] revealed signifi -
cant correlations between IEQ parameters (temperature, TVOC, and 
HCHO levels) and the spatial distribution of patient behavioral changes.

social aspect. These clusters suggested that IEQ preferences were related

5. Critical review of control strategies on healthcare facilities 
considering occupants’ perceptions
To address RQ3, occupants take many actions to control interior 
equipment, and much guidance is proposed to accomplish the proper 
actions. Nowadays, many standards are generally considered best 
practices and are often incorporated into national regulations and 
guidelines [ 101 ]. ISO Standards provide clear standards for environ -
ments with high requirements, such as operating theatres, ASHRAE 
Standards [ 204 ] are technically detailed, particularly suitable for tem -
perature and air quality control, LEED Certification [ 310 ] is designed for 
eco-friendly and energy-efficient healthcare buildings, like using natural 
light and sustainable materials, and lastly, JCI Standards mainly focus 
on processes and management, may lack specificity in environmental 
details.
These standards each have their unique features and considerations. 
But in actual applications, considering their specific environmental 
contexts, patient care needs, and local regulations, healthcare facilities 
often need to integrate various standards. However, there is a large gap 
in the performance capacity of medical institutions [ 181 ] and the 
standards set by the hospital are not consistent with the actual satis -
faction of the occupants [ 176 ], and adaptation strategies seem to be 
influenced in a psychological way [ 302 ]. Besides, people’s life behaviors 
have different degrees of influence on the elements of indoor physical 
environments [ 338 ].
Based on analysis above, this section will be divided into two parts: 
the collection and description of existing control behaviors ( Section 5.1 ), 
and the analysis and summary of the latest automated control and 
related methods ( Section 5.2 ).
5.1. Data collection and control behavior
Based on the results of the previous study and referring to the pre -
vious study [ 312 ], types of data that control behaviors and influencing 
factors can be divided into two categories: external factors and internal 
factors. External factors are environmental factors, physical information 
factors. This data is mainly based on environmental parameters, mainly 
objective data and statistics collected by sensors. The internal factors are 
mainly based on human information data. This includes physiological 
and psychological information, as well as data from surveys of an in -
dividual’s subjective willingness to control. In this paper, the occupant 
control behavior and the design and adjustment of the indoor environ -
ment are collected and analyzed.
5.1.1. Interior design control
There are a variety of factors that need to be considered to control the 
design of a hospital, and it needs to consider the requirements of effi -
ciency, capacity, zoning, etc. According to the classification in Section 
4.1 , it could be divided into three perspectives, including spatiality: 
choosing the right space size [ 113 ], increasing the protection of privacy 
and safety [ 64 , 97 ], balancing the efficiency and comfort of the 
streamlined design [ 276 ]; green: the arrangement of green plants in the 
interior, the choice of the natural environment outside the window and 
increasing the window-to-wall ratio [ 300 ]; and decoration: the selection 
of floor, artwork [ 205 ], decorations [ 114 ], and wall materials and colors 
[ 264 ] and so on. Those factors are related to designers, as the confir -
mation of materials, layout, and circulation need to be based on the 
actual conditions.
In the past, based on the selected factors from occupants, designers 
often used the Evidence-Based Design (EBD) method to create physical 
environments that positively impact patients, staff, and visitors [ 59 ]. 
Recently, space syntax [ 117 , 276 ] and even AI methods [ 172 ] were 
proposed to model layout and circulation designs as well as critical 
qualities in healthcare settings. These methods help designers do inte -
rior design.
Many designers welcome EBD and focus on creating positive physical 
environments and building standards like the LEED [ 277 ]. It is trace -
able. Hospitals are among the most expensive facilities to build and their 
design should be guided by evidence-based research [ 152 ]. This study 
provides evidence that family rooms off wards and mixed-sex communal 
areas can potentially increase treatment satisfaction. Selecting key fac -
tors is important. Bingham et al. [ 50 ] documents the implementation of 
EBD elements relating to patient privacy and comfort in hospital reno -
vation projects. Qi et al. [ 228 ] identified four first-level indicators in 
four dimensions: functional layout flow organization, supporting facil -
ities and environmental details in the waiting space environment of 
pediatric clinics. Based on EBD methods, many researchers developed 
tools to assess buildings. Huisman et al. [ 141 ] selected the impact of 
physical environmental factors on users in EBD and designed a tool to 
evaluate building performance. Similarly, Joseph et al. [ 151 ] built a 
POE toolkit to help workers to evaluate building performance. Alotaibi 
et al. [ 20 ] proposed a new design tool considering inpatients’ various 
medical and complex conditions to fill the gap. For special patients, 
Kirch and Marquardt [ 164 ] developed a validated assessment tool on 
design for dementia available to improve the functional ability of pa -
tients at discharge from the hospital.
Space Syntax is a traditional design method, usually used to optimize 
the streamlined design to connect different components in hospitals 
efficiently [ 58 ]. Two Space Syntax-based metrics [ 276 ] are introduced 
to model proximity and ease of access in layout designs to control 
infection. Brambilla et al. [ 56 ] provided meta-design indications for the

impaired people walking along prescribed routes, including electrical 
skin activity, heart rate variability, and electroencephalograms, and 
then compared the physiological monitoring signal data of each route to 
help designers design and transform the space. The most advanced 
technology currently is the brain-computer interface (BCI), Yang et al. 
[ 314 ] have developed a BCI tool to help designers manipulate the po -
sition and size of windows in a virtual space, helping to refine the design 
process. Similarly, Jung et al. [ 154 ] facilitated the widespread adoption 
of biophilic design by measuring emotional changes associated with 
biophilic design in an experimental setting through a combination of 
electroencephalography (EEG) and VR methods.
5.1.2. Visual environment-related control
Lighting is considered one of the key architectural factors affecting 
individuals [ 106 ]. Early designs for lighting control schemes have been 
proposed [ 26 ]. But literature surveys indicate a divergence in percep -
tions and needs regarding lighting control between healthcare staff and 
patients, with both groups showing a high preference for natural light 
[ 73 ]. Nurses desire increased brightness to aid their work in wards 
[ 196 ], whereas patients often seek greater autonomy, possibly prefer -
ring a dimmer environment, leading to potential conflicts [ 64 ] However, 
enhancing their control over lighting can improve satisfaction with the 
light environment.
Lighting control is categorized into natural light control and artificial 
lighting control. Control of natural light primarily involves modifying 
the window-to-wall ratio [ 23 , 279 ] adjusting blinds [ 93 ], and utilizing 
shades to regulate natural light exposure [ 297 ], which helps avoid glare 
[ 24 ]. Besides, roofs also impact on humans. As to roof design, applying 
green roof could improve physical health and emotional well-being 
[ 215 ]. Selecting suitable furniture surface materials can also improve 
daylighting conditions [ 104 ]. Besides, roof materials are important, the 
transparent ethylene-tetra-fluoro-ethylene (ETFE) membrane roof may 
cause visual discomfort [ 273 ]. Artificial lighting control is more com -
plex, encompassing relocating light sources to improve the hospital’s 
lighting environment [ 174 ], altering spectral composition to allow 
variable color temperatures in different areas, implementing automatic 
lighting control technologies to reduce blue light and increase red light 
[ 107 ] for alertness and circadian rhythm-aligned lighting schemes 
[ 250 ]. However, success requires close collaboration with occupants 
and continual adjustments.
5.1.3. Acoustic environment-related control
For the control of noise, providing patients with a sound environ -
ment that is conducive to recovery and satisfaction, and reducing the 
stress of healthcare workers, is the core of current work [ 187 ]. The 
World Health Organization (WHO) recommend that hospital noise levels 
should not exceed 35 A-weighted decibels (dBA) during the day and 30 
dBA at night [ 47 ]. They are still difficult to achieve for many hospitals, 
even indoors and outdoors [ 226 ]. Many studies do not provide recom -
mendations for the control of sounds, and there is a lack of multiple 
scenario validations [ 173 ]
Sound control can be implemented from three perspectives: the 
sound source, the propagation route, and the human ear. Source control 
includes control of human voices and device sounds [ 142 ]. For the 
control of human voices, it is necessary to carry out noise reduction 
education and training for employees, set up conversation areas, or 
install noise-sensitive lights to remind employees of activities and set 
quiet times [ 132 ]. On the other hand, for patients or visitors, it is 
necessary to set up reminder signs and limit the patient’s visit during 
quiet moments. However, for the ICU environment, behavioral modifi -
cation alone is not adequate to control excessive noise [ 166 ]. For en -
vironments with special purposes, patients can make a lot of noise and 
need to set up sound locks to meet their needs [ 223 ]. For equipment 
sounds, it is possible to change the wheels that make noise from the 
trolley, change the position of the equipment, and keep maintenance of 
the equipment or doors that make noise [ 142 ].
The control of sound propagation pathways is mainly carried out by 
setting up materials, such as the use of vegetation [ 294 ], installing 
high-performance sound-absorbing materials for ceilings and walls, and 
noise-reducing bed curtains. Another approach is to close windows and 
doors. The control of the human ear, such as the provision of earplugs or 
noise-cancelling headphones [ 220 ], or the playing of soft music to mask 
noisy environments and calm the patient. In addition to this, there may 
be a combination of other factors, such as visible vegetation could 
improve human perception of environmental noise [ 294 ], and the 
comfort thermal environment may also improve the evaluation of the 
acoustic environment [ 294 ].
5.1.4. Thermal environment-related control
Enhancing occupant satisfaction with the thermal environment is 
challenging due to the complexity of thermal comfort. This comfort is 
influenced by several factors including the building’s size, the facilities 
and equipment available, and the number of people present. Addition -
ally, individual perceptions of heat play a significant role, varying with 
each person’s body type, age, clothing, activity level, and even gender 
[ 158 ]. Considering the differences between individuals and the envi -
ronment, Zaniboni et al. [ 326 ] proposed to develop a database to meet 
the thermal needs of residents of different identities through a combi -
nation of subjective and objective research. Similarly, Alotaibi et al. [ 20 ] 
proposed a comprehensive framework, and Dovjak et al. [ 83 ] also sug -
gested related control methods, but there are still many restrictions on 
how these can be applied. Because climatic conditions vary from country 
to country, the necessary temperature range and relative humidity levels 
differ. To address this, centralized air conditioning systems are shifting 
toward personalized comfort solutions tailored to individual needs [ 7 ,
200 ]. This is also in line with the patient’s need for relevant control 
autonomy [ 64 ].
The control scheme can be divided into two schemes: active and 
passive heating [ 282 ]. Active heating is the setting of products that can 
be heated, such as personal items, heated blankets, and warm or heated 
clothing, which are often used in the Operation Room [ 157 ]. Passive 
heating solutions are closely linked to ventilation installations, and the 
effects of natural and mechanical ventilation need to be taken into ac -
count during the set-up process [ 120 ]. The advantage of natural venti -
lation is the ability to achieve a high ventilation rate. This emphasizes 
the impact of the sensation of the outside, which gives them the 
impression that they have a higher degree of control over their envi -
ronment [ 325 ]. Buildings’ thermal environment and energy can be 
significantly affected by their natural ventilation strategies [ 7 ]. Besides, 
Using the radiant ceiling panel (RCP) systems [ 292 ] to heat air is also 
useful. The main advantage of mechanical ventilation is the ability to 
precisely set indoor conditions. This is the most common method for 
maintaining a hospital indoor thermal environment, but due to indi -
vidual differences, it is difficult for control strategies to meet the needs 
of all individuals [ 293 ]. The use of a personalized ventilation system 
may improve their thermal comfort [ 200 ] but substantial research on 
this new solutions is needed. At the same time, it is also necessary to 
meet the control strategy for IAQ. Combining natural and mechanical 
ventilation also could help improve thermal comfort in the wards [ 105 ,
201 ].

5.1.5. IAQ-related control
The ventilation system not only has an impact on thermal comfort 
but also plays a major role in controlling IAQ [ 238 ], which is the focus of 
current research. Zhang et al. [ 332 ] proposed that the traditional 
methods of improving the aerosol removal capacity of ventilation sys -
tems could be divided into three main categories: increasing air changes 
per hour (ACH) [ 108 ], changing the location of inlets and outlets [ 248 ], 
and adding inlets to existing overall ventilation [ 271 ]. Therefore, the 
control of the ventilation system is divided into three parts in this paper: 
device setting, policy control, and position control. Besides, applying air 
quality monitoring systems could enhance indoor comfort for patients 
[ 77 ].
When it comes to device setting, it mainly refers to replacing 
equipment or optimizing equipment design. Increasing upgrading filters 
is a common method. At present, it mainly refers to the use of high- 
efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration systems [ 329 ]. Improving 
the ventilation system can also have the same effect. Zhai and Osborne 
[ 329 ] showed that maximizing the area of the laminar flow diffuser 
allows optimal air sterility to be fully achieved and provides very low 
levels of contamination. Modifying the design of air-conditioning and 
mechanical ventilation (ACMV) systems has been proven to be effective 
[ 320 ]. IAQ audits in hospitals have shown that occupants tend to feel 
more discomfort under centralized ACMV systems compared to 
non-centralized systems. Nonetheless, hospitals with centralized ACMV 
systems demonstrate superior control over chemical and particulate 
contaminants in their IAQ [ 320 ]. Wang et al. [ 300 ] used CFD to simulate 
the load distribution of air conditioning design in large spaces and 
suggested a layered air conditioning approach. In negative pressure 
wards, ventilation system designs include ceiling-supplied floor return 
(CSFR) and ceiling-supplied ceiling return (CSCR) as well as additional 
curtain ventilation (ACV) systems [ 332 ]. Zhang et al. [ 332 ] proposed to 
reduce ACH and improve energy efficiency by evaluating the accuracy of 
ventilation systems and using ACV as a single method.
Policy control strategies can be divided into active and passive 
strategies [ 1 ]. Natural ventilation strategies often use CFD to model. 
Adamu et al. [ 7 ] evaluated four buoyancy-driven natural ventilation 
strategies (single window, double openings on the same side, low intake 
and exhaust chimneys, and ceiling-based natural ventilation) for single 
rooms using a regional dynamic thermal model (DTM) and a CFD model. 
Verma and Sinha [ 296 ] used CFD analysis to simulate and compare the 
removal of contaminating particles (viruses, fungi, bacteria, etc.) using 
several different ventilation systems. Adamu and Price [ 6 ] set a new 
system called natural personal ventilation (NPV) is feasible to decouple 
airflow delivery from windows. Berlanga et al. [ 48 ] evaluated ventila -
tion performance and contaminants released by health workers exposed 
to closed patients, evaluating the ease of using four different mixed 
ventilation configurations in individual wards. The local exhaust strat -
egy could give the best performance for proper airborne infection con -
trol [ 95 ]. As a supplement to the traditional evaluation indicators 
introduced before, Lu et al. [ 180 ] introduced two indicators to help 
researchers improve the evaluation of risk control strategies for airborne 
infections.
Position control, which compensates for the lack of policy control 
strategies. For example, the conventional approach is to increase the 
fresh air supply rate (or ACH), but this does not always ensure a 
reduction in the build-up of stale air in all areas of the ward. In fact, 
under-planned designs may be a tendency to do the opposite [ 242 ]. CFD 
simulations [ 4 , 110–112 , 239 , 240 ] and experiments are normal methods 
to design hospital contamination. Many studies have been tested 
through them to design and model HVAC systems [ 313 , 315 ], helping 
designers to design accurately and avoid the spread of infections such as 
the COVID-19 virus [ 147 ]. Erdogan and Yilmazoglu [ 95 ] simulated the 
exclusion effect of air aerosols from three different ventilation layouts in 
the ICU and judged that local exhaust can effectively improve the 
exclusion rate. Aganovic et al. [ 9 ] tested a new ventilation in a 
single-bed isolated room to verify that a concept called protected 
occupied zone ventilation (POV) could improve the quality of inhaled 
air and satisfy occupants’ comfort needs. Ho et al. [ 135 ] did a 
three-dimensional analysis of thermal comfort and contaminant 
removal in hospital operating rooms and found that better overall per -
formance could be achieved if the supply grille was placed closer to the 
vertical centerline of the wall, while the location of the exhaust grille 
was insignificant. Stale air tends to accumulate near the central roof area 
[ 332 ], and placing the outlet grille near the roof along the roof center -
line is an effective measure to expel the stale air that accumulates. 
Exhaust locations should be positioned as close as possible to the source 
[ 95 ]. It is also a good practice to place the patient’s head towards the 
posterior wall with an exit grille because due to the presence of the exit 
grille on the posterior wall, stale air tends to accumulate in the patient’s 
leg area, but not directly above the patient’s head [ 332 ].
Many other aspects other than the ventilation system can also affect 
IAQ. Decorative materials can also affect IAQ because some pollutants 
may remain in fixed locations [ 177 ]. Besides, clothing materials and 
types, such as disposable spun-bond polyester gowns and reusable 
woven polyester gowns, may impact IAQ [ 238 ]. Specific shapes in the 
environment can also affect airflow, for example, in the operating 
theatre, surgical lights, surgical equipment and the surgeon’s posture 
can seriously interfere with ventilation airflow [ 238 ]. Frequent opening 
of doors can also have a disturbing effect on airflow, so in operating 
theatres equipped with hybrids, preoperative planning and communi -
cation should be improved to minimize unnecessary opening of doors 
[ 238 ]. Rooms or spaces with specific requirements should be controlled 
with differential pressure control and directional airflow [ 332 ]. Nursing 
staff should open windows and ventilate rooms depending on actual 
conditions, and clean and sterilize rooms on time to avoid pollutants 
[ 254 ].
5.2. Automatic control methods considering occupant perception of 
restorative
Beyond the initial, hard-to-alter setup of a space (like equipment, 
natural scenery, and decorations), most behaviors are under individual 
control [ 339 ]. With technological advancements, building automation 
systems have found applications across various scenarios, yet their 
integration into hospitals is still at a preliminary stage. A critical gap 
identified is the lack of methods that combine individual perception 
models with control systems, which benefits users [ 82 ]. For instance, a 
user-centered system developed for burn wards [ 85 ] was tested in a lab 
setting. Effective indoor environment control requires a comprehensive 
strategy that recognizes different occupant needs [ 339 ]. This paper 
identifies three main control methods based on literature: adjustments 
of automatic window openers or blinds [ 255 ], HVAC adjustments, and 
lighting automation [ 192 , 250 ], covering the majority of key personal 
actions in managing the indoor environment.
For a variety of control behaviors, it is important to establish a 
suitable model for the control strategy. Current models, such as PMV, 
are often thought to be influenced by individual differences [ 79 ]. The 
development of the Personal Comfort System (PCS) has made it possible 
to provide more refined control schemes to address individual differ -
ences, which have been studied mainly by occupant behavior [ 156 ] and 
occupancy. Due to the uncertainty and unpredictability of occupant 
behavior, as well as the complexity of the interaction between occupant

behavior and the building ’ s physical system, there are many ways to 
classify suitable models [ 311 ] but there is no consensus on them. Ac -
cording to the topic of personalized control, the classification is now 
mainly focused on human modeling and human-environment modeling. 
In this paper, we divide the modeling methods into three categories 
according to the data source and processing method: white-box models, 
black-box models, and grey-box models, and summarize their applica -
tions in the context of healthcare facilities.
5.2.1. The application of white-box models
White-box models, characterized by their transparency, play a 
crucial role in understanding the intricate dynamics between individuals 
and their environments and are widely used in actual projects. For 
human perceptions, studies like those by Zaniboni et al. [ 327 ] have 
explored the impacts of metabolic rates and subjective parameters on 
thermal comfort, employing models like the Fanger [ 100 ] and adaptive 
models [ 249 ] alongside statistical analyses to assess neutral temperature 
preferences and the interaction between thermal comfort and other 
domains, highlighting the challenges and adaptability in estimating 
thermal satisfaction across different populations. U ´scinowicz and Bog -
dan [ 285 ] also proposed ways to modify the thermal perception model. 
In recent years, the consideration of physiological signals [ 123 ], 
including EEG signals, has also become a new trend in modeling, which 
can be combined with predictive models to propose more specific con -
trol schemes for relevant building systems.
On the environmental front, CFD simulations epitomize the appli -
cation of white box models in predicting airflow within architectural 
spaces, thereby facilitating the design of buildings optimized for natural 
ventilation and energy efficiency. This approach relies on solving 
Navier-Stokes equations to simulate air movement and distribution, 
crucial for effective ventilation strategies. It could be used to test a va -
riety of hybrid ventilation strategies [ 201 ]. Research by Kong et al. 
[ 165 ] and works by Dovjak et al. [ 84 ] have extended these models to 
virus control strategies and energy-efficient thermal comfort solutions, 
respectively. Furthermore, in the lighting control scenario, the integra -
tion of lighting control based on circadian rhythms into white box 
modeling, as discussed by Barroso and Den Brinker [ 43 ], underscores 
the significance of aligning environmental controls with human bio -
logical rhythms for optimal comfort and energy use.
What is more, from a whole perspective, Maleetipwan-Mattsson et al. 
[ 192 ] integrated theories like the Theory of Affordances, Theory of 
Planned Behavior (TPB) and Planned Behavior Control (PBC) to link 
lighting design with user interaction, aiming for optimal usage that 
supports occupant comfort and energy conservation. These instances 
illustrate the breadth of white box modeling in bridging the gap between 
theoretical understanding and practical applications for enhancing 
human-environment interactions, highlighting the potential for more 
targeted and efficient interventions in building design and environ -
mental control.
5.2.2. The application of black-box models
In contrast, black-box models are essential tools in many fields, 
particularly in scenarios where the underlying mechanisms of a system 
are complex, unknown, or deliberately hidden. A key aspect of black box 
modeling is its reliance on empirical data to establish mathematical 
relationships between inputs and outputs. For instance, Fattahi and 
Sharbatdar [ 103 ] highlight the importance of system performance 
measurement data in constructing these relationships, often utilizing 
real-time data components like seasonal variations and setpoints for 
training models. It utilized data from the Green Buildings Innovation 
Cluster (GBIC) project. By considering a variety of factors, including 
physiological, environmental, and personal, the study designed several 
feature sets to develop predictive thermal models of individual comfort 
responses.
Moreover, black-box models have been employed to assess the ef -
fects of physical characteristics and configurations of building 
components, like windows and shading devices, on energy usage, 
lighting, and visual comfort. In the healthcare domain, preliminary ev -
idence suggests benefits from daylight in hospital bedrooms, yet the 
effects of using automated dynamic lighting systems remain inconclu -
sive [ 60 ]. Eisazadeh et al. [ 93 ] found that the right combination of 
windows and blinds can significantly reduce cooling loads while main -
taining adequate lighting levels, emphasizing the importance of select -
ing appropriate configurations for overall performance. Research by Jia 
et al. [ 149 ] and Shi et al. [ 255 ] utilizes logistic regression to model 
human behaviors, such as window opening and closing in hospitals, 
based on seasonal variations, demonstrating the adaptability of these 
models with accuracies exceeding 70 %.
In summary, black-box models offer a pragmatic approach to dealing 
with complex systems by focusing on observable inputs and outputs. 
Their utility across various applications — from building environmental 
control to healthcare — demonstrates their versatility and effectiveness 
in scenarios where a detailed understanding of internal processes is 
either unavailable or unnecessary.
5.2.3. The application of grey-box models
Grey-box models meld the detailed insight of white-box models with 
the efficiency of black-box models, making them ideal for systems where 
complete information is not available. These models use a combination 
of empirical data and theoretical principles to predict system behavior, 
which is particularly beneficial in complex environments like buildings 
or for applications such as Model Predictive Control (MPC) [ 37 ]. 
Grey-box models can provide a more feasible and reliable basis for MPC.
They also can adeptly manage the intricacies of thermal dynamics in 
buildings, leading to more optimized and energy-efficient control stra -
tegies. The research conducted by Nimlyat [ 207 ] illustrates the appli -
cation of grey-box modeling through structural equation modeling 
(SEM) to establish relationships between IEQ parameters, patient 
satisfaction, and health outcomes. Lan et al. [ 169 ] combined thermo -
dynamics, CFD, and thermal comfort models for effective analysis and 
comparison of thermal comfort impacts stemming from various 
low-energy building retrofit concepts. Khan et al. [ 159 ] explored a 
grey-box modeling approach to enhance infection control in hospital 
wards by combining response surface methodology (RSM) and genetic 
algorithm (GA). This work aimed at optimizing ward design to reduce 
airborne transmission risk while maintaining patient comfort and 
minimizing environmental impact.
These examples highlight the strengths of grey-box models in various 
applications, from building energy management to healthcare environ -
ment optimization. By judiciously combining theoretical knowledge 
with empirical data, grey-box models enable more nuanced and effective 
solutions to complex problems where neither purely theoretical nor 
empirical approaches alone would suffice.
6. Discussion
6.1. Summary of main findings
There are many literature reviews on the indoor environment design 
of healthcare facilities and some factors of IEQ, but there is no relevant 
review on the impact of the overall indoor environment of healthcare 
facilities on human restorative perception. This paper summarizes the 
factors of interior design and environmental quality that affect the res -
idents ’ sense of restorative perception in healthcare facilities and eval -
uates the related control behaviors and the advanced automatic control 
methods.
This paper first explores and defines the perception of recoverability 
in healthcare facilities, determines the scope of the search, and finally 
selects 249 related articles for literature review. This paper then ana -
lyzes these articles quantitatively and identifies the current research 
status and basic information in this field, including the changes in the 
research situation in various countries, the research methods and sample

size, and the objects and proportions of attention. The content of the 
studies, including personal information, research subjects, research 
scenarios and research topics was analyzed, and the relevance was 
determined. The analysis reveals that hybrid methodologies are the most 
prevalent (42.01 %), followed by objective (34.25 %) and subjective 
(23.74 %) approaches, indicating a trend toward integrating diverse

Among the factors studied, design-related factors are the most

Wards, the most studied hospital settings, encompass specific 
research areas involving various departments, followed by workplaces, 
outpatient areas, ICU, operating rooms, waiting areas and other sce -

hospital residents: patients, staff, and visitors, among which the research 
on staff accounts for a higher proportion, followed by patients, and there 
are many surveys of various types of groups, but many studies do not 
indicate specific research objects. At the same time, personal physio -

more, and social attributes are less considered. Personal information can 
have an impact on the conclusions of relevant research, which may be 
relevant to specific research methods and research scenarios.
Regarding the review of the overall environment of the hospital, this 
paper examines two aspects: interior design and IEQ. For interior design, 
there are two perspectives: occupants and designers. For occupants, the 
design of the indoor environment will have an impact on their percep -
tion, comfort, health, privacy, and more. There are many ways in which 
research has been categorized in this area, and many papers have 
focused on the setting of physical space in general. This article elabo -
rates on spatiality, decoration, and natural objects, and recommends 
meeting the spatial requirements of occupants for safety and privacy as 
much as possible, adding appropriate decorations and helping them to 
get in touch with nature as much as possible. As for the designers, they 
care about materials, layout, and circulation, which could relate to oc -
cupants’ needs. Balancing efficiency, safety and comfort are crucial in 
designing. The design approach they adopt is mainly focused on 
evidence-based design research, and the development of new technol -
ogies, such as spatial syntax and AI methods, also helps them. The use of 
new technologies to improve feedback between designers and occupants 
can be more effective in helping them, but this needs to be further 
studied.
For the research related to IEQ, this paper summarizes the visual 
environment, acoustic environment, IAQ, thermal environment, and 
overall evaluation scheme. Diverse factors on occupants and environ -
ment may impact the restorative assessments. The demands for staff, 
patients and visitors are different, causing some conflict, but the needs 
for a clear and healthy environment are consistent. At present, the 
research on IEQ in healthcare facilities is still mainly based on the 
evaluation of a single factor, and the overall evaluation mainly focuses 
on the establishment of evaluation standards and systems for the med -
ical environment, but there is still a lack of clear analysis of the com -
posite impact of multiple factors. As to the related control behavior, 
circadian rhythm lighting and more natural lighting, personalized sound 
control but without disturbing others, increasing natural ventilation and 
pollution control, as well as proper heating methods control for occu -
pants, could improve occupants’ restorative perceptions. Increasing 
control over residents, setting suitable materials [ 308 ], and regulating 
simple control behaviors could effectively improve evaluations. 
Applying appropriate and automatic control methods may be helpful for 
the overall control system in the immediacy, personalize, and compre -
hensiveness. But the application is still limited, and developing suitable 
control models (black, white, and grey models) in specific scenarios is a 
challenge, especially for individuals. Considering all factors to build and 
maintain a suitable indoor environment can help residents recover 
faster, relieve stress and anxiety, increase productivity, improve sleep, 
improve comfort, and promote healing, which can reduce hospital stay 
for patients and increase efficiency and strengthen links for workers.

Based on the aforementioned analyses, future suggestions are pro -

- Applying advanced technologies to increase information form 
occupants’ feedback collection. 
Getting precise feedback from occupants is a fundamental step; 
however, comprehensive and real-time collection methods of per -
sonal data are still lacking in application. There are many studies on 
satisfaction with IEQ, and most of them still rely on questionnaires 
and monitoring data on the environment. Whether from the HCAHPS 
(Healthcare Provider and Systems Assessment) survey [ 171 ] or the 
researchers’ questionnaires, the results are subjective, and there is a 
lack of uniform criteria. With the advancement of technology and the 
application of various new wearable devices, it is possible to test 
people’s EEG, ECG (electrocardiogram) [ 343 ] and GSR (electrical 
skin response) [ 113 ] anytime, anywhere. With these tools, more 
accurate personal information can be obtained, and future satisfac -
tion studies will have more consistent measurements and more sci -
entific indicators and results.
- Utilizing human-environment interaction methods and 
advanced design techniques to achieve fast and accurate inte -
rior design. 
The initial layout and environmental design of healthcare build -
ings are influenced by personal perception, making traditional layout 
designs inadequate for swiftly meeting individual needs. The 
knowledge gap between designers and occupants presents a chal -
lenge and the connection between the environment and personal 
perception warrants further investigation. This indicates a need for 
rapid design changes and advanced methods of integration and 
experience to meet practical demands [ 150 ].
Simulation for architectural design is a common strategy, with re -
searchers employing artificial intelligence to generate layouts, such 
as improved Genetic Algorithms (GA) or Particle Swarm Optimiza -
tion (PSO), aiming to fulfil objectives centered around staff efficiency 
and patient-centric goals [ 172 ]. However, achieving high precision 
in simulations is challenging due to the nonlinear nature of human 
behavior. Therefore, creating interactions with users in 
yet-to-be-realized environments might offer a more effective solu -
tion. Utilizing technologies such as (VR and 3D printing to facilitate 
layperson participation in design can lead to more precise and tar -
geted outcomes. It also aids in understanding the impact of the 
architectural environment on individual perception [ 303 ].

significantly, influenced by specific environments and populations,

Meanwhile, most studies lack a categorized investigation into

optimization to better align with individual needs [ 267 ] and enhance 
system efficiency [ 51 ]. Current methods, including automated win -
dows, shading devices, and HVAC systems, demand modular yet 
comprehensive strategies. However, the adoption of such automated 
systems remains less common in healthcare compared to traditional 
methods due to challenges in integrating multiple control capabil -
ities to satisfy diverse objectives. These should be addressed using 
different models of control methods, especially the grey-box model 
that combines individual behavior [ 96 ] and automated control 
without sufficient data, but this still needs to be discussed.
To bridge this gap, future improvements should focus on advanced, 
occupant-centric controls [ 198 , 199 , 202 ]. This includes developing 
sophisticated human detection technologies, multisource data 
modeling, and collaborative control behaviors within a central 
control system. Specifically, implementing a Building Management 
System (BMS) [ 148 ] equipped with AI [ 131 ] capabilities may revo -
lutionize patient care environments. This system may utilize 
real-time data on room occupancy and individual preferences to 
adjust environmental conditions automatically, thus ensuring both 
energy efficiency and enhanced patient comfort. Such a personalized 
approach, supported by AI ’ s ability to learn from historical data, 
allows for dynamic adjustments across different facility zones, 
ensuring optimal environmental conditions tailored to individual 
needs and activities.

the collection, integration, and processing of vast amounts of data

change, and for the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers should chase the 
changing needs of occupants for multiple environmental requirements.
6.3. Advanced assessment system and control framework
Based on the review results and analysis before, similar to those 
assessment systems [ 19 , 222 , 254 ] but different, a dynamic evaluation 
system and comprehensive control framework is designed for the 
personalized indoor environment in healthcare facilities, as shown in 
Fig. 7 . This system first considers the underlying individual information 
and environmental settings and makes connections about possible con -
trol behaviors. The system can help the occupants evaluate the indoor 
environment and make all aspects of the indoor environment have a 
positive impact on the occupants and improve the resilience of the oc -
cupants. The system applies to different countries or regions and 
different populations, but it needs to be evaluated more accurately based 
on actual and individual data.
The proposed restorative evaluation framework explicitly accounts 
for individual differences in restorative perception by integrating 
personalized data collection and adaptive control systems. For instance, 
occupant-specific data, such as age, health status, and occupational 
roles, are collected via surveys and wearable sensors to tailor environ -
mental conditions (e.g., lighting, temperature) to individual needs. 
Adaptive control systems, leveraging AI and MPC, enable real-time ad -
justments, ensuring environments are responsive to diverse occupant 
profiles, such as patients with chronic conditions or staff with high 
cognitive demands [ 92 ]. This approach enhances the framework ’ s 
applicability across varied healthcare settings.
It is worth noting that this paper analyzes and summarizes all the 
searched literature to illustrate the impact of indoor environment- 
related factors on individuals and related interventions, which can 
effectively help the impact of the healthcare environment on occupants. 
However, when used in practice, accuracy needs to be considered, and 
the evaluation and adjustment of specific parameters for different in -
dividuals in the actual environment should be based on prior assessment 
of the individual. At the same time, we recommend that we should 
maintain flexibility for specific measures, as a single measure often 
proves to be effective, while the combined application effect of multiple 
measures needs to be verified and evaluated after the fact to determine 
whether to proceed. Moreover, the measures are not limited to the 
systematic approach, which can be continuously improved, which are 
mentioned before. In addition, increasing occupant control can also 
significantly increase their satisfaction [ 82 ].
7. Conclusion
Improving the overall indoor environmental factors is crucial for 
occupants (patients, staff, visitors, etc.) in healthcare facilities, as it af -
fects their restorative perceptions. This impacts their evaluation of the 
living environment, determining their willingness to stay and thereby 
influencing hospital revenue and costs. Compared to former similar 
research [ 5 , 209 , 222 , 305 , 340 ], this paper presents the first compre -
hensive review and evaluation of occupant restorative perceived quality 
on the overall indoor environmental factors (IEQ and physical design 
factors) of healthcare facilities, alongside occupant-centric control 
methods. It identifies and analyzes the current state for research and 
growing interest in healthcare facility indoor environments, proposing 
recommendations for an occupant-centric evaluation and related po -
tential manual-automatic comprehensive control schemes. This study

moves the body of scientific knowledge forward by introducing a novel 
restorative perception framework that integrates physical design, IEQ, 
and adaptive control solutions, addressing critical gaps in healthcare 
facility assessment models and providing a foundation for 
occupant-centric research and practice [ 210 , 254 ]. Specifically, the 
detailed points are summarized as follows, which reflects the in -
consistencies in former research implementations: 
• Research in healthcare settings reveals inconsistencies in sample 
sizes and methodologies, leading to poor transferability of findings 
across different studies. To address this, future research must focus 
on more detailed categorization, such as differentiating between 
hospital wards, public spaces, and patient treatment areas, and 
employing advanced monitoring techniques like real-time sensors 
and physiological data collection. These approaches could help 
standardize environmental assessment systems. Currently, studies 
tend to focus on hospital wards, neglecting multi-occupant public 
spaces. Given the importance of shared environments, future 
research should prioritize settings that accommodate multiple users. 
Furthermore, disease types, treatment protocols, and professional 
roles influence outcomes, adding complexity to comparisons across 
studies between studies. The predominance of cross-sectional de -
signs limits the ability to infer causal relationships or assess long- 
term impacts, highlighting the need for longitudinal studies.
• Assessing environmental factors within healthcare facilities reveals 
that individual differences and actual needs play a crucial role. This 
paper categorizes previous studies into three primary areas: 
improving greenery, optimizing space while maintaining privacy 
and accessibility, and enhancing decor. These elements can promote 
recovery, but differences in task demands between healthcare staff 
and patients require further investigation. For instance, healthcare 
staff prioritize convenience, while patients prioritize privacy. Addi -
tionally, even for the same individual, needs may vary over time. 
Therefore, hospital interior environments should be optimized based 
on the specific tasks conducted in each area. Implementing pre- 
assessment protocols, automating design generation, and utilizing 
virtual reality for scenario planning can improve design efficiency 
and reduce costs.
• The evaluation of indoor environments typically falls into two cat -
egories: single-modality studies, and cross-disciplinary research. 
Current studies often rely on surveys to explore the relationship 
between group demographics (age, gender, BMI and so on) and 
environmental factors, providing qualitative insights but lacking 
quantitative assessments. Personal evaluation models like PMV are 
frequently debated for their validity. Single-modality studies, pri -
marily based on simulations, yield effective conclusions but have 
limited real-world application. Overall, reducing noise, enhancing 
natural light, maintaining circadian rhythms, reducing blue light at 
night, increasing natural ventilation, adjusting temperature and 
humidity according to demand, and adopting personalized ventila -
tion can effectively improve occupant satisfaction. The next step is to 
identify the acute and personal needs and enhance the rapid 
adjustment. Cross-disciplinary research, though more relevant to 
real-world settings, the way the factors are combined are different, 
and the conclusions need to be subject to quantitative studies. 
Moreover, even for the same individual, environmental needs may 
change over time. Existing standards, such as LEED, do not suffi -
ciently account for the complexity of individual experiences. Based 
on these findings, optimization of different IEQ areas within hospital 
interiors should be task-specific. Hence, more advanced measure -
ment techniques and adaptive systems are required to capture the 
dynamic and diverse impacts of environmental factors across 
different contexts.
• The exploration of control behaviors and methods in healthcare 
settings indicates that occupants should receive training on the 
importance of IEQ and its impact on health outcomes. Such 
awareness can empower them to implement simple measures effec -
tively enhance their comfort. Additionally, there is a need for more 
sophisticated automated control methods in hospitals. Advanced 
control methods should be adjusted to the personal assessment 
framework. Current methods predominantly focus on zone-level 
controls (e.g., thermostats, light switches) rather than system con -
trols (e.g., Air Handling Units, chillers, boilers). Integrating these 
approaches into the future is essential. Notably, providing occupants 
with control over their environment can enhance their satisfaction, 
making it worthwhile exploring a balance between automated and 
manual control methods, while also considering occupant privacy 
and data protection.
This review offers significant contributions to knowledge and valu -
able implications for stakeholders involved in healthcare and architec -
tural design, as outlined below: 
• Comprehensive review and policy formulation: This paper guides 
researchers in identifying evolving focuses and methodological gaps 
and assists policymakers in enhancing regulatory frameworks based 
on proven effectiveness. It serves as a foundational tool for shaping 
informed, evidence-based policies and research directions.
• Establishment of design standards: The findings aid architectural 
designers in creating environments that support occupant well- 
being, providing healthcare administrators with actionable insights 
for facility improvements that enhance recovery and performance. 
This promotes a holistic approach to design that prioritizes human 
health and comfort.
• Training and advanced control technology implementation: Effective 
management awareness is enhanced through targeted training pro -
grams, which are recommended for staff and extended through 
educational materials such as videos and posters for patients and 
visitors to improve environmental quality at minimal costs. The 
introduction of automated technologies enhances operational effi -
ciency and occupant comfort, contributing to smarter building 
management.
• Development of a dynamic framework: This paper presents a 
comprehensive control system that integrates person-environment- 
behavior dynamics, combining individual evaluation systems with 
existing environmental control strategies. It empowers healthcare 
administrators and facility managers to prioritize modifications that 
enhance patient recovery and staff performance, laying the 
groundwork for developing responsive healthcare environments 
through advanced technology.
• Application of advanced technologies: In addition to implementing 
automated control technologies, this review advocates for enhancing 
the digital sophistication of entire medical facilities, establishing a 
robust digital twin control platform. This approach provides a stra -
tegic direction for future research and practical applications, 
encouraging the integration of cutting-edge technologies and data- 
driven designs to align healthcare interiors with occupant needs, 
facilitating personalized and efficient healthcare environments.
The findings of this review have significant implications for health -
care facility design and operation, offering practical applications and 
future research directions. The proposed restorative perception frame -
work can guide architects and facility managers in creating occupant- 
centric environments that enhance patient recovery and staff well- 
being, for instance, through adaptive lighting and improved ventila -
tion systems. We recommend integrating these findings into healthcare 
architectural policies, aligning with standards like WELL Building Cer -
tification, to promote health-focused design. Future research should 
focus on validating the framework through empirical studies in diverse 
healthcare settings, exploring cultural and temporal influences, and 
developing simulation models to optimize control solutions [ 254 ]. 
These efforts will further advance occupant-centric approaches in

healthcare environments.
However, there are some limitations in the current paper as follows: 
• Cultural, social, and economic influences may also play a significant 
role in shaping restorative perception in healthcare facilities, but are 
not fully addressed in this study. For instance, cultural preferences 
for natural elements, social expectations for privacy, and economic 
constraints on advanced control systems can influence occupant 
experiences and framework applicability [ 162 ]. While the proposed 
framework focuses on technical integration, future research should 
incorporate these factors to ensure broader relevance across diverse 
healthcare settings, particularly in low-resource environments or 
culturally distinct regions.
• While this study summarizes control behaviors and automated sys -
tems, it does not propose specific models to simulate human 
controller behaviors in hospital settings. Simulation models, such as 
agent-based modeling, offer significant potential to predict how oc -
cupants interact with environmental controls, such as adjusting 
lighting or ventilation based on personal needs. These models can 
enhance the design of occupant-centric control solutions by simu -
lating dynamic interactions and optimizing system performance 
[ 214 ]. Future research should prioritize developing such models to 
validate the proposed framework and improve its applicability in 
healthcare facilities
• Temporal variables, such as day/night cycles and length of stay, are 
critical for understanding restorative perception in healthcare facil -
ities but are under-explored in the reviewed literature. For instance, 
circadian lighting that mimics natural day/night cycles can enhance 
patient recovery, while prolonged hospital stays may alter occupant 
sensitivity to IEQ factors like thermal [ 229 ]. The absence of temporal 
considerations in current models limits their applicability to dynamic 
healthcare environments. This study highlights the need for future 
research to incorporate these variables into assessment frameworks 
to better capture occupant experiences.
• The proposed restorative evaluation framework is designed for 
practical implementation in real-world healthcare facilities through 
strategic integration with existing BMS and pilot testing. By 
leveraging BMS, the framework can utilize real-time data from sen -
sors (e.g., air quality, lighting) to adjust environmental conditions, 
ensuring scalability and cost-effectiveness. Pilot studies in diverse 
healthcare settings, such as intensive care units and outpatient 
clinics, can validate the framework ’ s effectiveness and identify 
context-specific adaptations [ 91 ]. Stakeholder engagement, 
including facility managers and healthcare providers, is essential to 
address implementation challenges, such as retrofitting costs and 
staff training, ensuring the framework ’ s applicability across varied 
healthcare environments.
• This study does not analyze the geographical diversity or regional 
comparisons of the reviewed studies. Future research could explore 
regional variations to enhance the framework ’ s applicability across 
diverse healthcare contexts.
