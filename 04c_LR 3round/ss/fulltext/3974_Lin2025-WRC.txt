ABSTRACT
Worker-robot collaboration (WRC) has emerged as a transformative approach to augmenting the productivity of 
the construction industry. However, the development of a safety monitoring method or system for stopping robot 
operations in emergency is imperative, especially for seamless WRC on site. This paper presents a digital twin- 
enabled safety monitoring system for seamless WRC on site, characterized by its comprehensive perception of 
dynamic entities and dynamic calculation of protective separation distances during seamless WRC. The effec -
tiveness of the proposed system is substantiated through a series of experiments, the result demonstrates its 
proficiency in mitigating collisions during robot operation in both static and dynamic WRC scenarios. The system 
achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 frames per second, an average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and a 
positional perception error of 0.09 m. It not only provides a practical tool for the implementation of seamless 
WRC on site, but also offers valuable insights for future WRC research.
1. Introduction
The construction industry has shown a keen interest in robotic 
technology to address issues such as labour shortage, low productivity, 
and poor working condition. Various automated construction robot 
systems have been developed [ 1 ], however, challenges arise when these 
systems are deployed in a highly dynamic and complex construction 
environment, leading to reduced precision and efficiency. Worker-robot 
collaboration (WRC) has been recognized as a promising method by 
leveraging the complementary skills of humans and machines to 
enhance the on-site usability of construction robots [ 2 ].
The predominant form of WRC in construction currently relies on 
remote control methods [ 3 ]. However, closer collaboration, both 
spatially and temporally, can transform the role of human workers from 
robot operators to teammates, enhancing the productivity and flexibility 
of on-site WRC [ 4 ]. Despite these benefits, safety concerns remain a 
significant barrier to seamless WRC in construction [ 5 ]. Since workers 
and construction robots share a same operational space in seamless 
WRC, physical risks arise due to planned or unexpected contact caused 
by the movement of either party [ 6 ]. When workers need to collaborate 
closely with robots to complete tasks, physical risks in WRC can further 
increase [ 7 ]. Therefore, an effective safety monitoring system is essen -
tial for achieving seamless WRC in construction.
Despite its critical importance, implementing a safety monitoring 
system on construction sites presents significant challenges. The un -
structured and dynamic nature of construction scenarios may decrease 
the efficiency and precision of the safety monitoring system [ 8 ]. 
Traditional safety monitoring methods in industrial WRC, such as using 
physical or virtual fences to spatially separate robots and workers, are 
impractical. These methods tend to fragment a continuous workspace, 
reduce operational efficiency, and fail to adapt to rapidly changing 
construction environment. Therefore, there is a need for an advanced 
safety monitoring system with fine-grained monitoring capabilities and 
spatial flexibility that can be easily integrated into construction robots to 
support safe and seamless WRC on site.
This research proposes a digital twin-enabled safety monitoring 
system (SMS) for seamless WRC on construction sites. The contributions 
of this research are two-fold. First, it introduces a digital twin (DT)- 
based system capable of comprehensively perceiving and integrating the 
spatial information of workers, construction robots and construction 
materials during WRC, and establishing their approximate geometric 
occupation representations for spatial collision monitoring. Second, it 
proposes a rapid separation distance (SD) and a dynamic protective 
separation distance (PSD) calculation method for real-time safety 
monitoring in seamless WRC. The rest of this paper is organized as fol -
lows. Section 2 reviews related research on WRC in construction and

2. Literature review
2.1. WRC in construction
Combining the hierarchical structure of construction works proposed 
by Bernold [ 9 ], with the Level of Robotic Automation (LoRA) defined by 
Beer et al. [ 10 ], it establishes a robust theoretical foundation for cate -
gorizing WRC in construction. Based on the work hierarchy at which 
WRC occurs, the collaborative system can be classified into three types: 
Division Collaboration (DC), Sequence Collaboration (SC), and Task 
Collaboration (TC) ( Table 1 ). Each collaboration approach involves 
construction robots with different activity capabilities and correspond -
ing LoRA that align with their designated collaboration roles.
DC focuses on appropriate labour division between workers and ro -
bots, such as task allocation [ 11 , 12 ] and scheduling planning [ 13 , 14 ]. 
Construction robots in DC are usually assumed to have a high LoRA 
(Levels 7–10) and can autonomously execute assigned tasks consisting of 
complex processes similar to workers. Through adequate labour divi -
sion, workers and robots perform tasks independently in terms of time or 
space, resulting in clear temporal and spatial boundaries regarding their 
operation space [ 8 ]. Correspondingly, safety risks are minimized 
because there is minimal shared workspace between humans and robots, 
as well as physical risks.
SC involves a series of subtasks executed in a predetermined process 
to achieve the collaboration objective, with each subtask requiring 
specific skills from either workers or robots [ 15 ]. In SC, human workers 
typically handle the more fragile and dynamic subtasks, while robots 
undertake those with high physical demands. Related research focuses 
on designing optimal processes to maximize the full potential of human- 
robot collaboration. For example, MitterBerger et al. [ 2 ] developed a 
wood framework construction method, where robots hold and position 
wooden beams and workers then tie them together with ropes. Con -
struction activities involving the installation and delivery of construc -
tion materials are a key focus of SC research [ 16 ]. For instance, Wu et al. 
[ 17 ] analyzed the efficiency of bricklaying teams, where robots lay 
bricks supplied by workers. In SC, robots’ actions may need to be 
planned or authorized by human workers [ 18 ], but be executed auton -
omously by robots, demonstrating a middle LoRA in Levels 4–6. 
Nevertheless, the autonomy of robots and complex spatial interaction 
increases physical risks in WRC.
In TC, human workers and construction robots jointly complete a 
single challenging task, which involves the highest level of human-robot 
interaction (HRI). The task is typically characterized by the high de -
mand of both physical and cognitive efforts, exceeding the sense and 
plan capabilities of current construction robots as well as the physical 
ability of human workers. Therefore, human workers usually act as 
operators and control construction robots manually or remotely through 
HRI devices, such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR) and 
force interaction devices [ 19 ]. For example, Chen et al. [ 20 ] presented a 
WRC approach for the sorting of construction wastes, where workers use 
AR devices to guide a robot based on their judgement of potential waste 
materials. Given the low LoRA (Levels 1–3) of construction robots in TC, 
physical risks are inherently controlled as robots are under human 
supervision.
In practical applications, WRC is usually based on hybrid configu -
rations. For instance, a robot only possessing motion capability might 
initially collaborate with a worker to form a human-robot team for TC, 
thus engaging with another worker in process-level SC. Such hybrid 
WRC strategies become particularly prevalent when construction ro -
bots’ LoRA remains insufficient to support their independent partici -
pation in higher-level collaborative activities. The operational 
compromise enables robotic systems to contribute to advanced con -
struction operations while compensating for current technological lim -
itations. However, the hybridity inevitably introduces systemic safety 
concerns across robotic applications - irrespective of their designated 
roles in WRC or attained LoRA levels. This further underscores the 
critical need for developing adaptive safety monitoring systems in WRC.
2.2. Safety monitoring method for WRC
Safety monitoring methods are pivotal in enhancing robotic safety 
[ 16 ]. Unlike industrial robots that are typically mounted on static bases, 
construction robots are usually mobile, comprising mobile platforms, 
kinematic structure, task-specific end-effectors, and integrated sensors 
[ 1 ]. The foundational architecture, first established by Shimizu Site 
Robot [ 21 ], remains fundamentally retained by modern construction 
robots. While dedicated safety standards for construction robots remain 
under development, ANSI/RIA R15.08 [ 22 ] provides critical normative 
guidance as the first safety standard for industrial mobile robots (IMR). 
Particularly, its classification of Type C IMR, defined as “mobile plat -
forms integrated with manipulator attachments”, is a configuration 
structurally analogous to modern construction robots. The standard 
mandates that manipulators mounted on mobile platforms comply with 
ISO 10218 [ 23 ] requirements, which specifies four principal collabo -
rative methodologies: Safety-Related Monitoring Stop (SRMS), Hand 
Guidance (HG), Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM), and Power and 
Force Limiting (PFL). The subsequent publication of ISO 15066 [ 24 ] 
significantly advances WRC safety frameworks by providing detailed 
design guidelines for safe WRC methods. Therefore, different safety 
monitoring methods can be designed based on WRC methods adapted in 
production.
SRMS demands that robotic operations are conducted at a pre -
determined distance from human workers, commonly achieved through 
the implementation of physical or virtual barriers to demarcate zones 
separating humans and robots. While effective and widely used in in -
dustrial environments, the method has been criticized for creating 
spatial segmentation and may potentially undermine on-site produc -
tivity [ 8 ]. The HG method facilitates teaching and programming robots 
through direct physical interaction [ 25 ], however, SRMS must be the 
prerequisite for HG. SSM defines separation distance (SD) and protective 
separation distance (PSD) based on the speed of humans and robots, 
dictating that robotic actions are permissible when SD exceeds PSD. 
Since SSM requires simultaneous detection of the spatial position and 
speed of both humans and robots, the corresponding safety monitoring 
method requires higher sensing resources. PFL safeguards human 
workers from physical harm by limiting the force output of robots during 
contact [ 26 ]. However, PFL serves as a post-contact protective measure 
and is not applicable when robots possess surface dangers, such as

becoming increasingly cost-effective and feasible.
The key of SSM is to calculate SD and PSD according to the spatial 
motion information of both robots and collaborating workers, which 
requires advanced robot intelligence. For monitoring human bodies 
within collaborative workspace, sensors with three-dimensional 
perception capabilities, such as light detection and ranging (LiDAR) 
[ 27 ] and depth cameras [ 28 ], are commonly employed. For seamless 
WRC, limb-level monitoring becomes imperative for accurate SD 
sensing. The effectiveness of SD sensing is heavily influenced by how 
obstacles are represented. For instance, Lucci et al. [ 26 ] utilized depth 
cameras for the limb-level detection of human workers, but the collision 
representation relied on point clouds, which are less versatile than 
surface-based representations like triangle meshes. Similarly, Rose -
nstrauch et al. [ 29 ] used Kinect V2 for human body detection, with a 
simplified representation consisting of joint keypoints without collision 
volumes. Li et al. [ 30 ] also adopted joint keypoints for SD calculations. 
However, in close WRC scenarios, keypoints may not always represent 
the closest points on a worker ’ s body relative to a robot, potentially 
leading to inaccurate estimation for SD. Surface-based representations 
for both workers and robots can provide more accurate SD measure -
ments. Nevertheless, overly complex representations, such as the Skin -
ned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model [ 25 ], need substantial 
computing resources, thus hindering real-time performance. Simplified 
geometric representations, including spheres, boxes, and capsules 
[ 31 , 32 ], offer a viable alternative, but the level of detail (LoD) must be 
carefully designed to strike a balance between computational efficiency 
and representation accuracy.
While ISO 15066 provides calculation methods for PSD, obtaining 
necessary value for calculation, particularly the speed of workers and 
robots, renders it challenging. A common approach is to set a constant 
value for workers ’ speed to estimate PSD [ 33 – 35 ], where a maximum 
speed assumption for workers is often established for safety. However, 
the assumption also constrains robots ’ workspace, potentially reducing 
the efficiency and flexibility of WRC [ 36 ]. Besides, current research for 
safety monitoring concentrates on WRC in manufacturing, where robots 
usually operate on a tabletop scale, using stationary arms to handle 
small objects. However, in construction sites, components and objects 
such as drywall panels manipulated by robots may have a large size and 
should not be overlooked [ 18 ]. In summary, although SSM is the most 
suitable collaboration method for WRC in construction, there is still a 
lack of an appropriate safety monitoring system with capabilities of 1) 
balancing the computational accuracy and efficiency of SD, 2) dynam -
ically calculating PSD, and 3) monitoring objects held by robots to 
support safe WRC in construction.
3. Methodology
This research employs a constructive research approach [ 37 ], 
encompassing three phases: problem identification, method develop -
ment, and validation. The challenges identified in current safety moni -
toring methods or systems for WRC on site include a lack of efficient and 
dynamic sensing methods for SD and PSD, as well as integrating 
detection for manipulated materials by construction robots during 
monitoring. To address the gap, this research introduces a digital twin- 
enabled SMS for WRC. The conceptual framework of the proposed sys -
tem is depicted in Fig. 1 .
Digital twin fundamentally comprises three core components: 
physical assets in operational environment, their synchronized virtual 
replica in cyberspace, and bidirectional data pipelines enabling data and 
information exchange between the two domains [ 38 ]. Through the 
integration of multimodal sensors, heterogeneous data and computa -
tional intelligence, digital twin establishes a bridge between physical 
and virtual environments. The intrinsic demand for DT-enabled safety 
monitoring stems from the inherent complexity of construction robots. 
Beyond safety monitoring, construction robots require basic capabilities 
in path planning and motion control to execute tasks. Maintaining 
functional decoupling while achieving resource reuse across subsystems 
becomes critical in such complex architectures. In the proposed system, 
safety monitoring functions as an upper-layer application are built upon 
the foundational digital twin infrastructure, thereby achieving systemic 
decoupling from other operational functions. It is crucial that the virtual 
representation of physical assets, as a digital avatar, can be simulta -
neously leveraged for diverse operational requirements - from real-time 
path planning to high-level situational awareness - beyond its primary 
safety monitoring purpose.
In the SMS, the perception of physical assets, including robots, 
workers, and materials, is achieved through sensors integrated with 
robots. The sensors collect environmental data, from which algorithms 
extract the spatial poses, geometric representations, and velocities of 
physical assets. The information is then represented as virtual assets or 
values within the digital twin system. The virtual assets or values pro -
vide essential information for calculating SD and PSD according to the 
SSM method outlined in ISO 15066 [ 24 ]. SD and PSD are compared 
circularly to assess the real-time safety of WRC. If PSD is exceeded by SD, 
the system accesses the physical actuators of robots to stop it, thereby

4. Safety monitoring method
The proposed SMS mainly comprises two components: the percep -
tion of physical assets, which provides basic information for detecting 
safety states, and safety monitoring based on SSM, which offers the 
criteria for assessing safety states.
4.1. Perception of physical assets in the digital twin system
Physical assets for WRC in construction include construction robots, 
construction workers, and construction materials. Due to the heteroge -
neity of their movement patterns and physical characteristics, it is 
necessary to adopt different approaches to perceive their spatial poses 
and occupancy so as to construct their virtual representations in the 
digital twin system.
4.1.1. Construction robot
The construction robot prototype used in this research ( Fig. 2 a) 
consists of 1) a four-wheel omnidirectional mobile chassis, 2) a 7-de -
gree-of-freedom (DoF) Kinova Jaco 2 robotic arm with a maximum 
payload of 2.6 kg, 3) a two-finger gripper as the end-effector, and 4) an 
attached depth camera for environmental perception. The configuration 
of this robot mirrors that of the previously described general construc -
tion robot configuration. As a prototype, it can perform general WRC 
tasks including material transportation (e.g., handling materials for 
workers) and cooperative operations (e.g., workpiece stabilization 
during work machining tasks).
To establish a digital twin representation of construction robots in 
virtual environments, it needs to sense the spatial poses of their moving 
components (represented via coordinate systems) and model their 
spatial occupancy. The mobile base of construction robots is the primary 
reference as the base coordinate system. The X-axis of the base coordi -
nate system is aligned with the positive direction of robots ’ movement, 
while the Z -axis is aligned with the upward vertical direction and the Y- 
axis is determined by right-hand rules ( Fig. 2 a). The spatial occupancy 
representation of mobile bases in the virtual environment is based on a 
simplified cuboid model that approximates their physical dimensions.
The kinematic structure of construction robots, such as robotic arms, 
can be modelled as the assembly of multiple rigid bodies interconnected 
through rotational and translational joints. The pose of kinematic 
structure can be comprehensively represented using individual coordi -
nate systems of its joints. The Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) method provides 
a parameterized approach to describe the transformations among the 
coordinate systems using four parameters, including α , d , a , and θ . 
Taking the 7-DoF robotic arm adopted by the prototype as an example, 
α , d , and a are constants related to the inherent dimensions of the links. 
The joint angles θ of its rotational joints are the sole variables and can be 
measured through the integration of rotary encoders within the joints. 
The Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) is utilized to describe and 
store the constant DH parameters of construction robots, complemented 
by the real-time sensing of joint angles to reconstruct the pose of kine -
matic structure. Given that the links of robotic arm are generally cy -
lindrical in shape, it is suggested to use capsules to represent their spatial 
occupancy in the virtual environment ( Fig. 2 c).
Vision sensors are critical for construction robots to collect external 
information in a dynamic environment. Accurately determining the pose 
of sensors within the base coordinate system is crucial for robots to 
precisely perceive the pose of other external objects in the environment. 
Eye-in-hand calibration is utilized to obtain the precise pose of sensors 
relative to the base coordinate system, denoted as T

4.1.2. Human worker
To accurately perceive workers ’ poses, this research employs a 3D 
human pose estimation algorithm to derive a skeletal model from RGB- 
depth (RGBD) images captured by depth cameras. Commonly-employed 
skeletal models for human pose estimation include the 17-point model 
delineated by Common Objects in Context (COCO) [ 39 ] and the 32-point

demonstrated superior real-time performance and accuracy in human 
factors and safety applications [ 41 ]. The pose estimation process in -

network and estimating the position of individual ’ s joints. Then, depth 
values are assigned to each pixel of human masks based on depth data, 
and each pose of joint is determined via random forest voting. As a 
result, AKBT outputs a 32-point skeletal model, as depicted in Fig. 3 b.

4.1.3. Material
Due to the diverse forms of construction materials, it is essential to 
establish a consistent standard to define their coordinate systems to 
unambiguously describe their poses. According to their approximate 
geometric forms, common materials can be categorized into volumetric, 
planar, and linear types. Their coordinate systems can be defined as 
follows.
4.1.3.1. Volumetric material. Materials possessing comparable di -
mensions in three dimensional space, such as prefabricated beams, slabs, 
masonry blocks, and I-beams, are considered volumetric. The Z -axis of 
the volumetric material ’ s coordinate system should be vertically upward 
and aligned with one of its edges, while the X-axis aligns with the longer 
side of the remaining two ones, and the Y-axis aligns with the shorter 
one ( Fig. 4 a).
4.1.3.2. Planar material. Materials with comparable dimensions in 
length and width but exhibiting minimal thickness, such as tiles, ceil -
ings, curtain wall panels, doors, and windows, are classified as planar. 
Planar materials often exhibit surface anisotropy. For instance, tiles 
have distinct glazed and rough surfaces. The Z -axis of the planar mate -
rial ’ s coordinate system is defined to point toward the “ external ” side 
after installation if materials exhibit surface anisotropy (relative to the 
side contacting the working surface), while the X-axis aligns with the 
longer side of the remaining two ones, and the Y-axis aligns with the 
shorter one ( Fig. 4 b).
4.1.3.3. Linear material. Materials with significant length but relatively 
small dimensions in the other two dimensions, such as rebar, rods, and 
pipes, are considered linear. Linear materials are typically geometrically 
symmetrical about the central axis, such as scaffolding pipes, drainage 
pipes, and rebar, with circular cross-sections. For linear materials, the X- 
axis of their coordinate system should align with the longitudinal axis of 
materials. If linear materials are rotationally symmetric about the X- 
axis, the Y and Z axes can be arbitrarily oriented; otherwise, they should 
follow the same definitions as volumetric or planar materials ( Fig. 4 c).
Fig. 4 shows the geometric representation of materials in the virtual 
environment, designed to closely resemble their actual shapes. Volu -
metric and planar materials are typically represented by cuboids, while 
linear materials are represented by capsules.

recognition, making them widely used in augmented reality and mixed

4.2. Safety monitoring based on SSM
The key to SSM-based safety monitoring lies in comparing SD with 
PSD as a threshold. SD is an objective physical quantity, and its rapid 
calculation is crucial to meet the real-time requirements of safety 
monitoring. Meanwhile, the calculation method for PSD should be 
designed to account for dynamic collision risks in WRC.
4.2.1. Separation distance calculation
Based on the pose and geometric representations of physical assets 
perceived by the digital twin system, SD is expressed as the minimum 
distance between the geometric representations of a human worker and 
a construction robot (including both the robot and material it handles). 
To calculate SD, it must consider all possible configurations of workers ’ 
limbs relative to the components of robots. Human workers ’ represen -
tations are a capsule set C
h
, and robots ’ representations include a 
capsule set C
r 
and a box set B
r
. Given that all entities ’ representations 
are designed as the combinations of capsules or boxes, the calculation of

4.2.2. Protective separation distance calculation
The design of PSD should be universally applicable to all types of 
construction robots, irrespective of their size, shape, or functionality. 
Achieving the universality necessitates a focus on the intrinsic safety of 
human-robot collaboration. Fundamentally, the risk of human-robot 
collisions stems from kinetic energy possessed by construction robots

such collisions occur, or at the very least, to ensure that robots dissipate 
their kinetic energy upon impact. To apply the principle across various 
robotic platforms, PSD design must account for the differing stopping 
times required by different robots and the performance capabilities of 
their sensing systems. The ISO 15066 standard defines the general

5. Experiment and result
To evaluate the feasibility and performance of the proposed SMS, a 
series of experiments were designed and conducted in an construction 
setting. The system was comprehensively tested across non-conflict, 
static-conflict, and dynamic-conflict scenarios.
5.1. Experiment setting
The construction robot used in the experiment is identical to the 
prototype in Fig. 2 a. The depth camera utilized by the robot is Obbrec 
Femto Bolt, providing a relative depth accuracy of 0.15 % at 1 m. The 
safety monitoring system was tested on an Ubuntu 20.04 platform with 
hardware specifications of an Intel i7 – 11,730 CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 
3070 graphics card. Three scenarios were designed to comprehensively 
test the performance of the system, as shown in Fig. 9 . 
1) In the non-conflict scenario, the robot repeatedly moved its end- 
effector along a fixed route while gripping a pipe, with the worker 
positioning outside the robot ’ s workspace and remaining stationary. 
This experiment aimed to evaluate the SMS ’ s feasibility and its la -
tency and accuracy in perceiving SD.

2) In the static-conflict scenario, the worker placed their hand in the

simulated a static human-robot conflict scenario, where a robot 
needs to stop before a collision occurs.
3) In the dynamic-conflict scenario, the worker initially stayed outside

more dynamic conflict scenario, which more closely mimicked WRC

Three participants were invited in the experiment, with their heights 
being 167 cm, 175 cm, and 181 cm, corresponding to the 50th (P50), 
80th (P80), and 95th (P95) percentiles of adult static anthropometric 
dimensions respectively. For safety considerations, all participants were 
asked to wear appropriate protective equipment in the static-conflict 
and dynamic-conflict scenarios to prevent injuries from potential 
safety monitoring failures. The construction robot was supervised by an 
operator who ensured that the robot could be manually halted in case 
the SMS failed.
5.2. Result analysis
Utilizing the DT system, virtual assets and values, including spatial 
data related to construction robots, workers, and materials, as well as SD 
and PSD, were recorded and stored as datasets. The performance of the 
proposed SMS was analyzed using the datasets from each scenario.
5.2.1. Non-conflict scenario
In the non-conflict scenario, the primary focus of analysis was on the 
system ’ s accuracy and efficiency under various WRC situations. The 
participants were instructed to assume various postures throughout the 
experiment, including standing facing forward, standing sideways, and 
squatting, to validate the system ’ s effectiveness across a range of 
simulated WRC situations. Fig. 10 illustrates the visualization of 
perception and SD calculation for a worker and robot across different 
poses. Under stable relative postures, the perceived SD was recorded, 
and the actual minimum human-robot distance was measured using a 
tape measure. Contributions from the three participants yielded 21 sets 
of perception-measurement data, as plotted in Fig. 11 . Special attention 
was given to errors where the actual measurements were less than the 
perceived SD, as these discrepancies could lead to overly optimistic 
collision assessments. The results show that the maximum SD error, 
where the actual measurement is less than the perceived value, is 9 cm. 
It means that the proposed digital twin system can accurately perceive 
the poses of all entities and precisely calculate SD under various 
postures.

reciprocating horizontal motion in the X-axis direction of the base co -
ordinate system, while the worker maintained a relatively stationary 
position. The robotic arm ’ s self-position perception measurement was

5.2.4. Comparison with other SSM methods
To further illustrate the sophistication of the proposed SMS, quan -
titative and qualitative comparisons were made with other similar 
methods based on SSM, detailed in Table 5 . Firstly, the proposed SMS 
monitors the entire human body and uses geometric shapes to represent 
the collision regions of entities, which provides a more accurate repre -
sentation of entities ’ spatial occupancy. Therefore, it offers refined SD 
perception compared to methods that rely on single point or keypoints 
for entity representation in human-robot distance calculation. The more 
precise SD perception allows for closer WRC while ensuring safety, 
which is essential for achieving seamless WRC on site.
Secondly, the proposed SMS considers the manipulated materials by 
construction robots during safety monitoring, offering a more compre -
hensive assessment of on-site WRC risks. Meanwhile, the dynamic PSD 
calculation method facilitates a more flexible WRC organization, 
allowing workers to operate closer to robots at low speed and maintain a 
greater safe distance at fast speed, manifested by an automatically 
adjusted PSD.
Lastly, the detection speed of the SMS is 9.8 FPS, achieving finer SD 
perception and dynamic PSD calculation without significantly compro -
mising computational speed, manifesting its capability for real-time 
safety monitoring in WRC on site.

6. Conclusion
Seamless WRC has considerable potential for enhancing productivity 
in construction but must be supported by adequate safety monitoring 
methods or systems. Although SSM is deemed suitable for WRC on site, 
the practicality of existing methods is limited due to that they are 
designed especially for industrial WRC that is less dynamic and complex. 
To bridge the gap, this paper introduced a digital twin-enabled safety 
monitoring system. The proposed SMS offers the following contribu -
tions: 1) a comprehensive perception method for WRC entities, 
including construction workers, robots, and materials; 2) a geometric 
solid-based virtual representation method for WRC entities that balances 
sensing accuracy and efficiency; 3) a rapid calculation method for SD, 
and 4) a dynamic calculation method for PSD. The above-mentioned 
physical entities, their virtual twins, and relevant functions are inte -
grated within a digital twin system. Experiments have shown that the 
proposed SMS achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 FPS, an 
average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and an SD perceiving error of 0.09 
m. This system has been validated in both static and dynamic-conflict 
scenarios, demonstrating its safety-guarding capabilities.
The future directions for further enhancing the proposed SMS are 
discussed as follows. First, this system will be validated on a broader 
range of construction robots beyond the mobile manipulator configu -
ration, such as humanoid robots. Second, it will continuously benefit 
from more advanced perception methods for human workers and con -
struction materials with less latency and higher accuracy. Finally, the 
incorporation of predictive methods for workers ’ and robots ’ motion 
will further enhance the performance of PSD calculation, empowering a 
more robust and flexible WRC on site.
