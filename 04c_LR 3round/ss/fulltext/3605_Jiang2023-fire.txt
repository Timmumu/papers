ABSTRACT  
A fire protection system takes on a critical significance to building operation. This paper describes the use of 
digital twins (DTs) and semantic web technologies for the intelligent control of building fire protection (BFP) 
systems in fire accidents. Specifically, a data fusion stage and several information-based control mechanisms are 
involved in the use of the two technologies. A designed BFP ontology is considered as the semantic model and 
basis of data fusion between the static building geometric information and the dynamic sensing data. The above 
information is incorporated into a DT data model, which is considered as the mapping of physical space. 
Moreover, rule models and process models are developed to achieve intelligent control mechanisms and keep the 
DT data model synced with the physical space. A case study based on a fire accident simulation was conducted to 
verify the feasibility of the use of DTs and semantic web technologies.   
1. Introduction 
Fire protection has become a vital research topic in the field of public 
safety protection since there have been considerable property damage 
arising from fire incidents and fire casualties. Building fire protection 
(BFP) system is capable of responding quickly to a fire emergency, which 
is of great significance to reduce losses. In conventional fire alarm sys -
tems, human reactions are required to inform the fire department to 
track down the fire location and organise an evacuation after a fire 
incident is detected [ 1 ]. In most cases, if people are not alerted in time or 
fail to control the fire at its early stages, the fire will be more serious and 
cause more losses [ 2 ]. For instance, the delayed evacuation of the 
Grenfell Tower Fire in London in June 2017 was cited as a great tragedy, 
which gutted the entire building, killing 80 people and injuring over 70 
people [ 3 , 4 ]. In addition, insufficient information regarding the fire 
incident may hinder the organisation of evacuation and rescue [ 5 ]. 
Accordingly, there is an urgent need for an intelligent fire protection 
system that can display information regarding fires and provide rational 
strategies to deal with fire incidents automatically at an early stage. 
An intelligent fire protection system refers to a comprehensive 
building automation system that comprises several basic subsystems (e. 
g., fire alarm systems, smoke control systems, fire door monitoring 
systems, sprinkler systems, emergency broadcast systems, as well as 
evacuation guidance systems). Intelligent fire protection entails acti -
vating the fire alarm system, analysing the current fire situation, and 
then sending commands to other subsystems to respond to fire emer -
gencies. In the above processes, timely feedback and integration of in -
formation are of high importance. The application of some emerging 
technologies in this regard has been investigated extensively. For 
example, building information modelling (BIM) provides a three- 
dimensional (3D) view of a building and stores and retrieves the infor -
mation regarding the building. The vivid 3D environment created using 
BIM may provide visual guidance for fire rescue operations [ 6 ]. 
Furthermore, the Internet of Things (IoT) is an integration of technolo -
gies that creates connections between all information sensing devices 
and the internet to support effective sensing, reliable transmission, and 
centralised management of data collected by IoT end products [ 7 ]. The 
integration of BIM and IoT systems is capable of presenting the real-time 
state and the as-built structures of a building. Fang et al. [ 5 ] developed a 
framework of an IoT-aided system that can integrate real-time moni -
toring data of the fire environment and people counting collected by 
multiple BIM detectors and sensors to dynamically optimize building 
evacuation. Moreover, the integration of BIM, IoT, virtual reality (VR), 
and augmented reality (AR) can underpin path finding in fire rescue

[8,9]. In brief, the above existing technologies have the potential to 
increase BFP efficiency. However, since recorded data are collected in 
different formats from a wide variety of sources, and the design of 
building structures and IoT networks are complicated, data interopera -
bility and system coordination pose critical challenges to intelligent 
control of the fire protection system during fire emergencies. 
Digital twins (DTs) are considered as an applicable solution to the 
above challenges. The emergence of the DT has increased efficiency in 
numerous industries. DT’s applied research in the architecture, engi -
neering, and construction (AEC) domain has aroused wide interest since 
2018 [10]. The DT-driven approach is capable of facilitating the man -
agement, planning, forecasting, and presentation of buildings, in -
frastructures, or city assets [11]. Since DT enables the integration of 
virtual representation and real-time data, dynamic monitoring and 
predictive operation can be achieved through facilities management 
[12,13]. The predictive approach can reduce operational cost and time 
loss. On that basis, the fire scene information collected from the IoT 
systems can be integrated with BIM models to provide a digital repre -
sentation of the fire protection system. Data analysis and emergency 
simulation can be achieved using the virtual fire protection system. 
Thus, DT serves as the real world’s intelligent brain to coordinate 
various subsystems to cope with fire incidents. The application of DT to 
the intelligent control of BFP systems comprises two crucial aspects as 
follows: (1) the digital representation is a high-precision mapping of 
real-world objects; (2) dynamic updating mechanisms are essential to 
ensure that the virtual is always in sync with the real. However, previous 
research has placed a major focus on anomaly detection or prediction 
based on deep learning methods. Research on the interactive feedback 
between the virtual and the real is still required, which takes on a great 
significance to the intelligent control of the fire protection system. 
Furthermore, as the requirements for building information vary 
throughout the lifecycle and the data are in different formats (e.g., 
building models, videos, pictures, documents), a unified data descrip -
tion is expected to solve the issue of data exchange and reuse. 
This study describes the use of DTs and semantic web technologies 
for the intelligent control of BFP systems. Data are organised as a linked 
graph based on the Resource Description Framework (RDF) [14], such 
that a uniform representation syntax is provided for building data in 
different formats. Accordingly, the query language, SPARQL Protocol 
and RDF Query Language (SPARQL) [15], can be adopted to retrieve 
building information efficiently [16]. Moreover, ontology is a common 
vocabulary and formal description [17] to define how the above data are 
linked, and how computers understand the above data, such that the 
semantic interoperability between the data can be addressed. In the AEC 
domain, since a unified data integration environment is created, the use 
of semantic web technologies has been studied as a promising strategy 
for data interoperability [18,19], information exchange [20,21], and 
complex cross-domain linkage [22]. A series of works on the develop -
ment of ontologies (e.g., IoT sensor observations ontology (SSN) [23], 
brick ontology [24], and building topology ontology (BOT) [25]) have 
laid a basis for the integration of BIM data and IoT data. In this study, an 
ontology is discussed for data fusion based on the above ontologies, and 
a rule model and a process model are built to achieve the interaction 
mechanism between the virtual and the real. 
The rest of this study is organised as follows. In Section 2, recent 
literature on the application of BIM and IoT in fire risk management, DT 
modelling, and semantic web technologies used for data interoperability 
in the AEC domain is reviewed to identify the research gap. In Section 3, 
the use of DT and sematic web technologies is described for the intelli -
gent control of the fire protection system. Moreover, a brief introduction 
of the critical procedure of the use of the two technologies for the 
intelligent control of BFP systems in fire accidents is given in this sec -
tion. Section 4 verifies the feasibility and efficiency of the use of the two 
technologies with a case study, and the discussion is given in Section 5. 
Section 6 draws a conclusion of the study and elaborates on future work. 
2. Literature review 
2.1. Fire risk management: application of BIM and IoT 
Fire risk management is of great significance to building operations 
that comprise fire protection design, fire/smoke spread limitation, fire 
extinguishing, and escape probability [26]. It is a complicated task, and 
there are several factors for the efficiency of fire risk management, 
which include the characteristics of the occupants, the building facil -
ities, and the features of the fire [27]. An integrated environment 
capable of managing and analysing the above factors should be estab -
lished [28]. The use of BIM has shown its potential as such a solution. 
From the perspective of modelling, BIM can be considered as a 3D 
model with considerable building information. The geometric data of 
BIM are beneficial to the assessment of the reliability of the design of fire 
protection facilities [28], planning escape routes [29,30], and demon -
stration of the real-time fire status [31]. In addition, the informatization 
features of BIM (e.g., information storage, extraction, and retrieval) can 
be employed as the database for BIM-based fire safety information 
management. For instance, Wang et al. [32] applied BIM to support asset 
management for fire safety (e.g., the maintenance information of fire 
safety equipment). Wu and Ma [33] proposed a BIM-based fire emer -
gency management system that comprises a fire intelligence monitoring 
module, a fire warning module, a fire response module, as well as a fire 
treatment module. The proposed system is capable of acquiring fire in -
formation, determining fire status, and making decisions for trapped 
people. However, existing research has mainly focused on the fire pro -
tection design and escape probability, whereas a series of closed-loop 
control logic of fire sensing, fire/smoke spread control, fire extinguish -
ing and evacuation security based on BIM has not been established. To 
achieve the above goals, BIM should be integrated with the fire pro -
tection system to achieve fire protection equipment management while 
enabling the fire protection system to respond promptly and rapidly in 
fire emergencies. 
In general, the rapid response of fire protection systems comprises 
early detection and reasonable reactions. The former provides more time 
for escaping and extinguishing the fire by using the detectability of the 
terminals (e.g., the very early smoke detection apparatus [34]). In this 
study, the reasonable reaction of the fire protection system is primarily 
analysed, with a stress on fast actions after a fire incident is detected (e. 
g., activating fire extinguishing terminals, opening smoke systems, and 
guiding escape). Compared with conventional manual operation, the 
emergence of IoT, a network of smart devices, enables automated con -
trol of sensing terminals by providing an environment where the devices 
can communicate with and sense each other. Nevertheless, existing 
research has placed a focus on monitoring [35,36] and sensing data 
management [2]. Since BIM provides a 3D geometry model and building 
design information, and IoT enables data connection, communication, 
and collection from devices, the integration of BIM and IoT has been 
applied to road compaction quality monitoring and management [37], 
automatic fault detection and building diagnostics [13], as well as 
thermal comfort building assessment [38]. However, there has been rare 
research on fire risk control, especially on data-driven decision-making 
about the control of fire protection systems. 
As a result, a methodology integrating BIM and IoT is proposed to 
develop an intelligent control strategy for BFP systems. The challenges 
originate from the realisation of the control mechanism and the inter -
operability between BIM data and IoT data [39]. Accordingly, a DT 
framework is built, and semantic web technologies are adopted to solve 
the above challenges.

The concept of the DT originated from the concept ideal for product 
lifecycle management proposed by Grieves in 2003. Subsequently, the 
definition of DT has been enriched in the application of the aerospace

industry [40], smart manufacturing [41], infrastructure [42–44], and 
facility management [13]. Although the understanding of DT varies with 
application scenarios, the connotation has come to a consensus that DT 
stresses a digital representation of real-world objects and the connection 
between virtual representation and physical objects. Furthermore, 
model construction and model interaction are involved from the 
modelling perspective. 
In the AEC domain, the DT model has been commonly constructed 
based on BIM. A light weighted BIM is employed to establish the DT-BIM 
hoisting planning model for prefabricated component hoisting man -
agement [45]. The model covers hoisting environment information (e. 
g., construction site layout), hoisting route information (e.g., hoisting 
point and installation point), and prefabricated component information 
(e.g., 3D shapes). At the city and building levels, BIM is considered as 
one of the digital models of physical city assets [46,47]. Since the In -
dustry Foundation Classes (IFC) format gives a standardised and digital 
description of BIM, Stojanovic et al. [48] converted the 3D cloud data 
into IFC data to support the DT representation. Lu and Brilakis [49] 
developed a bridge geometric DT, comprising a wide variety of IfcOb -
jects. Moreover, Lu et al. [13] adopted IFC as a centre data model of BIM 
and IoT data to achieve DT-based anomaly detection during daily 
building operation and maintenance (O&M). However, the above 
models are only concerned with the geometric information or operation 
information of the building objects. 
The mechanism of the operation process takes on a great significance 
in describing the model interaction, and the mechanism is formed as 
various models according to the application scenarios. During building 
O&M processes, the mechanism of intelligent control is formed as a 
behaviour model and a rule model [50]. The behaviour model can make 
response and feasibility analyses for the manual operation and system 
directives; it comprises diagnostic rules, prediction rules, and decision 
rules. In safety risk control during prefabricated building hoisting op -
erations, the safety risk mechanism was modelled as a Bayesian network 
to support decision-making [51]. 
A BFP system comprises several interoperating subsystems. Each of 
them implements a certain set of functions but also participates in the 
processes of handling fire emergencies. Process models are preferred to 
represent the procedures or workflows [52]. Accordingly, to express the 
system interoperating process and enable people to participate in the 
process, a process model is built based on the business process model 
and notation (BPMN) [53] to express the intelligent control mechanism 
of the fire protection system in this study. 
2.3. Semantic web technologies for data interoperability in AEC domain 
Data integration is recognized as a basic challenge of DT-based ap -
plications in practice. In the AEC domain, a plethora of data are obtained 
for a project due to the multiple phases involved in its lifecycle (e.g., 
design, construction, production, procurement, operation, and mainte -
nance [10]). The above data may have a wide variety of modes (e.g., 
recording data, videos, figures, and building models). On that basis, the 
interoperability of the above heterogeneous data becomes the dominant 
issue in data integration. With the emergence of BIM, some studies have 
suggested that IFC, an open standard data schema of BIM, lays a basis for 
the data integration of building information, sensor data, and 3D point 
cloud data [13,48,49]. However, the complex nature of the IFC schema 
limits its extensibility, modularity, and cross-domain linking ability 
beyond geometric representation [54]. 
Semantic web technologies provide a paradigm shift for data inte -
gration across domains. It provides a common structure to represent 
data and a set of tools to support the manipulation, sharing, reuse, 
retrieval, and integration of heterogeneous data [55]. Data are modelled 
as a directed, labelled graph based on the RDF. The respective pair of 
nodes and their relationship, namely, the RDF triple, establishes a link 
between information from disparate data sources. Based on the RDF 
graph, SPARQL can be employed for sematic and relational searching. 
Rule languages, (e.g., Semantic Web Rule Language (SWRL) [56] and 
Jena [57]) are used for semantic reasoning, which can infer and derive 
new knowledge from existing information to enrich the RDF graph. 
Furthermore, a set of predefined vocabularies are provided by the RDF 
and RDF Schema (RDFS) [58] to define the metadata schema, such that 
the data representation can be formalised. The above predefined vo -
cabularies can only describe basic relationships between data. Complex 
knowledge and relationships can be represented by the Web Ontology 
Language (OWL) [59], which is defined as an ontology that employs a 
broad set of vocabularies to express domain-specific entities and their 
relationships. 
There have been extensive studies of semantic web-based approaches 
of data interoperability in the AEC domain. Domain experts have 
developed a range of ontologies for specific domain information 
(Table 1). Since ifcOWL maintains the complex structure of the EXPRESS 
schema leading to low efficiency in practice, simplification based on 
ifcOWL can be an alternative to domain ontology construction [60,61]. 
The BOT ontology defined by the W3C linked building data (LBD) group 
is preferred for smart building DTs [54]. Based on reusing and inte -
grating the above ontologies, linked building data models have been 
built to manage cross-domain information in building automation and 
control [62], building energy performance assessment [63], as well as 
property management [64]. During the above processes, semantic 
reasoning could facilitate indirect and implicit relationship processing 
for multi-ontology integration [60,63,65]. 
Thus, in this study, a BFP ontology is developed as a semantic model 
that can serve as a bridge between BIM information and sensor data. In 
this way, the BIM information and the fire scene information are

3. Method 
3.1. Theoretical framework 
Fig. 1 illustrates the framework of the intelligent control of a BFP 
system. The physical space provides dynamic monitoring information to 
the virtual space in real-time. In the virtual space, five models (i.e., the 
geometric model, semantic model, DT data model, rule model, and 
process model) are established corresponding to the physical space. A 
deep integration of the above five models is formed as a DT to truly 
reflect the intelligent control process of a physical BFP system. 
The geometric model refers to a 3D model constructed based on BIM 
and describes static building data of the physical space (e.g., the 
building topology, shape parameters, and positional relations). The 
dynamic building data (e.g., the sensing data, device status, and fire 
scene information) are monitored by different IoT terminals. A BFP 
ontology is created as the semantic model, which can provide domain 
knowledge for how the above data are linked, to achieve the fusion of 
the above two types of data and form a linked-data diagram. The fused 
data model is called the DT data model and is represented as an RDF 
graph. 
Based on the DT data model, the intelligent control of the BFP system 
is achieved by a set of information-based control mechanisms that are 
accomplished by the rule model and process model. The rule model 
involves several diagnosis rules, process rules, as well as update rules. 
The process model specifies the control strategies containing several 
steps and is activated by diagnosis rules. For each step of the control 
strategy, the process rules are adopted to target the controlling objects 
based on the DT data model and produce control commands. The above 
feedbacks are executed in the physical space. Simultaneously, to keep 
the DT data model always a digital mirror of the physical space, the 
information in the DT data model (e.g., the device status changes based 
on the control strategy and real-time monitoring data), is updated ac -
cording to the update rules. Iteratively, the process model provides a 
subsequent step of the control strategies in accordance with the updated 
DT data model, and the control mechanism is achieved.

3.2. Building fire protection ontology 
The BFP ontology is proposed to support the modelling of three in -
formation requirements: the geometric topology of the building; the 
physical devices of the fire protection system and their location, affili -
ation and technical properties; and the operating information of the fire 
protection system. During the ontology development procedure, reusing 
existing ontologies is an important step and can promote the efficiency 
of development [ 67 ]. Accordingly, to meet the above information re -
quirements, the BFP ontology is constructed based on the modular as -
sembly and expansion of existing domain ontologies, such as the BOT 
ontology, brick ontology, SOSA ontology, and QUDT unit vocabulary. 
The BOT ontology contains the main topological concepts of buildings, 
and the above concepts are used to describe the geometric topology of 
the building in the BFP ontology. Brick ontology provides a standard 
sematic description of building assets and their relationships. Relational 
descriptions between devices in the brick ontology are mainly refer -
enced in this study. The SOSA ontology is adopted to express the oper -
ating information, including the observed values and time. The units of 
the observed values are specified using the QUDT unit vocabulary. The 
namespaces and related prefixes of the ontologies in this study are listed 
in Table 2 . In addition, the above ontologies lack descriptions related to 
the fire protection domain. The expansion is necessary, and the name -
spaces of the extensional concepts are defined as “ bfp ” for distinction. 
Accordingly, static building information and dynamic building infor -
mation can be integrated into an RDF graph (i.e., the DT data model) 
using the terminologies and relationships defined in the BFP ontology. 
The instances in the DT data model possess the prefix “ bfpi ” . 
The BFP Ontology contains four top classes at the highest level of 
abstraction: bfp:Construction , bfp:System , bfp:Device , and bfp:Operation , as 
presented in Fig. 2 . The bfp:Construction is the class of building entities, 
and its core hierarchy structure of subclasses is derived from the BOT 
ontology. Additionally, more subclasses and relations are specified as 
follows:  
• Two classes are defined as the subclasses of the bot:Space , i.e., bfp: 
EscapeSpace and bfp:GeneralSpace . The spaces adopted for guiding 
people to safety in a fire emergency are defined as individuals of the 
bfp:EscapeSpace , such as staircases and corridors. Other spaces are 
considered individuals of the bfp:GeneralSpace, which can also be 
subdivided into several classes in accordance with their functions (e. 
g., a reading room and washroom).  
• The bfp:BuiltElement is defined as a subclass of bot:Element and is 
decomposed based on two parallel criteria. The built elements are 
classified as doors, walls, windows, floors, and so forth in accordance 
with usual practice. Additionally, the built elements are classified as 
fire-resistant built elements and nonfire-resistant built elements 
based on their technical functions in fire protection. For example, 
bfp:FireDoor can be defined as a subclass of both bfp:Door and bfp: 
FireResistantBuiltElement . The correlation between a zone and a built 
element is defined as bfp:hasBuiltElement , which is a sub property of 
bot:hasElement .

respectively. The subclasses of bfp:System are extended in accordance 
with the concepts from brick ontology (e.g., a brick:FireSafetySystem , a 
brick:LightingSystem , and a bfp:CCTVSystem ). The brick:FireSatetySystem 
comprise several subclasses, including a bfp:FireSuppressionSystem that 
serves to suppress fire hazards, a bfp:FireDoorMonitoringSystem that 
controls the opening and closing of the fire door, a bfp:FireAlarmSystem 
that alerts people to fire emergencies, a bfp:SomkeControlSystem that 
regulates smoke, and so forth. The relationship indicating that a bot: 
Building possesses a bfp:System is defined as bfp:hasSystem . 
Instances of bfp:Device can be classified as equipment or sensors. The 
bfp:Equipment refers to the device that serves a building (e.g., the alarm 
bell and fire suppression terminal). The bfp:Sensor represents measuring 
devices, including fire detectors (e.g., smoke detectors, heat detectors, 
and flame detectors). Correlations of the devices are expressed mainly 
using the relationships defined in brick ontology. The location of a de -
vice is specified via brick:hasLocation . The sequential relations between 
devices are expressed through bfp:connect or brick:feeds . The former 
places a greater focus more on the connection, whereas the latter 
stresses the media passing between two devices. Besides, the relation 
brick:feeds can express the media passing from a room to a device. For 
instance, the relation between a smoke exhaust fan and a room is defined 
as “ (Room, brick:feeds , SmokeExhaustFan) ” , i.e., the smoke is discharged 
to the outside through the smoke exhaust fan. 
The bfp:Operation is an abstract class that reports the current building 
operating situation received from the IoT system, which comprises the 
working record of the devices (i.e., bfp:Record ), the status of the devices 
(i.e., bfp:Status ), and the observed results of the devices (i.e., bfp:Obser -
vation ), their observed objects (i.e., bfp: ObservedObject ), and so forth. An 
individual of the bfp:Observation represents an observed result for the 
devices. The relations bfp:hasObservation and bfp:hasObservedObject 
indicate the data source. Likewise, an individual of bfp:Record stores the 
working status via bfp:hasStatus . Moreover, relations defined in the 
SOSA ontology, sosa:resultTime and sosa:hasSimpleResult , can be adopted 
to specify the recording time and value. The units of the value are given 
by the QUDT unit vocabulary via qudt:unit . More details are presented in 
Section 3.3.2 . In this study, bfp:Status and bfp:ObservedObject are defined 
as enumerated classes with several frequently used values in the oper -
ation. Some of the values are listed in Table 3 . 
3.3. Data fusion 
3.3.1. Information extraction from IFC file 
The proposed BFP ontology is considered as the backbone of the DT 
data model. It defines the way in which the static building information 
and dynamic monitoring information are linked and fused. As 
mentioned above, the static building information is presented by the 
geometry model (i.e., BIM model). In practice, the BIM model is created 
based on different BIM authoring software applications, such that 
various BIM data formats may be produced. IFC refers to an open data 
exchange model for BIM application. Since IFC is widely accepted and 
supported by most BIM authoring software applications, the IFC file 
serves as the data source for the static building information. Thus, 
establishing static building information in the DT data model is depen -
dent on information extraction from the IFC file. The extracted IFC en -
tities and their characteristics are defined as instances with attributes in 
the DT data model in accordance with the mappings between the IFC 
schema and the BFP ontology. Moreover, the correlations between the 
IFC entities can be obtained and then defined in the DT data model to 
generate a linked data graph. In this study, the IFC4 schema is selected 
for analysis. The information extracted from IFC files mainly involves 
the description of building topology, the location and aggregation of 
devices, and the creation of fire compartments. An open-source toolkit, 
IfcOpenShell [ 68 ], is employed for IFC schema parsing. 
To describe the building topology in the DT data model, the IFC 
entities are first defined as the individuals of corresponding classes in 
accordance with the mapped terminologies between the IFC schema and 
BFP ontology ( Table 4 ). To specify the subclass to which the IFC entity 
belongs, predefined properties are required to provide mapping re -
strictions. For instance, only the instances of IfcDoor with a fire rating 
definition are defined as the individuals of bfp:FireDoor ( Fig. 3 ). The 
predefined fire rating property is stored as an IfcPropertySingleValue 
(#416615), which is connected to IfcDoor entity (#416612) via IfcPro -
pertySet entity (#416616) and IfcRelDefinesByProperties entity 
(#416621). 
Next, the correlations between individuals can be defined in accor -
dance with their related IfcRel-entities ( Table 5 ). SPARQL queries can be 
employed to supplement the relations. For example, the query “ SELECT 
?a ?b WHERE{?a rdf:type bfp:SmokeExhaustOutlet. ?b rdf:type bfp: 
SmokeExhaustFan. ?a bfp:connect + ?b .} ” can be adopted to retrieve 
several pairs of smoke exhaust fans and linking outlets. Subsequently, 
the brick:feeds relation is created, starting from the smoke exhaust outlet 
to the connected smoke exhaust fan. 
The device locations (i.e., brick:hasLocation ) are created by geometric 
processing of the IFC file. Since IfcOpenShell can convert the implicit 
geometry in IFC files into explicit geometry, the Boolean intersection 
operation is executed for the respective pair of devices and spaces. If the 
interaction set is not empty, the space is defined as the location of the 
device. In addition, semantic reasoning can be adopted to obtain the 
aggregation relationships between the system and devices. For instance, 
the instances of bfp:FireAlarmSystem refer to the systems with several fire 
alarm devices and thus can be modelled by the reasoning in accordance 
with the semantic rule “ ( ?a bfp:hasDevice ?b )( ?b rdf:type bfp:Fire -
AlarmDevice )- > ( ?a rdf:type bfp:FireAlarmSystem ) ” .

Since the IFC lacks a description of some domain-specific concepts, 
the information of the fire compartment cannot be directly acquired 
from IFC. As a result, starting from the definition of the fire compart -
ment that represents a limited range of areas separated by fireproofing 
constructions, the disjoint-set union-finding algorithm is used to create 
fire compartments, as presented in Fig. 4 . Two relations (i.e., bfp:fire -
IsolatedTo and bfp:fireConnectedTo ) are created between spaces accord -
ing to their splitting built elements first. For example, the relation 
between Space_1 and Space_2 is expressed as bfp:fireConnectedTo for 
their relating non-fire door (i.e., Door_3), while the relation between 
Space_1 and Space_3 is expressed as bfp:fireIsolatedTo since they are split 
by a fire door (i.e., Door_2). Next, a set of connected graphs is obtained 
based on the above two relations. The nodes of the graphs represent the 
spaces, and the edges are created based on the bfp:fireConnectedTo re -
lations. Notably, those connected spaces belong to the same fire 
compartment. As a result, the creation of fire compartments is for -
malised as a union-finding problem. In this way, the spaces are organ -
ised as a disjoint-set data structure, and the respective disjoint-set is 
defined as an instance of bfp:fireCompartment . The spaces are connected 
to the fire compartment via bot:hasSpace . 
Finally, the completeness and correctness of the DT data model are 
important basics of the implementation of the use of the two technolo -
gies for the intelligent control of BFP systems in fire accidents. A set of 
verification rules are developed based on SPARQL to ensure an accurate 
transformation from the IFC file to the DT data model. The verification 
rules can be divided into three types, and some examples are listed in 
Table 6 . First, the SPARQL query in SELECT form with COUNT clauses is 
employed for a consistency check between the IFC file and DT data 
model. For instance, the verification rule VR_1 is used to obtain the 
number of spaces in the DT data model, which is then compared with the 
number of spaces in the IFC file. Second, the classification check rules, 
which are used to ensure accurate classification of individuals according 
to the BFP ontology, are developed in ASK form and result in Boolean 
values (i.e., “ True ” or “ False ” , which means the DT data model passes or 
fails in the verification process, respectively). For instance, the verifi -
cation rule VR_2 is adopted to check whether the classification of spaces 
is correct. According to the BFP ontology, the number of individuals of 
bot:Space (i.e., the variable ?all in VR_2) should be equal to the sum of 
those of bfp:EscapeSpace and bfp:GeneralSpace (i.e., the variable ?s1 and ? 
s2 in VR_2). The comparison is achieved by the HAVING clause. The 
third type of verification rule is also developed in ASK form and is used 
to ensure accurate compositional relationships between individuals. For 
example, a CCTV system is composed of at least one camera, and this 
compositional relationship is verified using the verification rule VR_3 
with the FILTER NOT EXISTS clause.

In the DT data model, the information collected from IoT devices is 
described as individuals of bfp:Observation or bfp:Record . The observed

3.4. Information-based control mechanism 
The control mechanism of the DT framework is established based on 
the rule model and the process model, as presented in Fig. 6 . The rule 
model contains a set of diagnosis rules, process rules, and update rules. 
The diagnosis rules are executed on the time slice of the DT data model 
at set intervals to check if a fire is detected. Time slices of the DT data 
model can be obtained by SPARQL queries. For instance, the SPARQL 
query in Fig. 6 is employed to construct a sub data model from 13:38:00 
to 13:38:10. At each time interval, a diagnosis rule is executed to check 
for the fire incidents. If none is found, the diagnosis rule is executed on 
the DT data model of the subsequent time interval. Otherwise, the 
process model is activated. Process rules are adopted to complete the 
tasks within the process model. Meanwhile, update rules are employed 
to update the status of entities and the information collected from IoT 
devices into the DT data model. 
The process model is developed based on BPMN and executed using 
the Camunda Platform as a workflow engine. A typical process is 
modelled as presented in Fig. 7 . As mentioned before, the intelligent 
control strategy consists of several subtasks that are completed based on 
the process rules; model updates are implemented based on update 
rules. The above two processes constitute a single cycle. A judgement 
condition is also set to determine whether the loop is terminated, which 
can be either an automatic judgement criterion or a manual judgement

4.3.2. Intelligent control of fire doors 
Smoke is another dangerous lethal factor for a fire accident, espe -
cially during evacuation. One of the functions of fire doors is to stop the 
fire and smoke spreading from one fire compartment to another. How -
ever, a fire door related to the staircase also serves for evacuation. The 
time to turn off fire doors that can enable people to escape and prevent 
broadening the spread of smoke should be determined. 
The floor plans of the library are illustrated in Fig. 15 . In this study, 
two strategies were considered in controlling fire doors. First, the fire 
doors located on the first floor were turned off immediately, and people 
on the first floor were guided to escape through the two main entrances. 
Second, the fire doors located on the other floors were turned off after all 
the people escaped from this floor. The camera was beneficial to capture 
the evacuation progress. 
The process model established for this scenario is shown in Fig. 16 . 
The rule R1, as introduced in Table 8 , was also considered as the diag -
nosis rule in this scenario. The process rules and update rules are listed 
in Table 9 . The SPARL query outlined as R4 was employed to obtain the 
fire doors located on the first floor. The above fire doors were closed as 
soon as the smoke detector was alarming. Next, a process rule, R5, can 
be adopted to examine the number of existing people on the respective 
floor except for the first floor. A VALUE restriction specifies which floor 
was examined. The calculation was performed based on the cameras ’ 
observation. The filter restriction “ FILTER NOT EXISTS {?space rdf:type 
bfp:Staircase.} ” indicated that the number of people in the staircase was 
not included in the calculation. The sequential restriction “ ORDER BY 
DESC(?time) LIMIT 1 ” ensured that the query results of the inner SELECT 
clauses were sorted by the reversed order of examining time, and only 
the first result was considered. Accordingly, the examination was per -
formed on the latest observations. If the result of R5 is “ true ” , the process 
rule, R6, can be used to retrieve the corresponding fire doors. The 
operation signals were transmitted to the above fire doors, and their 
status was updated in the DT data model using update rule R9 simul -
taneously. Afterwards, the process model checked whether all the fire 
doors were closed based on the process rules R7 and R8. The process rule 
R7 was used to obtain all the fire doors connecting the staircase and 
corridor, and the process rule R8 was adopted to check the status of all 
fire doors. If there are still fire doors that are not closed, the camera 
monitoring data can be updated into the DT data model by the update 
rule R10. The process model executed the above tasks iteratively till all 
the fire doors were closed. 
In this scenario, a room located on the second floor was selected as 
the fire origination to verify the proposed process model. The smoke 
detector alarmed at 13:38:27, and the closing time of the fire doors is 
presented in Fig. 17 . Another strategy in which the fire doors remained 
open to ensure evacuation was also simulated for comparison. The 
comparison results represented in Fig. 18 suggested that the intelligent 
control mechanism can keep most smoke outside the staircases and 
ensure evacuation simultaneously. 
4.3.3. Intelligent control of smoke control systems 
The smoke control system also plays a critical role in preventing 
damage caused by smoke. In accordance with the Code for Design of 
Automatic Fire Alarm System in China, the smoke control system is 
required to start up in the fire compartment where there are two inde -
pendently alarming smoke detectors. An earlier start-up time can 
improve the performance of smoke control [ 71 ]. However, the start-up 
time of the smoke control system heavily relies on manual activation, 
including estimating the fire location and confirming the present fire 
compartment. In some cases, people should find the corresponding 
smoke exhaust fan and turn on it by hand, which is time-consuming. 
In this study, a process model ( Fig. 19 ) and a set of rules ( Table. 10 ) 
are proposed to achieve the intelligent control mechanism of the smoke 
control system. First, the diagnosis rule, R11, was employed to check 
whether there were two alarming smoke detectors for the respective fire 
compartment. The process model was activated when the query result of 
rule R11 was “ true ” . Subsequently, two strategies were considered in the 
process model. First, the exhaust fans of the fire compartment where 
there were two independent alarming smoke detectors started. The 
process rule, R12, was adopted to find the corresponding outlets and 
fans. The inner SELECT clauses aim at limiting the variable ?fc as the 
target fire compartment. Second, the air supply fan of the staircase 
through which people were escaping became open. For each staircase, 
the process rule R13 was used to examine the number of existing people. 
If the query result of R13 is “ true ” , the process rule, R14, is used to obtain 
the corresponding devices. For both strategies, the opening signals were 
transmitted to the devices, and the model was updated based on the 
update rule R16 simultaneously. Next, rule R15 was performed for the 
respective device of the air supply system to check if it was working 
sufficiently. If not, the DT data model was updated according to R10, as 
introduced in Table 9 , and the process model executed the next iteration.

5. Discussion 
DTs have been considered a comprehensive solution for improving 
the efficiency of building automation systems. The core of DTs is the DT 
model, which enables organic association of multiple pieces of infor -
mation and supports virtual-real interaction and optimal control on this 
basis. Focusing on data integration and system cooperation of the 
intelligent fire protection system, this study proposes a DT-based 
approach of the intelligent control of building automation systems. 
From the perspective of information flow, the use of the two technolo -
gies for the intelligent control of BFP systems in fire accidents contains a 
data fusion stage and several information-based control mechanisms. 
Data fusion aims to use a unified data model to organise and store the 
information from various data sources. The information-based control 
mechanisms serve as the interaction between the real and the virtual. 
From the perspective of modelling, the use of the two technologies 
consists of five basic models, namely, the geometric model, semantic 
model, DT data model, rule model, and process model. BIM models are

Compared with the existing DT framework, this study makes two 
main contributions. First, the BFP ontology is designed as the semantic 
model to address the data interoperability between geometric data (e.g., 
the building model data) and real-time data (e.g., the monitoring data 
collected by IoT sensors). The BFP ontology is developed according to 
the idea of ontology reuse and modularity. The concepts and relations in 
the BFP ontology are developed in accordance with the baseline of the 
application to more effectively describe the building ’ s geometric, posi -
tional, and operating information, as well as the BFP system. Based on 
the BFP ontology, heterogeneous data and various sources of informa -
tion can be integrated into a DT data model to support retrieval, reuse, 
and decision-making. In addition, three types of verification rules, 
namely, consistency check rules, classification check rules, and 
composition check rules, are developed to ensure an accurate mapping 
from the BIM model to the DT data model.

proposed to represent the control mechanism, such that different sub -

The limitations of this study are summarised as follows. First, the 
proposed approach is only verified by simulation; thus, on-site experi -
ments need to be implemented. Second, this study focuses on the data 
integration and information-driven intelligent control of a BFP system, 
including an assumption that the data of the IoT terminals can be fully 
obtained and well-processed. For instance, the idea of the acquisition of 
the number of people in a room was inspired by [ 73 ], which used deep 
learning and thermal cameras for human detection in heavy thermal 
scenarios. This idea also requires further on-site validation and 
improvement. Third, only three basic fire protection scenarios are 
investigated. The application of the proposed DT-based framework to 
achieve the complicated control mechanism of the BFP system should be 
studied in depth. 
Future work can focus on developing a platform unifying the hard -
ware and software to support the implementation and validation of the 
proposed DT framework for the intelligent control of BFP systems. In 
addition, the application scope of this study can be promoted from two 
aspects. First, because the development of the sematic model, process 
model, and rule model relies on manual work, domain experts can 
improve the above models to achieve complicated control mechanisms 
for more fire protection scenarios. Furthermore, it is advised to apply 
and extend the proposed DT framework to other system control tasks, 
such as the active control of seismic responses. 
6. Conclusions 
This paper describes an intelligent control of BFP systems based on 
DT and semantic web technologies. In general, the implementation of 
DT faces the issues of cross-domain collaboration and multi-mode and 
multi-source data interoperability. This study has shown that semantic 
web technologies, especially the ontology engineering and linked data 
approaches, can be utilized for the fusion of domain knowledge, BIM 
information, and IoT sensing data. The integrated data is formed as a DT 
data model and semantic web technologies can also support efficient 
information acquisition, extraction, verification, and update. The above 
findings may be considered as a solid foundation of the implementation 
of DT. 
The second major finding is that a digital thread is necessary for 
information-driven prediction, logical deduction, and intelligent deci -
sion making in DT implementation. The digital thread can be achieved 
based on the DT data model, a rule model and a process model. The 
process model, which is executed on a workflow engine, reflects intel -
ligent control mechanisms of BFP systems. Human-machine collabora -
tion is also taken into consideration in the process model to improve the 
robustness of DT implementation. The rule model is conducted to ach -
ieve the activation of the process model and the interaction between the 
virtual space and the physical space. 
Moreover, simulation experiments demonstrate that the proposed 
intelligent control approach can make rapid response to fire incidents 
and facilitate human evacuation. To the best of the author ’ s knowledge, 
this work is the first attempt to explore the potential and feasibility of 
integrating DT and semantic web technologies in the intelligent control 
of BFP systems.
