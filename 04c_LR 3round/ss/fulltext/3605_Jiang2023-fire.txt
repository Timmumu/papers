ABSTRACT
Virtual modeling of building systems presents significant challenges due to the prolonged oper-
ation under dynamic conditions. This study proposes a novel, nonintrusive in-situ modeling
method to overcome the practical measurement difficulties while providing reliable long-term
operational data. Previous research has achieved high-accuracy virtual models; however, their
applicability was often limited to specific time periods or singular operating conditions. Building
on these efforts, this study establishes an integrated framework that incorporates multiple virtual
models developed through a sequence of prediction, benchmarking, and correction processes. To
optimize model performance, data-driven and physics-based modeling approaches were
employed for comparison. Field studies were conducted on a real heating, ventilation, and air
conditioning system (HVAC), and long-term modeling performance was validated over a one-year
period. The physics-based corrected model demonstrated superior performance, with the annual
root mean squared error (RMSE) value significantly reduced from 0.592 m³/h to 0.034 m³/h, and
the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) decreased by 26.7 %. In summary, physics-based
nonintrusive in-situ models offer excellent long-term applicability and facilitate optimal control
and holistic monitoring of building system operations.

1. Introduction
The global warming issues caused by the overproduction of greenhouse gases from power generation have become increasingly
serious. With the rise in national living standards, the application of HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) systems in the
building sector has rapidly spread [ 1 ]. Due to the extensive use of operations, HVAC systems consume approximately 50 % of the total
building energy to maintain the required indoor environment [ 2 ]. Additionally, unreasonable control sequences [ 3 ], inefficient set-
points [ 4 , 5 ], and degradation of aging equipment [ 6 , 7 ] have led to various malfunctions [ 8 , 9 ], further contributing to increased
energy consumption and the deterioration of indoor environment quality [ 10 , 11 ].
Building digitalization technology has been introduced to improve building performance and enhance operational efficiency. Building
digitalization can be achieved through the concepts of Building Information Modeling (BIM), cyber-physical systems, and digital twins
(DTs) [ 12 , 13 ]. These concepts leverage advanced sensing technologies, data-driven analytics, and machine learning to enable intelligent
operational applications, thereby actively improving the overall performance of building systems [ 14 ]. In recent years, the concept of BIM
and digital twins has been widely adopted and applied in the building sector. Petri et al. [ 15 ] proposed a digital twin-based adaptable
architecture for resilient infrastructure, focusing on energy efficiency, sustainability, and advanced monitoring through wireless sensors in
industrial and construction applications. Jiang et al. [ 16 ] explored the use of digital twins and semantic web technologies for intelligent
control of building fire protection systems in fire accidents, integrating BIM and IoT data for enhanced virtual-real interaction. Spudys et al.
[ 17 ] addressed deficiencies in operational building assessment practices by proposing the use of digital twin technology, BIM files, and
smart sensors to connect physical and virtual data, enabling real-time energy performance assessment and advancing digital construction
practices with a focus on energy efficiency indicators. Targeting building HVAC systems, Xie et al. [ 18 ] presented a digital twin-based
approach using symbolic AI to detect HVAC faults by identifying key sensory data, labeling sensors with machine-readable fault tags,
and automating real-time fault annotation and data filtering for dynamic asset management. Hosamo et al. [ 19 ] proposed the Digital Twin
of the HVAC system method, integrating BIM, Simulink, and multi-objective genetic algorithm to optimize HVAC systems by processing
IoT data, validating models, and identifying optimal energy and thermal comfort solutions, with results streamed back to BIM for
implementation. Digital twins aim to accurately reflect physical objects in a digital form, span their life cycle, and use real-time data to
analyze behaviors and operations [ 20 ]. The original digital twin model comprises three main elements: (1) the physical entity, (2) the
virtual model, and (3) their real-time interactions for continuous data and information flow [ 21 ]. To create a comprehensive digital twin
environment, virtual space should integrate various types of models, such as geometry, processes, physics, behaviors, and rules for building
operations. By employing assembly, integration, and fusion techniques, the virtual model can describe and depict physical entities from
multi-dimensional, multi-domain, and multi-scale perspectives. Such comprehensive virtual models are essential for digital twin appli-
cations and services, including virtual sensing [ 22 ], simulation [ 23 ], diagnosis [ 24 ], prediction [ 25 ], and optimization [ 26 ].
Focusing on the unique inherent and operational characteristics of building, Yoon [ 27 ] introduced the concept of Virtual Building
Models (VBM), defined as mathematical models that simulate the physical behavior of operational buildings. Unlike other models within
BIM and DT frameworks, which primarily focus on geometry and rules, VBMs emphasize mathematical, physical, and dynamic behavior
models. These VBMs are composed of sub-virtual models (observed, unobserved, and designed), ranging from the initial designed model
to fully detailed in-situ operational representations. With the objective of providing deeper insights into the behavior and operational
status of real buildings, surpassing the limitations of textual, geometrical, and static information, VBMs must rely on in-situ sensor
measurements during the modeling process. Therefore, sufficient sensors, datasets, and building domain knowledge are crucial to
establishing a virtual model that accurately reflects real building operations. However, in real-world building systems, practical issues
such as sensor absence, malfunctions, low redundancy, and sparse sensor deployment often limit the sensing environment [ 28 ]. As a
result, a new modeling approach is essential to meet the conditions necessary for digital twin-enabled intelligent operations.
The concept of in-situ modeling methodologies [ 29 ] has been developed to establish a mathematical modeling process for building

behavioral models during real-time operations. In-situ modeling involves constructing mathematical behavioral models directly
within the target building operations, aiming to produce more accurate models than those developed outside the target building
environment (e.g., in different buildings, modeling environments, or time periods) [ 29 ]. This methodology accounts for the long
operational life cycle of buildings, their large scale, on-site dynamic behaviors, and the challenges of replicating a laboratory envi-
ronment for mathematical modeling. The in-situ modeling approach addresses uncertainties in physics-based models during their
operational phase by applying mathematical correction techniques. Additionally, it incorporates sensor data to facilitate data-driven
modeling, which is crucial for accurately representing real building operations. In this context, in situ modeling encompasses two main
approaches: (1) intrusive in-situ modeling and (2) nonintrusive in-situ modeling. Intrusive in-situ modeling involves constructing
virtual models, particularly for target models that are unobserved by sensors during building operations, by mathematically incor-
porating additional data obtained through intrusive efforts, such as measurements collected using specialized instruments operated by
personnel within the building. Compared to the nonintrusive in-situ modeling approach, which relies solely on operational data
collected by building management and automation systems (BAS), Intrusive modeling is relatively straightforward and accessible for
non-professionals to understand and apply. However, conducting intrusive measurements during real operations is often challenging
due to practical installation constraints and high external costs. Additionally, temporary sensors may introduce measurement errors,
thereby increasing overall modeling uncertainties. Consequently, the key research focus is the development and validation of unob-
served virtual models through non-intrusive approaches, thereby eliminating the reliance on additional intrusive measurements.
In the domain of building in-situ modeling, Wang et al. [ 30 ] employed an intrusive method to develop a principal components
analysis model for the target sensor using early-stage accurate data. This approach enabled the detection and diagnosis of faults in
temperature and pressure sensors. Xu et al. [ 31 ] diagnosed faults in temperature sensors and flow rate meters with varying amplitudes,
using intrusive data to construct the sensor model. This method demonstrated improved performance in fault detection rate, diagnosis
rate, and estimation accuracy. Yu et al. [ 32 ] applied an intrusive in-situ modeling technique for calibrating supply air temperature
sensors, achieving a measurement uncertainty of 0.7

While data-driven corrected models have achieved reasonable accuracy, their performance remains highly sensitive to the hyper-
parameters, and input features used during the modeling process. In this context, physics-based modeling presents significant potential
for offering more robust and reliable performance, effectively addressing these limitations. Existing studies have demonstrated virtual
modeling accuracy over limited operation periods (e.g., a few weeks, months, or a single season), however, as these models are
functioned as the virtual equivalents of physical sensors without corresponding hardware, long-term reliability is crucial for the
deployment of virtual models in real building operations. Furthermore, the developed modeling process must undergo rigorous
evaluation to support the implementation of virtual models within digital twin environments.
Accordingly, this study aims to propose a nonintrusive in-situ modeling framework for unobserved virtual models in digital twin-
enabled building HVAC systems, thereby bridging the gap between virtual modeling and digital twin environments. To enhance exist-
ing methodologies, a physics-based benchmark modeling approach is newly adopted into the nonintrusive modeling process to address the
identified limitations in current research. this study aims to propose a nonintrusive in-situ modeling framework for. Case studies were
conducted in a living laboratory for building HVAC systems, where data-driven and physics-based virtual models for chilled water flow
rates were developed, refined, and validated over a one-year period. During the modeling process, BIM data played a pivotal role in
establishing the prediction model as a robust foundation and providing reliable information for the development of benchmark models.
The findings of this study have the potential to facilitate broader applications of virtual models, extending their use beyond traditional
monitoring to encompass roles such as control feedback sensors or input sensors for intelligent applications in digital twin-enabled building
operations. The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 outlines the overall framework and the three development steps for each
modeling approach. Section 3 details the field application used for the comparative study. Section 4 presents the application results and
discusses the model ’ s effectiveness and potential usability for real-world applications. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper.
2. Methodology
2.1. Overall framework
As shown in Fig. 1 , the modeling process using the proposed nonintrusive in-situ virtual modeling method consists of three key steps: 1)
initial model development, 2) benchmark model development, and 3) correction model development. In the absence of physical obser-
vations, the target variable is initially predicted using a white-box modeling approach based on physics and design information. A rela-
tional variable R , is introduced for benchmark and nonintrusive correction modeling processes. Benchmark models are developed to define
R , and are constructed using data-driven or physics-based approaches, with the initial model serving as a mandatory input. The resulting
benchmarks are compared to corresponding measurements of R , enabling the formulation of distance functions that indirectly measure the
accuracy of the initial model. Finally, correction models are built based on these formulated distance functions within the nonintrusive
calibration method, facilitating the determination of correction parameters. Notably, target measurements obtained from real sensors are
not used throughout the modeling process. Instead, measurements from building management and automation system (BAS) (e.g., physical
sensors, control signals), design information (e.g., equipment specifications, test reports), and system operation knowledge (i.e., physics)
are essential references and inputs. Detailed explanations of each step are provided in the following subsections.
2.2. Development of initial prediction model
As shown in Fig. 1 (a), the initial model is constructed in the first step to predict the unknown target y. In the absence of physical
measurements, the target variable is defined using a white-box modeling approach that leverages BIM data, including building design
information and equipment specifications, as input parameters. Standard value calculation sheets, along with mechanical, electrical,
and plumbing plans, serve as critical references for the initial modeling process. The BIM data is extracted and derived based on the
target variable and integrated into the corresponding operational physics equations. Rather than emphasizing prediction accuracy, the
primary objective of the initial modeling is to capture the operational patterns of the target variable, thereby improving the perfor-
mance of subsequent correction models. In this context, the equipment-level function ( f

2.3. Development of benchmark model
In the second step, data-driven and physics-based benchmark models are developed, as shown in Fig. 1 (b). A relational variable R is
introduced and employed as the modeling target. The objective of benchmark modeling is to define R in terms of initial model output,
thus enabling the formulation of the indirect distance function required in Step 3. Among the existing variables in the target system, R
is expected to exhibit the strongest correlations with the target variable. Due to the differing development concepts of data-driven and
physics-based models, R is selected separately based on either intended physical relationships or data similarity analyses. The

performance of the developed benchmark model is crucial to the effectiveness of the virtual modeling and correction processes.
Modeling accuracy is expected to improve as the corrected initial model output aligns more closely with the ground truth.
The data-driven (black-box) benchmark model is established using regression techniques, such as neural network-based machine
learning algorithms, as shown in Fig. 1 (b-1). The model is developed through training and testing processes to identify underlying
patterns between inputs and outputs. Input variables are selected based on data similarities and prior experience [ 34 ], while the output
is the measurement of the relational variable. The training results depend on the quality and quantity of the input datasets, the
designed features and complexity of the model. Inaccurate or insufficient data can lead to modeling errors, and overfitting problems
may occur due to repeated training. To mitigate these issues, the testing process is conducted to evaluate the trained model using
additional datasets. The model outputs are compared against the provided data to identify significant discrepancies or poor gener-
alization, thus ensure modeling accuracy [ 40 ].
The novel physics-based benchmark model is developed based on comprehensive building physics, as depicted in Figs. 1 (b-2). A
system-level function, such as energy and mass balance equations, that describes the physical relationships among multiple system
variables, is employed as the governing equation. However, uncertainties inevitably arise in the system function due to theoretical and
practical assumptions. To enhance the reliability of the physics-based benchmark model, an unknown parameter ( u ) is designed to
represent overall operational uncertainties and is incorporated into the formulation as part of feature extraction, as shown in Eq. (2) .
This unknown parameter is initially defined based on research related to similar buildings or operating systems and is corrected
simultaneously with the initial model in Step 3. Rather than directly correcting the initial model output, the use of u serves as an
auxiliary approach to improve the performance of the physics-based virtual models during the correction processes.

3.3. Benchmark model design
The data-driven benchmark model was developed using a multi-layer neural network, with the initial model output and related
system variables as inputs. Pump power ( P ) was selected as the relational variable based on data similarity analyses, as suggested by
Choi et al. [ 43 ]. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) was trained using operational data of June 2022. To enhance modeling efficiency, data
preprocessing techniques such as simple moving average, z-score outlier removal, and min-max normalization were applied. The MLP
was structured with four hidden layers and activation functions. The Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) was applied to the first three hidden
layers, while a linear function was used for the output layer. Random shuffling and early stopping rules were implemented to mitigate
overfitting issues [ 36 ]. Detailed information about the MLP data preprocessing, structure and hyperparameter settings is provided in
Table 3 .

4.1. Performance of initial prediction model
The comparison between the initial model outputs and the ground truth, along with RMSE calculations, is presented in Fig. 4 . G
eva
was generally overestimated, with notably high seasonal RMSE values throughout the year. The primary and common reason for these
significant uncertainties is the omission of heat loss and lack of consideration for actual building conditions. In real operations, heat
loss through water flow is unavoidable, and system configurations are often adjusted to meet specific construction and usage re-
quirements. The seasonal RMSE values were calculated as 0.607 m³/h (summer), 0.560 m³/h (winter), and 0.609 m³/h (transition),
indicating minimal differences across the seasons. However, substantial differences were observed in the distribution patterns of the
data. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the model exhibited poor performance during the summer season, characterized by the widest data
distribution range. In contrast, the distribution range narrowed in the winter season. Notably, during the transition season, a unique
pattern emerged, where the majority of data points were concentrated in a specific region.
The observed performance differences can be attributed to unstable operating conditions. The valve opening rate fluctuated
frequently between 30 % and 100 % in response to the comparison of the supply-air temperature with its setpoint. These variations
directly resulted in significant changes in G
eva
, thereby increasing modeling complexity and reducing estimation accuracy. As depicted
in Fig. 4 (b), the system exhibited the highest valve adjustment rates in summer, leading to the most unstable operational status.
Conversely, during the winter season, the system operated more stably with reduced variations and uncertainties. During the transition
season, when neither heating nor cooling was required, the system was turned off, and the pump operated independently. Under these
conditions, the valve remained fully open without fluctuations, resulting in consistent system operation throughout the season.
The inconsistent seasonal performance of the initial model underscores the challenges of achieving long-term reliability using a
single model. These findings highlight the necessity of implementing lateral, non-intrusive correction processes to improve model
performance and adaptability under varying operating conditions.
4.2. Performances of data-driven benchmark and corrected models
The training and testing results of the data-driven benchmark model were compared, as shown in Fig. 5 . As shown in Fig. 5 (a),
significant errors were observed at the beginning of the training period, particularly with negative values occurring between 500 and
1000 episodes. These errors can be attributed to the nature of the data-driven modeling approach, which lacks consideration of
physical principles. Consequently, the model may produce unrealistic outputs that deviate from real-world behavior, especially under
extreme conditions. Better performance was observed during the testing period, with RMSE values decreased from 0.43 W to 0.37 W,
indicating the importance of the training process on data-driven modeling accuracy. However, despite these overall improvements,

significant errors were still present at the upper and lower extremes of the data range, as depicted in Fig. 5 (b). This issue arises from an
inherent limitation of data-driven approaches, where the model ’ s output is highly dependent on the data distribution in the training
dataset. When the model is trained with limited extreme data, predictions tend to converge towards the majority range (i.e., between
1.5 and 2.5 W), leading to overestimations at lower power values and underestimations at higher power values.
The long-term validation results of the data-driven corrected model are presented in Fig. 6 . The RMSE and MAPE values were
significantly reduced from 0.59 m³/h to less than 0.11 m³/h and from 30 % to within 4.2 %, respectively, demonstrating substantial
improvement over the entire validation period. However, notable seasonal inconsistencies were observed. After training and testing
process, the data-driven model performed best during the summer season, achieving the lowest MAPE of 2.15 %, while performance
declined during the winter and transition seasons, with MAPE peaking at 4.17 %. These seasonal declines highlight the limitation of
data-driven correction models, which rely on training patterns rather than physical interactions, making them unable to self-adjust to
operational variations. Consequently, additional training and correction processes are necessary for winter and transition seasons to
maintain long-term modeling accuracy.

4.5. Implementation of nonintrusive modeling using Dynamo
The proposed virtual models were visualized in Revit as part of a digital twin environment, utilizing a workflow implemented
through the Dynamo interface, as illustrated in Fig. 13 . Within the Dynamo, the modeling framework, including design logic and
interactions, was organized into five components: 1) Importing sensor information. According to the requirements of the virtual model
construction, data and information from BAS system are imported in real-time into the Dynamo interface. 2) Loading the prediction
model. Python scripts for various needs are used in the Dynamo interface to construct the prediction model. First, the real-time im-
ported data is preprocessed to remove outliers and singularities, and then the processed data is imported into the model construction
script to obtain the output of the prediction model. 3) Constructing benchmark models. Different Python scripts are used to construct
physics-driven and data-driven benchmark models as needed for this study. During the benchmark model construction process,
outputs from the prediction model are used as inputs to achieve nonintrusive calibration. Additionally, preprocessing of other input

5. Conclusion
Long-term virtual models have great importance for providing insightful data on unknown variables in dynamic, sensor-limited
environments. This study presented a nonintrusive in-situ modeling method with broad applicability to real HVAC systems. The
modeling process consists of three key steps of prediction, benchmarking and correction. In the absence of target physical observations,
the relational variable R is introduced to formulate indirect distance functions and enable nonintrusive corrections. Using the proposed
physics-based approach, benchmark model performance is enhanced by incorporating the unknown parameter u. The annual RMSE of
the prediction model was reduced from 0.592 m³/h to 0.034 m³/h, and the MAPE decreased from 28.03 % to 1.32 % through a single
correction process. Long-term modeling efficiency was significantly improved while minimizing costs. The strong modeling capability
for correcting u was validated through analyses, and the modeling processes were visualized in the digital twin-enabled environment
using Dynamo.
Thus, for the target building or HVAC systems, the developed physics-based virtual models can be applied as virtual sensors to
provide unobserved data, enabling holistic monitoring and advanced applications for optimal operational control. Meanwhile, the
data-driven method, with its capability to model using limited inputs, can serve as a supportive approach to estimate missing or faulty
input values, thereby improving the performance of the physics-based virtual models.
In this research, a long-term reliable physics-based nonintrusive in-situ virtual model was developed through a three-step manual
process. For future research, it is recommended to investigate advanced methodologies aimed at automating the modeling procedures.
This can be achieved through the integration of cloud platforms and advanced data communication technologies, which would
significantly enhance the efficiency, scalability, and applicability of the proposed virtual modeling methods. Such advancements
would enable their deployment in more sophisticated intelligent building applications, extending their use across a wider range of
systems and promoting the development of next-generation smart environments.
