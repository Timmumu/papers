ABSTRACT
Digital twin (DT) provides effective pathways to solve issues in the construction industry, particu-
larly smart construction and carbon emissions in prefabrication. Past DT research explored facil-
ity management and fault detection, highlighting a knowledge gap on the use of DT for smart
construction and emissions monitoring in prefabrication supply chain (PSC). Therefore, the aim
of this study is to present a holistic view of DT applications in PSC by exploring real-time smart
construction and carbon emissions monitoring. A mixed-method review was adopted in two-steps
involving scientometric and qualitative analysis in this study. Findings from the scientometric
analysis revealed high interest in research themes such as emissions and energy control, artificial
intelligence-based decision-making and blockchain integration in DT for prefabrication. Further-
more, the findings from the qualitative analysis demonstrated how smart technologies such as ra-
dio frequency identification (RFID), global positioning systems (GPS), laser scanners and sensors
have been employed at the production, transportation, and on-site assembly stages of PSC for
buildings. For real-time carbon emissions monitoring in DT, this study revealed various smart
technologies and their corresponding information requirements for materials/components, ma-
chinery, and processes at each stage of the PSC. Five future research directions were provided on
effective ways to advance DT in PSC for intelligent building processes and monitor emissions.
Therefore, this study not only shows smart technologies suitable for DT in PSC, but also con-
tributes to knowledge on using DT to monitor real-time carbon emissions in PSC for buildings.
This study would aid researchers and practitioners with systemic approaches to employ when ap-
plying DT in PSC.

gence and reducing emissions [3]. Among these technological advancements, the concept of digital twin (DT) offers an overarching
sphere to monitor, manage and evaluate technological systems in AEC [4]. Even though DT is a relatively new subject within AEC, it

evaluation of emissions in buildings. To achieve the study’s aim, two main objectives were formulated: (1) to explore the knowledge
landscape of DT regarding smart construction and carbon emissions in prefabrication; and (2) to assess DT applications at various PSC
stages regarding smart construction and carbon emissions. The reminder of this paper is organized as background and related work,
followed by research methodology and results for smart construction and carbon emissions. Subsequent sections include discussions
and future research directions, implications and conclusions.
2. Background and related work
2.1. DT in construction
The history of DT in construction is relatively brief compared to its emergence in the manufacturing and aerospace industries
[17,18]. However, this evolving technology/concept is developing at a rapid pace and seems to be a critical agenda for many con-
struction industries. This is because of the benefits it provides for real-time status and management of processes and systems (Royal
Institution of Chartered Surveyors (RICS), 2022). Due to its evolution in literature, there is no universal agreement on the definition
of DT. Hence, there are several definitions available from many domains. [19] envisioned product lifecycle management in a digital
platform, and [20] defined DT as a set of virtual information constructs describing an actual physical product. [21] defined DT as a
model representing real machine operation for simulation. [22] viewed DT as a digital counterpart of a physical product, and [23] de-
fined DT as a digital representation of a real-world object/product in a lifecycle. These definitions forge the idea of integrating the
physical world into digital environments. Nonetheless, few studies have highlighted that some of these definitions lack clarity on the
constituents of DT [24]; particularly in the AEC industry. Therefore, further definitions of DT have been provided to capture the func-
tions and characteristics of DT apart from the physical world and digital/virtual representation. Accordingly [25] provided three key
elements that define DT – physical object/asset, digital/virtual counterpart and a connection between the two. Usually, this connec-
tion involves the synchronization of data and information in a bi-directional mode between the digital model and physical reality. Ac-
cording to Ref. [26]; the Center for Digital Built Britain (CDBB) defined DT as a realistic digital representation of assets, processes, or
systems in the built environment. With this definition, emphasis is placed on closing the loop between digital models of construction
and their physical twin objects with real-time information updates. Furthermore, the construction digital twin approach views DT as
virtual representations of real-world systems and processes that are integrated at a specific frequency and accuracy [26,27]. By ex-
ploring the constituents and characteristics of DT, the main elements of DT consist of: (i) real-world or physical object/process, (ii)
virtual or digital representation, and (iii) connection/synchronization using sensing technologies for data, real-time monitoring and
the possibility of intervention based on simulation and artificial intelligence (AI).
In AEC, DT can be created in three levels; a component, a system, or a system of systems assembly of buildings and processes in an
interconnected ecosystem to reflect the real occurrence of events/activities [27]. Changes in the physical environment must be repli-
cated in the virtual twin model [28]. With the aid of sensing technologies, DT leverages two key things – (i) geometry (form) or as-
sembly relationship state of practice, and (ii) connectivity for real-time simulations and AI-enabled analysis in decision-making.

2.2. The BIM dilemma for DT
There are debates on the use of building information modelling (BIM) models to represent DT in construction literature. BIM – a
digital platform for object modelling, is increasingly becoming a common data environment that fills the need for digital prototyping
in design and construction [6,26]. Therefore, many practitioners view BIM as a key element to the DT evolution in AEC. As such, some
studies have perceived ‘as-built’ BIM for facility and asset management as DT [29]. In contrast, [30] highlighted that digital models of
assets that do not automatically interact with physical assets are digital shadows. The CDBB argues that DT goes beyond BIM creating
a virtual model of a physical object [31]. DT considers continuous connectivity and real-time monitoring throughout the project life-
cycle, which current ‘as-built’ BIM models fall short of. Other studies differentiated BIM models from DT by highlighting that the pres-
ence of a real-world physical object is not mandatory in BIM models, and when a physical object is present (e.g. as-built BIM), it lacks
connections with the physical part in real-time [17]. Considering that BIM has gained prominence as a common data environment for
as-built facilities, its growth to become DT is to have automatic connectivity with the physical object/process in real-time throughout
the project lifecycle. Thus, BIM models can be perceived as DT when virtual models represent their as-built assets/processes, and syn-
chronous information exchange occurs between the physical and virtual twin in real-time.
2.3. Prefabrication supply chain
Prefabrication has become an innovative technology in many countries to reduce carbon emissions and mitigate climate change
regarding construction activities (Oti-Sarpong et al., 2022). The concept of prefabrication is to produce building components and
modules in a factory-controlled environment and assemble them at their destination on-site [32,33]. There are many variations in the
prefabrication concept such as off-site construction, modular construction, design for manufacture and assembly (DfMA) and industri-
alized construction [34]. However, these variations do not deviate from the core concept of prefabrication [11]. Thus, shifting most of
the construction activities off-site to a factor-controlled environment. Amidst the optimism surrounding advanced technologies in
prefabrication, the extent to which embodied emissions are reduced at various stages of the PSC is still unclear and vague in research
[15]. This presents opportunities for DT to monitor real-time emissions with smart technologies. PSC typically involves activities such
as the manufacturing of building materials, transportation, factory production processes and on-site installation processes. Although
each of these activities may have its respective sub-activities, they can be aggregated into three main stages of PSC; (i) production, (ii)
transportation, and (iii) on-site installation for DT to explore smart construction and evaluate emissions in real-time. The prefabrica-
tion production stage focuses on building materials and factory-related processes for prefabricated components [35]. Further, the
transportation stage focuses on transport-related activities and the on-site installation stage addresses assembling processes for pre-
fabricated components [36]. For these three stages, smart construction technologies and the associated emissions would be examined.
2.4. Embodied carbon emissions in prefabricated buildings
Embodied carbon emissions (ECE) are emissions associated with producing materials and products for construction and buildings
[37]. It also includes emissions from processes and activities used to transform these materials and products for buildings [38]. ECE in
construction can be cumulatively stratified from material extraction to the end-of-construction [15]. In that regard, ECE is derived
from the energy consumed in extracting and transporting building materials, in addition to the emissions from processes and activi-
ties of prefabrication [25,39]. This provides an indication of the sources of ECE in prefabrication, which can hence be summarized in
terms of materials and processes involved. Within each stage of the PSC, these two sources of ECE are linked to key emission indica-
tors involving resource and energy consumption, equipment used, production chain and construction activities. The respective
sources of ECE for the stages of PSC are presented in Fig. 1.
To explore the potential of DT in this study, the key emission sources of ECE in the main stages of PSC will be explored in tandem
with the main constituents required for DT. By this, the possibilities of applying technologies at each stage of the PSC for automatic
real-time carbon emission evaluation in DT would be explored.

3. Research methodology
This study adopted the mixed-review approach in two-steps to investigate DT research regarding smart construction and emissions
in prefabrication. In the first step, scientometric analysis was used to explore and map the knowledge landscape of DT research in pre-
fabrication [40,41]. This creates an overview of research areas that are of interest in the literature using science mapping techniques
[42]. The second step involves qualitative analysis by critically reviewing the application of DT for smart construction and carbon
emissions at the main stages of PSC. Regarding this step, studies that have used or applied DT technologies to explore smart construc-
tion and assess carbon emissions at any stage of the PSC were extracted from the data collected in the first step and considered for crit-
ical examination.
3.1. Step 1: scientometric analysis
Due to its science mapping capabilities based on text mining, the scientometric approach focuses on identifying and visualizing
patterns in knowledge domains [43]. The scientific database for extracting data in literature was Scopus. Scopus was selected because
it has the largest index of academic literature and produces reputable results [44]. The literature search strategy began with the for-
mulation of a list of keywords. The main components of the list of keywords were related to terms used for DT and prefabrication.
Since different terminologies have been used interchangeably to represent these main components, a pilot review of related studies
was used to select suitable keywords (e.g. Refs. [6,17,45]. Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were used as loops to combine the
strings of keywords [5]. To improve the comprehensiveness and complement the potential of BIM towards DT, as argued by some re-
searchers as a pragmatic approach in AEC, additional sets of keywords were created using the constituents of DT as a requirement for
the case of BIM. Therefore, terms such as “as-built BIM” and “real-time connection” were used to depict the physical twin and connec-
tivity aspects of a DT if BIM is to be considered for DT in AEC. To that effect, the main components of the additional keyword string
were related to terms for as-built BIM, real-time connection and prefabrication. The lists of keywords for the search are provided in
Table 1.
3.1.1. Data collection and data cleaning
Independent searches were conducted using the two sets of keywords (Table 1) in Scopus. The inclusion criteria were peer-review
journal articles [46]. These criteria were selected to improve reliable findings concerning smart construction and emissions in AEC.
The initial search results from the two sets of keywords were filtered for English articles and there was no limitation on the publica-
tion year. From this process, the combined search using DT and BIM-related keywords identified a total of 136 articles. Since some ar-
ticles may appear in both searches, additional filtering was conducted to remove duplicate articles in the search results. The results
from these refinement processes retained 107 articles. In this study, VOSviewer was selected as the science mapping tools to analyze
and visualize data obtained from the Scopus database. VOSviewer uses text mining techniques and algorithms to create networks in
scientometric analysis [47]. Using this tool, the scientometric analysis explored networks and clusters pertaining to areas of research
interest and global research collaborations (Fig. 2).
3.1.2. Characteristics of documents extracted
The publication trend for DT concerning PSC as presented in Fig. 3, generally depicts a sharp increase in research interest from
2018 onwards. Since DT is an emerging concept in AEC, the proliferation of smart technologies and recent calls to reduce carbon
emissions may have contributed to this trend. This development indicates that not only is DT for PSC emissions attracting research at-
tention, but it also reveals a timely intervention on the potential of DT for carbon emissions assessments in AEC.
3.1.3. Knowledge areas of research interest
Keywords reflect the essential themes explored in a topic and aid in the detection of key research areas [48]. Hence, analyzing the

words was analyzed and the network map was visualized using VOSviewer. The proximity of nodes and the size of the links depict the
research interest between two or more nodes (topics) in the network [47]. The network map in Fig. 4 was developed by considering
the minimum number of co-occurrences of keywords to be three, and generic keywords relating to research methods such as ‘survey’
were removed. As a result, 66 keywords were retained as they met or exceeded the criteria. Table 2 presents data from the keywords
network.

tral position. On this basis, topics such as digital twin and internet of things (IoT) have been linked the most in scholarly discussions.

3.1.5. Smart construction technologies adopted for DT in PSC
From Fig. 4., there are some technologies that have gained prominence in enhancing smart construction regarding DT applications
for PSC. Smart technologies confer awareness and interactive abilities to objects or processes in prefabrication [51]. Such technolo-
gies include RFID, embedded systems and wireless sensors, blockchain, AI, 3D printing, visualization tools and environmental tech-
nology. As DT applications are expected to increase in AEC, these technologies provide effective avenues for integration growth, par-
ticularly for PSC.
3.1.5.1. RFID technology. The RFID technology has attracted significant attention as a critical element for IoT. Hence, its considera-
tion in the development of DT for PSC. RFID is an automatic identification technology that detects target objects through radio fre-
quency signals for data processing [36]. Furthermore, it aids in tracking items and the retrieval of product-related information. The
RFID technology uses a system of tags and readers to determine the status of targeted objects for data acquisition in real-time [52].
Due to the adaptability of RFID technology, it has been currently explored in tracking management systems and progress reporting
in attempts to digitally twin the physical prefabrication components and processes [10,53]. In addition, RFID provides the opportu-
nity to assign specific types of information to tags, and hence, data pertaining to emissions, management actions, quantity and pro-
ductivity can be transmitted in the system.
3.1.5.2. Blockchain. The blockchain technology enables traceability, transparency and immutability throughout a fragmented or iso-
lated chain of activities [54]. In this case, applying blockchain in DT for PSC builds trust in the data and information exchanged in
the smart construction management network involving multiple stakeholders. The architecture of blockchain addresses the threat of
having a single point of failure in the data exchange network system of DT for PSC and improves information visibility in collabora-
tive environments for prefabrication [55]. By this, information transmitted in the DT system is more reliable and resilient.
Blockchain provides a security framework in DT to enable smart contract management for suppliers/activities in modular construc-
tion supply chains. Therefore, information exchanged in DT for PSC has an added-value of accountability for activity-related infor-
mation sharing in real-time [56].
3.1.5.3. Wireless sensors and embedded systems. Numerous wireless sensing technologies have been explored regarding DT for PSC
based on functionalities and characteristics [57]. These sensors facilitate the co-creation of digital environments in real-time. This has
enabled PSC products, objects and processes to be made ‘smart’ via real-time data collection and decision-making [51]. More impor-
tantly, sensors have the crucial role of updating the digital environment with changes in the physical environment. Such timely infor-
mation exchange is carried out in a wireless network. Currently, most of the sensing technologies for DT in PSC include GPS, laser sen-
sors, pressure sensors and accelerations sensors. In application, the wireless sensors are attached or embedded into prefabricated
components or processes to transmit data characterizing duration, form, location etc.
3.1.5.4. Artificial intelligence (AI). The increase in information from heterogenous sensors makes AI more effective in improving deci-
sion-making in DT at each stage of PSC. With AI, the DT for PSC can perform self-diagnosis, self-configuration and self-optimization,
thereby influencing managerial interventions. These enriching capabilities of AI improve the accuracy and efficiency of technologies
employed in smart prefabricated systems [58]. Additionally, connected streams of data from prefabrication processes and objects
produce big data in real-time over a period in the DT system. Therefore, data-driven decision-making is required to generate intelli-
gent assets and processes [59]. AI has been adopted in DT for PSC for purposes of predictive modelling, defect detection, simulation
analysis and data segmentation [59,60].

3.1.5.5. 3D printing and robotics. The potential of 3D printing or additive manufacturing is fast making imprints in advancing smart
industrialized construction or buildings. 3D printing technologies leverage on robotic systems and design/construction process-
related data [61]. While the application of 3D digital fabrication is still an infant, it offers DT opportunities to improve the perfor-
mance of robots employed in PSC in real-time [59]. For instance Ref. [62], demonstrated a robot’s DT performing real-time monitor-
ing and simulation at the construction activity level for motion planning and control. The virtual robots created in the DT system si-
multaneously planned the physical robot’s trajectory and executed it in a real work environment [62]. Robotic systems aim to close
the added-value deficits in productivity for prefabrication [61] and facilitate the autonomous creation of prefabricated modules or
components. Therefore, having real-time data-driven robotic operations via DT enhances the synchronization and simulation of PSC
processes.
3.1.5.6. Visualization technologies. Visualizing the information from sensors in prefabricated systems helps to better appreciate the
state of real objects/components in real-time. This generates a comprehensive picture to complement the signals and outcomes from
sensors and data in decision-making. With visualization technologies, DT can strengthen the management and control abilities for
components/processes in PSC. Technologies such as virtual reality and IoT platforms perform visualized real-time monitoring of PSC
activities (e.g. hoisting operations, circular economy, GHG) as decision support systems [9,63,64]. Data received in DT for PSC can
be visualized as geometry or process state for decision analysis that involves simulation.
3.2. Critical review of DT in PSC
The critical review adopted the preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA) to examine empiri-
cal studies applying DT applications for (i) smart construction and (ii) carbon emissions in PSC. PRISMA has gained popularity be-
cause it incorporates two parts – systematic reviews and meta-analysis, for insights on a topic [65]. While the systematic review ex-
tracts evidence that fits a pre-defined eligibility criterion, the meta-analysis examines the outputs of pre-defined criteria from the in-
dividual studies for discussion. Steps in this process involve: (i) a target literature search; (ii) eligible case identification; (iii) data ex-
traction [15].
3.2.1. Target literature search
A search was conducted within the initial Scopus database to extract empirical articles as the target literature. The abstracts and
full texts of all articles in the initial search were carefully examined based on two criteria: (1) the article presented an empirical appli-
cation of DT in PSC, and (2) the DT was used to evaluate carbon emissions in PSC. This process resulted in identifying 25 relevant arti-
cles for further examination.
3.2.2. Eligible case identification
The 25 selected articles reported on DT applications at the three main stages of PSC (i.e. production, transport and on-site assem-
bly). Each of these PSC stages is referred to as a case application of DT in PSC for smart construction and carbon emissions. Therefore,
32 eligible cases were identified. Various variables pre-defined to be extracted from the eligible cases include emission sources, smart
technologies and sensors used, information requirements, smart technology purpose and real-time transmission mode.
3.2.3. Data extraction
To extract these variables, a systematic review was conducted to record the details of the case applications respectively. There-
after, meta-analysis was carried out on the identified variables from the case applications. The meta-analysis provides the quantita-
tive outputs alongside the ways in which DT has been applied for carbon emissions at each stage of the PSC. It also allows findings to
be compared among case applications.
4. Review results
The results of the critical review of DT case applications are presented in two parts – smart construction and carbon emissions.

blockchain can inspect transactions on prefabricated modules and invoke smart contracts based on trust. Quality assurance is crucial
in prefabrication production. Hence, [66] used 3D laser scanning techniques for as-built data capturing to update the DT of prefabri-

4.1.3. PSC on-site assembly stage
The on-site assembly stage has witnessed the use of various smart technologies to promote DT in prefabrication. Due to uncertain
outside conditions, [70] developed an intelligent modular construction system by converting on-site resources into smart objects via
RFID and ultra-wide band (UWB) attachments. The RFID and UWB collected and integrated real-time data on location, identity and
progress in the DT model. The high-fidelity DT model was used to monitor and transmit instructions to a robotic arm system for real-

GPS to make objects in construction ‘smart’ for timely data acquisition on the component status and location. The sensory data trans-
mitted to the DT makes progress monitoring effective for decision-making. Furthermore, [72] used RFID-LoRa for real-time status in-
formation of prefabricated modules on-site to monitor structural performance during installation in a digital replica. For on-site as-
sembly services, [10] employed the RFID system to capture real-time data of on-site working processes in an IoT platform. Subse-
quently, the captured data is visualized with BIM-VR technologies to monitor progress, supervise daily operations and improve effec-
tive decisions for site management.
With intelligent technologies communicating ubiquitously in smart construction, integrity has to be guaranteed in the information
that is shared. In this regard, [73] adopted a blockchain-based DT for real-time compliance checking to support accountable project-
related information sharing. Particularly, using blockchain’s time stamp function in DT. Normal data shared in DT is vulnerable to
overwriting. Hence, engaging blockchain in DT improves traceability in data communication from sensor networks and adds confi-
dence to data transactions from multiple stakeholders. From a different viewpoint [74], employed GPS to transmit the position infor-
mation of modules to an IoT hoisting platform. This is to strengthen the management and intelligent control of multi-level hoisting
processes in prefabrication.
In recent times, laser scanning technologies have been utilized to improve the quality of on-site prefabrication assembly. To accu-
rately locate and assess building components during assembly, [75] carried out omnidirectional laser scanning of components to ob-

AI in DT to predict safety risks for hoisting prefabricated components based on real-time site data gathered in the virtual model. In ad-

4.1.4. All-three-stages of PSC
Some studies have made attempts to use certain smart technologies throughout the three stages of PSC in developing a DT. For in-
stance, Ref. [53] adopted the RFID technology for real-time information and visibility of prefabricated modules from the off-site fac-

and process data was transmitted to the DT at each stage of the supply chain. By this, collaborative decision-making is enhanced for
inventory management at production, location status for transportation and on-site asset and quality management. Furthermore, the
potential of blockchain to boost confidence in real-time information shared among PSC stakeholders has been realized in DT. [54] in-
troduced a permissioned blockchain in an IoT-BIM platform to offer secured interoperability of information/data transmitted from
RFID technologies in the supply chain of modular units. The security in information transmission increases trust and privacy for vari-

which arises when different stakeholders across PSC interact in real-time. Using a DT platform, the blockchain facilitated data on real-
time progress of orders and materials from multiple stakeholders such as clients, module manufacturers and logistics company. Addi-
tionally, immutability in construction progress traceability and monitoring is enhanced for smart construction. Another blockchain-
based DT application for smart construction is smart contracts. As such, [78] developed a blockchain system component status shar-
ing for PSC stakeholders. This is to ensure the integrity of information transmitted in DT for smart contract applications. To trace the
location of modular units throughout PSC, [57] engaged GPS for real-time automatic data collection on the prefabrication delivery
process. Within the DT platform, the GPS facilitated traceability and adjustments for effective decisions to be made. Fig. 7 provides a

4.2. DT in PSC for carbon emissions
The findings from the critical review showed several smart technologies for estimating carbon emissions in PSC. Fig. 8 provides
the results of smart technologies employed to evaluate real-time carbon emissions in DT at each stage of the PSC.
4.2.1. PSC production stage
Various materials and processes are usually employed in the production stage. Therefore, smart technologies have to be carefully
considered to ensure real-time capturing of carbon emissions in DT platforms. To capture carbon emissions in DT, the most critical ac-
tion is to identify and replicate the emission sources in the DT. In the study by Ref. [36]; the RFID system and proximity switch sen-
sors were employed for emission sources involving raw materials and workstation equipment respectively. The RFID system tagged
basic information and embodied carbon emissions to components (i.e. steel and concrete) for data transmission in the DT. For ma-
chines used in the production line, the proximity switch sensors gathered time/duration related information to calculate emissions at
various workstations in real-time. These calculations were based on emissions coefficients derived from established emissions data-
bases. Furthermore, [35] used the RFID system to connect the composition of materials in a component for component information
identification. By this, embodied emissions can be assessed in the DT. With a different technique, [35] adopted laser photoelectric
sensors for real-time component location and processing time when a component enters and leaves the processing station. The time
difference serves as the basis for emissions in the DT computing platform based on the energy consumption data at the workstation.
4.2.2. PSC transportation stage
GPS has been the main technology for tracking distance in construction prefabrication haulage. [36] applied GPS alongside smart
applications on vehicles transporting prefabrication components. Tracking data was collected from the GPS and energy consumed by
the vehicle per distance travelled was computed in the DT platform in real-time. Consequently, the carbons emitted can be generated
for the transportation process instantaneously.
4.2.3. PSC on-site stage
The sources of emissions for on-site activities are mostly a result of machines and movements. Three types of construction equip-
ment have been explored for real-time carbon emissions on-site for DT. The first relates to the tower crane. In this regard, [36] intro-
duced acceleration sensors to estimate the working time of cranes by measuring the motion state of the tower rope. The data gathered
from the sensor is transferred to a DT platform in real-time. Likewise, acceleration sensors were also employed by Ref. [64] to capture
the motion state of the crane’s boom and hook to determine the running time for the actual operation duration. The second piece of
equipment is the construction elevator. Pressure sensors were adopted to monitor the movement of elevators for data gathering and
real-time transmission [36]. By using the pressure difference method, the pressure sensors measure height through the change of pres-
sure [64]. The resulting data from these pressure sensors are shared in a network to a DT platform for the corresponding carbon emis-
sions to be computed. The third equipment monitored for carbon emissions was on-site transfer vehicles. As such, [36,64] attached
GPS to transfer vehicles moving prefabrication components on-site. The GPS detected the position of transfer vehicles (material han-
dling equipment) and transmitted the working time of the vehicle to the DT platform for carbon emissions assessments. Fig. 9 summa-
rizes smart technologies employed for real-time information transmission from materials, equipment and processes at each stage of
the PSC regarding DT application.

5. Discussions and future research directions
This study highlighted the current issues requiring critical attention to develop DT for evaluating the carbon emissions of PSC and
the corresponding research directions. The contribution of the paper lies in applying both quantitative and qualitative analyses on the
literature of DT-based smart technologies that investigated the potential of carbon emissions evaluation in PSC. In this regard, five
critical issues are discussed as follows for the advancement of DT in PSC.
5.1. Increase in smart construction objects
In order to promote DT applications to be widely adopted in prefabricated construction practices, increased awareness and atten-
tion are needed to make the PSC process smarter. Thus, transforming products and processes in PSC into smart construction objects
(SCOs). With SCOs, products and processes are equipped with awareness and information transfer capabilities. Currently, efforts to
transform prefabrication products and processes into smart objects are at the small task level, involving few smart technologies. How-
ever, research on how these smart-enabling technologies would be integrated across different PSC stages for resources (e.g. machin-

5.2. Accuracy of DT in replicating emissions from physical activities/objects
Research on DT for carbon emissions in PSC is emerging in construction literature. Few attempts in this research agenda have
raised both difficulties and opportunities when considering DT as a tool for evaluating emissions. One of the main difficulties relates
to the accuracy of smart technologies in detecting calibrated processes and products. For instance, the RFID strength influences its
precision and is affected by metal surfaces in the working environment. Also, vibrations in the production environment can cause
laser sensor deviations, causing problems in their ability to capture work processes. This impacts the reliability of emissions informa-
tion transferred to the DT. Other sensor applications (e.g. phone GPS) used to monitor emissions at the transportation stage of prefab-
rication had human control limitations, making them prone to errors. In this regard, future research should focus on developing ap-
propriate methods and tolerance levels to improve information reliability for smart technologies used for DT in PSC. Another issue is
the weak link between information parameters derived from sensing technologies on physical assets/objects and the carbon emissions
databases or inventories. Different equipment, processes and materials may require different parameters such as operating time and
distance for carbon emissions computations. Furthermore, the local production setting may pose challenges to the accurate calibra-
tion of processes regarding emissions coefficients. Therefore, future research should focus on developing strong theoretical founda-
tions for linking emissions coefficients to various parameters of processes and equipment on a continuous basis in PSC. This theoreti-
cal lens can be used for creating system architecture for an efficient online system covering a variety of parameters.
5.3. Integrating AI into real-time camera capture for DT
The use of cameras in PSC working environments is increasing for purposes of security, worker safety and productivity monitor-
ing. However, its potential as a sensing technology for data transfer in DT has been under-researched in the literature. The ability of
cameras to capture object motions in real-time and provide visual identification of objects would be helpful for AI to compute, cali-
brate and programme activities in PSC for DT. For instance, AI can be incorporated into the real-time video images derived from vari-
ous cameras in the production line to determine two attributes: (i) the motion state of products/materials, and (ii) the type of prod-
uct/material. By this, developing an AI-based platform to compute and categorize methods for capturing the journey of products in
the production line with live video images would be a fertile avenue for future research in DT. This approach offers new opportunities
for acquiring data on the real state of practice at the production stage of PSC. Such data from the AI-camera integration (i.e. motion
state and type of product), can be subsequently converted for carbon emissions evaluations when applying DT in PSC. Furthermore,
with AI-camera integration, geometric quality can be assessed in real-time at the production and on-site assembly stages. The real-
time video images of as-produced prefabricated products can be compared with the real-time on-site video images of products and the
AI-based computing platform would determine quality compliance. In addition, the integration of AI into real-time video cameras has
great potential for robotic applications at the production and on-site assembly stages.
5.4. Standardizing the data structure for DT in PSC
The type of data to be transferred in DT for PSC still needs more research to strengthen data acquisition. Recent studies concerning
DT for PSC have highlighted diverse ways of acquiring data from some processes, machinery and materials used in PSC for DT. While
this development underscores alternatives to data acquisition, it raises concerns about the type and structure of data that would be ef-
fective for DT applications. Different machinery and processes would require different measurement units to acquire real-time data.
Therefore, future research can focus on providing standardized methods of structuring real-time data derived from different
processes, machines and materials for DT applications in PSC. This would enable a standardized structure of collecting and transfer-
ring information for DT computations in PSC such as carbon emissions. Also, such efforts would spur research to develop customized
DT platforms for PSC stages, hence promoting the use of DT in PSC.
5.5. Two-way data transmission in DT
Most of the studies applying DT in PSC concentrated on one-way data transmission. Thus, information transfers from sensing tech-
nologies (physical assets) to DT platforms. However, one main strength of DT is transferring instructions or decisions from the DT
platform to the sensing technologies (physical assets) [62]. This results in two-way data transmission. With this gap in literature, fu-
ture studies should focus on developing a mechanism for two-way data flow as the key feature of the DT model to the physical asset at
any stage of PSC in real-time (e.g. Ref. [70]. This would help instructions to be transferred from DT to physical assets to enable real-
time interventions when monitoring carbon emissions. Fig. 10 provides the directions for future research.

6. Theoretical and practical implications
The findings in this study have implications for research and practice. For research, the insights presented in the five research di-
rections for DT in PSC provide a firm foundation for the formulation and development of theoretical frameworks/models for real-time
carbon emissions monitoring. Theoretically, this study expands the discussions on emissions in construction literature to a new niche
(i.e. prefabrication) for the possibility of real-time monitoring. Such theoretical frameworks spurred by this study would enable re-
searchers to develop novel approaches suitable for capturing processes in PSC, and hence, advance the understanding and application
of DT in prefabrication.
Instead of one-off emissions calculations usually conducted by practitioners at the design stage of buildings, this study offers op-
portunities for continuous evaluation of carbon emissions in real-time for prefabrication. For practitioners, this would be useful in
making timely decisions at each stage of the prefabrication process for appropriate interventions to be carried out. In this regard, pro-
ject managers and policy makers would be able to assess the real emissions associated with prefabrication when carbon reduction tar-
gets or offsets are part of the project goals. Furthermore, the DT in PSC approached revealed in this study would provide practitioners
with relevant knowledge on the information requirements needed from smart technologies for real-time evaluations of emissions.
7. Conclusions
This study presents the findings of a comprehensive review of DT applications in PSC to advance smart construction and carbon
emission evaluation using the mixed-methods approach. From the scientometric analysis, the key areas of research interests regarding
DT for PSC relate to emissions and energy control, AI-based decision-making, automatic monitoring, visualization and blockchain in-
tegration. For research collaboration, China and the UK have collaborated the most with other countries to explore DT in PSC. In the
critical review, smart construction technologies predominantly applied in DT at each stage of the PSC for data real-time data acquisi-
tion were RFID and GPS. 3D laser scanning technologies are gaining traction for physical asset data capture and these technologies
could be beneficial in the initiation of DT in PSC. Furthermore, the critical review revealed that smart technologies such as RFID prox-
imity switch, laser sensors, pressure sensors and acceleration sensors are more suitable for representing real-time carbon emissions of
materials and processes at each stage of prefabrication in a DT platform. These smart technologies have exposed new ways to collect

real-time information status and operating time of various machines, processes and materials at the production and on-site assembly
for emissions computations in DT. For the transportation stage, GPS was the main sensing technology for real-time information collec-
tion in DT for emissions computations. The smart technologies identified in the study offer a comprehensive approach to develop and
implement DT in PSC. Particularly, the information requirements needed from these technologies to evaluate carbon emissions at
each stage of the PSC would motivate researchers and practitioners on how to improve real-time monitoring of processes and materi-
als in prefabricated construction.
With an emerging interest to advance DT at different stages of the PSC, several gaps and weaknesses were identified in extant liter-
ature. In that regard, five fertile avenues to advance knowledge and accelerate DT applications in PSC were provided in this study.
Thus, increasing SCOs in prefabrication, improving accurate replication of emissions in DT, integrating AI into real-time video cam-
eras, standardizing data structure for DT and exploring two-way data transmission in DT for PSC. This study makes significant contri-
butions to the body of knowledge on DT by: (i) revealing five core areas of interest and the crucial areas that are under-represented as
DT for PSC literature is emerging, (ii) providing deep insights on how smart technologies can be applied for carbon emissions moni-
toring in DT, and (iii) offering effective avenues for future research to improve and consolidate DT applications in PSC. The outcomes
of the study would aid researchers in developing principles for standardizing information requirements and measurement units for
prefabrication processes, machines and materials in DT for various purposes. Nonetheless, this study has the limitation of considering
English articles only. Also, another limitation is DT and its associated technologies are rapidly evolving. Hence, smart technologies
that are about to enter the construction market may be captured in the review. Therefore, researchers and industry practitioners
should closely monitor these developments and integrate smart technologies that add value over time. However, the insights pre-
sented in this study hold great potential for future research to advance the knowledge development on DT for PSC.
