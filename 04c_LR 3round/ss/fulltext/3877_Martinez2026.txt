Abstract
Purpose – This study explores the integration of monitored data from Tunnel Boring Machine (TBM) operations 
into a digital twin framework to generate real-time status information for mechanized tunnelling. The study 
investigates the potential benefits that can be derived in terms of lean operations and reduced waste of rework.
Design/methodology/approach – Using a design science research approach within a case study of a tunnelling 
project, the researchers developed, tested and refined the Digital Twin Construction (DTC) system and observed 
use cases of its application. The empirical data collected was analyzed to draw conclusions concerning the 
feasibility of the integration of TBM excavation data into a digital twin and the impacts of its use in improving 
flow and avoiding rework by reducing uncertainty.
Findings – The study identifies key technical challenges, such as imperfect data interoperability across different 
systems involved in tunnelling operations and the need for standardized approaches to modelling. It also highlights 
the value of the digital twin in supporting lean design and construction workflows by providing the project team 
with information drawn from a dynamic representation of the tunnel’s physical state in short cycle times.
Originality/value – This study presents an innovative computational design algorithm that automates data 
integration, enabling digital twin generation as a functional product. Furthermore, it includes use cases 
illustrating how the resulting digital twin supports the improvement of specific operations, such as bracketry 
design and construction. By addressing both technical and operational dimensions, this case study serves as a 
valuable resource for researchers and industry practitioners aiming to advance digital twin technology and lean 
construction in tunnelling and broader construction applications.

1. Introduction

amounts of data, making effective information management critical for project success. In this 
context, the use of Building Information Modelling (BIM) and digital twin technologies (Parn 
et al., 2024) offers significant potential to facilitate data integration, real-time monitoring and 
fact-based decision-making throughout the tunnel lifecycle. Beyond supporting data 
management, these technologies also align closely with lean construction principles. Many 
of the positive interactions are related to improving operations flow and reducing the waste of

TBMs are equipped with a rotating circular cutting head to excavate through various 
types of ground, including rock, soil and mixed conditions.  As the cutting head excavates 
and the TBM advances, the excavated material is conveyed away through an integrated 
system of conveyor belts, ensuring the continuous progression  of the excavation (Maidl 
et al., 2012). Simultaneously, the TBM places precast concrete segments via an automated 
system, creating a continuous lining behind it that shapes the tunnel structure. Advances in 
technology have enabled modern TBMs to integrate sophisticated  real-time monitoring and 
sensor systems, generating extensive datasets critical for precise management and 
optimization  of excavation processes (Garcia et al., 2021). These data streams include 
TBM performance  metrics,  tunnel lining integrity, segment positioning  and other 
operational  parameters.
Similarly, advancements  in BIM and digital twin technologies  have provided the 
construction  industry with powerful means to enhance productivity  and streamline 
processes through improved data integration  (Sacks et al., 2025), becoming instrumental  in 
enhancing collaboration,  efficiency and information  management within construction  
projects (Noor and Yi, 2018; Zhi et al., 2018). The evolution of real-time data collection and 
sensor networks has further facilitated  the transition to digital twins (Salzgeber et al., 
2024), enabling a more dynamic and responsive approach to project management called 
Digital Twin Construction  (DTC) (Sacks et al., 2020). In principle, a tunnel digital twin has 
two parts: (1) a BIM model of the tunnel’s design (its intent information),  and (2) an 
accurate virtual representation  of the tunnel that reflects its physical counterpart  in real time 
(its status information).  The status information  is compiled by integrating  various data 
sources, including sensor data, construction  models and operational  information  (Li et al., 
2024). This digital twin replica system can be used for monitoring,  analysis and decision-
making throughout the tunnel’s lifecycle, from design and construction  to operation and 
maintenance.
Although earlier studies have examined the use of digital twins for tunnelling (e.g. Wu 
et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2022), there are still several gaps in knowledge that 
must be addressed. These gaps can be organized into two categories, namely technical and 
operational. Technical gaps are associated with developing the digital twin system as a 
functional product. Challenges in this area derive primarily from the complexity of the data 
generated by TBMs during excavation. Specifically, the sheer volume of real-time data 
collected from various sensors can overwhelm traditional data processing systems, making it 
difficult to extract actionable insights (Getuli et al., 2021). Moreover, the lack of standardized 
data formats and interoperability between different systems often hampers effective data 
sharing among system components, limiting the collaborative potential of digital twins 
(Stascheit et al., 2018).
Operational gaps centre on the effective use of digital twins to inform and support project 
workflows. This fact is particularly crucial in the context of lean construction, where 
improving flow, reducing waste and continuous improvement are fundamental goals (Koskela, 
1992). Digital twins are not merely virtual models; they represent a dynamic integration of 
physical assets and digital environments, continuously synchronized through real-time data 
streams (Grieves, 2014). Their potential lies in their ability to bridge the gap between virtual 
planning and physical execution, providing a comprehensive, data-driven framework for 
optimizing construction processes. The seamless integration of diverse project status 
information into the digital twin is essential for proactively informing lean design and 
decision-making processes. By enabling real-time monitoring and predictive analytics, digital 
twins facilitate a more reliable workflow planning, enhance production control and support the 
systematic elimination of constraints, ultimately improving the quality of flow and reducing 
waste (Ara�ujo et al., 2022). However, a key challenge remains in the completeness and

This study investigates the effective integration of TBM-generated data into BIM to form

addresses the technical and operational gaps while demonstrating how specific use cases 
enhance lean tunnel construction. It contributes to knowledge in two key areas. First, it 
provides insights into the methods for data integration, the challenges faced during the process 
and formulates the lessons learned from real-world implementation. Second, it analyses use 
cases where information from previous project phases, such as segment production data, was 
integrated into the developed digital twin to facilitate related design and production processes, 
including bracketry design and construction. Ultimately, this case study serves as a valuable 
reference for industry practitioners and researchers aiming to advance the adoption of digital 
twin technology in tunnelling and other construction applications.
2. Research design
This study uses the Design Science Research (DSR) methodology to investigate the 
implementation of BIM and digital twin technologies within a tunnelling project. DSR is a 
problem-solving paradigm that focuses on the development and evaluation of artefacts 
intended to address identified issues (vom Brocke et al., 2020). This approach is particularly 
suited for studies that aim to design, implement and assess innovative solutions within 
complex socio-technical contexts, particularly in fields such as engineering, architecture and 
information technology (March and Smith, 1995). Accordingly, this research develops and 
evaluates a digital twin framework tailored to a tunnelling project.
The selection of the case study is based on its representativeness of mechanized tunnelling 
projects that integrate digital twin methodologies and address practical application challenges. 
This ensures that the study is grounded in empirical data collected directly from the project, 
providing a solid foundation for practical application and meaningful insights. The data used 
include project documentation, design information, TBM-generated data and tunnel segment 
production data. The iterative DSR process facilitated the design, implementation and 
evaluation of a digital twin prototype, ensuring alignment with the project’s specific 
requirements. The research design followed the key phases of the DSR methodology 
(Figure 1):
(1) Problem identification and motivation: The initial phase involved identifying the 
challenges associated with integrating BIM and digital twin technologies in tunnelling 
projects. This phase included a detailed analysis of existing processes and data 
structures to establish the need for a tailored digital twin solution.
(2) Objective definition and data collection: This phase aimed to define the specific
objectives of the digital twin framework. Data collection focused on acquiring 
comprehensive datasets essential for understanding the tunnelling processes and 
technological requirements. The data encompassed construction workflows, design 
models and sensor data from TBMs, forming the foundation for framework 
development.
(3) Design and development: The third phase focused on designing the digital twin 
framework. This included structuring data for integration, ensuring consistency and 
developing algorithms to facilitate seamless data integration. Prototypes of the digital 
twin were iteratively refined based on feedback and operational testing.

(4) Demonstration: The digital twin was implemented within the project environment to 
evaluate its functionality and utility. This phase involved testing the digital twin 
against defined use cases, such as supporting design validation and progress

(5) Iteration and refinement: Based on insights from the demonstration phase, iterative 
improvements were made to enhance the digital twin framework. This included 
addressing any identified limitations, optimizing algorithms and improving data 
integration processes to better align with project requirements.
(6) Evaluation: The evaluation phase involved analyzing the performance and effectiveness 
of the digital twin framework. Metrics included efficiency gains in the BIM-digital twin 
modelling time, usability and impact on decision-making processes. The results informed 
adjustments to optimize the digital twin’s performance and relevance.
The final analysis and conclusions phase helps synthesize the findings from all previous steps 
to evaluate what worked well and what did not. Lessons learned were documented to inform 
future research and practice, and results were contrasted with existing literature to highlight 
contributions to the state of the art, as well as implications for practice and future research.
The subsequent sections of the paper are structured to reflect this framework, detailing the 
processes, challenges and outcomes encountered throughout the DSR cycle. The findings offer 
insights into the socio-technical dynamics of BIM and digital twin implementation and 
contribute to advancing knowledge on their integration in complex construction projects.
3. Related literature: BIM and digital twin in tunnelling construction
BIM research for underground construction has traditionally been less developed than that for 
above-ground structures (Chapman et al., 2020). Nevertheless, in recent years, there has been 
growing interest in researching how BIM and digital twin technologies can support complex 
infrastructure projects. Research has progressed from utilizing the Industry Foundation 
Classes (IFC) schema to model individual components to adopting more holistic approaches. 
These advanced methodologies now encompass not only the tunnel structure itself but also the

Borrmann and Jubierre (2013) proposed a detailed shield tunnel product model 
complemented by a coherent multi-scale representation of semantics and geometry for 
visualization in IFC-based viewers. Aiming to extend the IFC schema, Vilgertshofer et al. 
(2016) introduced a concept to enable shield tunnel product representation based on three 
consecutive models of extension. Expanding beyond tunnelling products and components, 
Zhou et al. (2018) proposed a framework to include segment assembly operations into the IFC 
schema. The authors proposed using an algorithm to automatically identify design information 
required to allow linking different segment attributes in the model, proposing the application 
of IFC within parametric design software like Dynamo and Grasshopper for fast modelling and 
design optimization. In the same direction, Hegemann et al. (2012) explored extending and 
linking the IFC class hierarchy to mechanized tunnelling, particularly by considering the 
compatibility of IFC classes with TBMs. Similarly, Zhong et al. (2018) presented a BIM-based 
shield tunnel structure information model using an extended IFC description.
An important aspect of tunnelling is considering the relationships with the surroundings 
and underground conditions. In this area, and taking a holistic view, Koch et al. (2017) 
explored the integration of data from four different data models in mechanized tunnelling 
construction: a ground model, a boring machine model, a tunnel lining model and a built 
environment model. The four different models were linked through an open IFC environment. 
The resulting model was tested to verify its use in selected case studies. Some limitations of the 
proposed approach concerned visualization, which could be cumbersome when implemented 
on larger projects requiring detailed geometry. Furthermore, the model limited the scope of 
information that can be exchanged with the IFC protocol. Similarly, in related research, Ninic 
et al. (2020, 2019, 2017) introduced SATBIM, a concept for parametric modelling to assess the 
safety and stability aspects of tunnelling design. The concept enables the parametric 
representation of key system components such as soil excavation, tunnel lining and TBMs, 
within an information model structured for different levels of details (LoDs).
Specifically for mechanized tunnel construction, Hegemann et al. (2020) presented a BIM data 
integration model to derive as-built documentation of segmental lining rings. In addition to 
aligning the BIM model to the as-is position, the proposed model integrates data such as thrust 
cylinder loading history, operational and grouting pressures and segment lifecycle documentation. 
Similarly, Zhu et al. (2020) proposed a concept for the automatic generation of a tunnel 
information model leveraging BIM functionalities and visual programming language to 
manipulate BIM elements and process data. The authors validated the model by successfully 
transferring multiple design alternatives of a tunnel project into a comprehensive tunnel 
information model. Alsahly et al. (2020) explored the integration of BIM and finite element (FE) 
simulation in mechanised tunnelling, aiming to automatically extract important information such 
as geology, alignment, lining and process parameters, which are required to perform FE 
simulations and analysis of the tunnel drive. Getuli et al. (2021) presented a conceptual framework 
for the generation of as-built drawings combining BIM models and the information created from 
the TBM. The authors aimed to address the challenges posed by complex curved geometries by 
modelling curves’ segment parameters and automating the process through four interlinked 
algorithms. While the authors demonstrated the feasibility of their approach, the framework was 
not tested or validated in a real project setting, leaving its practical applicability unproven.
In another stream of research, some authors have explored the use of scanning devices for 
automated as-built model generation. Cheng et al. (2019) proposed a general approach to 
automatically identify the different types of components present in tunnels (e.g. rails, cross-
sections, pipes, catenary equipment and refuges) and create parametric as-is BIM supported by 
terrestrial laser scanning data. Arastounia (2016) proposed an automated method for as-built 
model generation in the context of subway tunnels using mobile Light Detection and Ranging 
data. Similarly, Xue et al. (2021) proposed a concept for a metro tunnel 3D reconstruction 
based on structure from motion (SfM) and direct linear transformation.

In the transition from BIM to digital twins, researchers have conceptualized the technology 
from diverse perspectives and for a range of applications. Yu et al. (2021) proposed a decision 
analysis framework for tunnel operation and maintenance based on digital twin technology. 
The framework introduces an organizational approach for tunnel twin data, which builds upon 
the extended COBie standard, and employs semantic web technologies to integrate data, 
objects and knowledge. This approach was tested and validated using a case study of a road 
tunnel in China. The authors further developed their concept to facilitate knowledge-driven 
decision support for tunnel electromechanical equipment maintenance. Likewise, Wu et al. 
(2022) explored the use of digital twins to monitor tunnel operations and traffic. The authors 
focused on constructing a tunnel digital twin and implementing virtual-real fusion operations 
through 3D video fusion. Tijs (2020) analyzed the prerequisites and structure of a digital twin 
for tunnels in the Netherlands. The author emphasized the significance of incorporating a 
digital twin into tunnel operations and maintenance based on demand analysis.
Zhao et al. (2022) introduced a digital twin model tailored for intelligent safety assessment 
in tunnel construction. The authors used real-space information collection technologies, 
including the Internet of Things (IoT) and intelligent sensor technology, to gather data from 
ongoing projects. The virtual twin model was created by integrating BIM technology and 
machine learning algorithms. The effectiveness of this approach was demonstrated through an 
example involving the settlement of the arch crown during tunnel construction. Sepasgozar 
(2020) explored virtual and augmented reality, as well as digital twin applications. The author 
developed and implemented an augmented reality system for construction, including Piling 
AR (PAR) and a virtual tunnel boring machine (VTBM) module. VTBM, designed as a 
multiplayer module with avatars, allows users to experience the operation of a tunnel boring 
machine. The study proposed using immersive construction modules and digital twins for 
educational purposes. Shen et al. (2022) explored the use of a digital-twin-based framework to 
improve the tunnel lighting environment. The study included the design of a virtual world 
through VR. The authors also explored numerical simulations and field experiments to 
enhance the design of the luminaires of the tunnel.
Salzgeber et al. (2024) examine the transition from digital models to digital twin in tunnel 
construction, emphasizing the critical role of structured, real-time data synchronization. 
They highlight the need to address both technical and non-technical barriers, such as data 
quality, ownership and collaborative management practices, to enable effective digital 
twin implementation. Although the authors propose a framework for tunnel twin development, 
it remains unvalidated in a real-world context, leaving a gap in practical application evidence.
In summary, research on the integration of BIM and digital twin technology in 
infrastructure projects has been steadily growing. There is a consensus that the full 
potential of BIM and digital twin applications in tunnelling construction has not yet been fully 
realized, and current research efforts are still in their early stages (Costin et al., 2018). 
Despite notable advancements in recent years, mechanized tunnelling digital modelling 
research has predominantly centered around conceptual frameworks rather than practical 
applications. The authors acknowledge that it is not easy to find facilities that possess 
comprehensive as-built relevant data beyond a 3D representation of distinct building 
components. There is a primary need to establish a standardized method for BIM modelling in 
tunnel projects during both the design and construction phases (Getuli et al., 2021).
The gaps in the transition from BIM to digital twin are similar. Current research is mainly 
centered on broad technologies, including conceptual frameworks and general modelling 
methods, with a particular focus on the operation and maintenance phase (Parn et al., 2024). 
Specifically, digital twin applications to support the design and construction phases in 
mechanized construction using TBMs remain underdeveloped. While studies such as Zhao 
et al. (2022) and Wu et al. (2022) explore innovative techniques for digital twin development, 
including real-space information collection and video fusion, they do not include in their 
methodologies the vast amount of data generated by TBMs. This is a critical gap, as TBM data 
is essential for optimizing tunnelling processes, enhancing decision-making and ensuring

4. Problem identification and objectives
The research involves the implementation of a digital twin system within the context of a 
2.4 km tunnel construction project in France using a variable density TBM. Recognizing the 
wealth of available data, the project team set the objective of integrating these resources to 
develop a digital twin system to support operations. Analyzing the construction process and 
the data from various systems provided the foundation for defining the requirements of the 
intended digital twin. The technical approach to design the digital twin (e.g. data model and 
algorithms) aimed to address three specific operational challenges and use cases:
(1) Automated generation of the tunnelling lining segment status model (as-built):
The TBM should follow a trajectory designed based on geo-exploration data to avoid 
unstable strata and protect underground resources. However, deviations from the target 
path can occur due to unbalanced loads in mixed geological conditions (Liu et al., 
2019). Therefore, the final location of the segments being automatically installed by 
the TBM system may differ from the as-plan model. Accordingly, the final position of 
the segments needs to be adjusted to derive an accurate as-built model. In modern 
TBMs (as in the case of this project), data about the final position of the segments can 
be extracted from TBM steering software. Nevertheless, this position may not be 
accurate in some cases due to deviations in the planned trajectory axis and general 
measurement inaccuracies. Therefore, the final segment position must be confirmed 
via additional surveying activities on the field. The data from both, TBM steering 
software and surveying confirmation should be combined to derive an accurate as-built 
position of the segments. The project team aimed to automate this process by 
integrating positioning data from both sources into the BIM model.
(2) Traceability of segment manufacturing information: beyond generating the 3D
representation of the as-built tunnel, the project team aimed to enrich the model with 
relevant pre-cast segment manufacturing data as a starting point for the digital twin 
creation. Tunnel segments are pre-casted outside the project in a controlled 
manufacturing environment. The quality control in this production process allows 
the individualization of each segment via serial numbers. The segments are transported 
to the construction site to be finally installed using the automated TBM systems. 
Attached to each segment serial number there is important segment production data 
such as segment type, production date, concrete batch, reinforcement design, etc. 
Incorporating this data into the BIM model generates an additional layer of valuable 
information which can be leveraged in future phases of the tunnel lifecycle. 
For instance, when there is a quality issue/request related to an individual segment, the 
information, including manufacturing data, could be retrieved through the model using 
a unique ID (Figure 2 left).

(3) Facilitate tunnel bracketry design and production workflows: an additional practical
application of having the segments’ manufacturing information available in the model 
is related to planning and installation of bracketry to support mechanical, electrical and 
overhead line equipment. After the lining of segments is done, the completion of 
the tunnel requires intense drilling on the segments to anchor the bracketry for 
supporting facilities. One of the main challenges of this phase is related to de definition 
of “no-drill zones.” The structural integrity of the tunnel relies on the segments. 
Therefore, defining the zones where drilling can compromise reinforcement is key to 
ensuring tunnel stability and functionality. It is common practice to indicate no-drill 
zones in the model. However, the segments as-built position is only known after 
installation. Additionally, the tunnel lining consists of rings containing various types of 
segments (e.g. key, counter key, standard), creating a complex array that complicates 
the definition of an efficient linear drilling configuration (Figure 3). Therefore, an 
as-built model including these no-drill zones can facilitate both design and 
construction activities. In practical terms, this model can then be utilized by 
mechanical, electrical and overhead line equipment designers through automated clash 
detection to establish a safe and efficient bracketry system, further contributing to the 
development of the digital twin. Furthermore, installation and drilling teams can also 
use the model to navigate around no-drill zones efficiently during construction. 
Considering these benefits, the project team aimed to develop an as-built model that 
clearly defines these zones. Figure 3, adapted from Douglas et al. (2019), provides an 
illustrative view of the tunnel lining on a plane, highlighting in red pattern fill the 
substantial and complex surface area taken up by no-drill zones.

5. Digital twin design and development
Figure 4 schematically illustrates the data integration concept developed to generate the digital 
twin. Original tunnel geometry and as-planned information are derived from the tunnel lining 
design and stored as Project Intent Information (PII). Segment production details, along with 
identification numbers, are sourced from the segment manufacturer’s proprietary software and 
are stored with the Project Status Information (PSI). Final segment positioning is established 
by integrating TBM positioning data with survey-based verification. Ultimately, the generated 
digital twin is intended to support future activities, particularly in bracketry design and 
construction.
5.1 Data sources
Figure 5 provides an overview of the different proprietary systems and their integration to 
derive the digital twin. To different degrees, data sources had to be structured and organized in 
a machine-readable format to be integrated using algorithms developed in Dynamo. Details of 
the data sources and the work done to prepare the data are as follows:
TBM data: All data from the segment production and the tunnel excavation using the TBM 
is collected in TPC (Tunnel Process Control). TPC is a specialized software solution designed 
to monitor and manage tunnelling operations in real time. The software integrates data from 
TBMs, surveying instruments and other construction equipment, allowing for continuous 
oversight of excavation progress, segment placement and alignment (Tunnelsoft, 2024). In this 
study, all data from the segment production (e.g. production date, segment ID, type) was 
collected within TPC. Since the TBM scans the tunnel segment before installing it, there are 
records about the time and place where each tunnel segment was installed.
Guiding system (VMT): TBMs commonly employ advanced guidance systems to achieve 
millimeter-level precision, even where tunnels extend over several kilometers. These systems 
continuously calculate real-time data essential for maintaining alignment with the planned 
tunnel axis. Data from the guidance system was used to validate and, when necessary, correct 
information obtained from the TBM’s TPC system. This validation was crucial for identifying 
measurement deviations caused by factors such as rolling (lateral shifts of the TBM against the 
tunnel face) and misalignments from the planned trajectory.

Dynamo. Segment parameters and their relationships were defined to allow segment rotation 
around a predefined tunnel axis (the as-is axis as captured from TPC and VTM systems). This 
is necessary to specify the keystone position correctly after placing the object. The parametric 
objects are also prepared to hold the information specified by the client (e.g. classification 
structure, segment ID).
6. Demonstration and refinement
An innovative algorithm was developed in Dynamo to generate the PSI (as-built model) based 
on the consolidated data from the various monitoring sources. Dynamo is an open-source 
visual programming platform that is widely used in the field of BIM, particularly with Revit 
applications (Dynamo BIM, 2021). Dynamo provides a graphical interface for creating custom 
scripts and workflows, enabling task automation and efficient control and modification of BIM 
elements in Revit.
In Figure 7, the developed algorithm performs a set of logical steps to create the digital 
twin: it reads data from the MS Excel database, places tunnel segments according to as-is 
coordinates, adjusts the object parameters according to the updated geometry and appends 
additional information (e.g. ring ID and manufacturing data).
As emphasized in previous research, the technical challenges associated with developing a 
digital twin as a functional product often derive from the complexity and volume of data to be 
processed, which can overwhelm data processing systems (Getuli et al., 2021). To address 
these challenges and streamline the data processing required for digital twin generation, the 
algorithm is designed to incrementally load information into the model, beginning with the 
basic geometry. Specifically, the algorithm utilizes a ring substitute object (Figure 8, left), 
which represents the front and back planes of each ring. By leveraging this approach, the 
algorithm dynamically adjusts the position of each substitute ring based on the corrected real 
centre point coordinates, ensuring both accurate and efficient model generation. The ring 
substitute serves as an optimization mechanism to mitigate excessive computational demands 
within the algorithm. It is initially positioned in the model using a minimal data set to

approach not only enhances accuracy but also significantly reduces the time and effort needed 
in model generation, ensuring that the as-built BIM model is a precise representation of the 
actual construction.
6.1 Results and evaluation
Figure 9 shows a 3D visualization of the model in an IFC viewer (left) and a superposition of 
the tunnel segments with a picture from the tunnel under construction (right). The final digital 
twin of the tunnel segment lining provided an accurate representation of what had been built at 
each stage. The model successfully integrates and displays data derived from previous phases 
in the project life cycle, such as the original BIM model and key manufacturing data from 
tunnel segment production. The model also integrates the “no-drill” zones, supporting the 
design and execution of future project phases. In this case, the model is used to optimize 
the design and construction of the bracketry system for mechanical, electrical and overhead 
line equipment and to perform clash detection with segment reinforcement. The availability of 
the model with this level of accuracy and information can also support additional use cases, 
both during construction and later for operations and maintenance.

7. Discussion
This case study makes an important contribution to the fields of BIM and digital twin systems for 
construction by applying computational design methods to integrate TBM and segment 
manufacturing data into an as-built BIM model, serving as the foundational framework for a 
forthcoming digital twin. In line with the objectives of the study, the authors analyzed the findings 
and experience of the study from a broader perspective in relation to literature considering the 
digital twin as a functional product and its use to facilitate design and lean construction workflows.
7.1 From BIM to a functional digital twin
Boje et al. (2020) highlight that within the interconnected relationship between the physical and 
digital components of a digital twin, BIM is often regarded as a foundational element. This is 
because the development of a digital twin typically originates from BIM, which initially serves as 
the digital representation of the asset. As the project progresses, BIM is incrementally enriched 
with real-time data streams, transitioning into a fully operational digital twin capable of 
supporting real-time monitoring and decision-making. However, the vast volume of real-time 
data collected from multiple sensor sources in TBM construction presents a significant 
challenge. Traditional data processing systems often struggle to manage and interpret this influx 
of information, limiting the ability to derive actionable insights (Getuli et al., 2021). A promising 
approach to address these data management challenges is the integration of parametric design 
tools, such as Dynamo and Grasshopper, into the DT framework. Zhou et al. (2018) suggest that 
these tools can facilitate automated tunnel model generation with precise segment placement,

design method applied in this study builds upon this idea and the successful integration achieved

Nevertheless, certain manual steps were necessary to ensure system functionality. This limitation 
primarily derives from the lack of integration between the various systems involved in tunnelling 
projects. Multiple stakeholders relying on separate, discrete systems, create more barriers to data 
sharing and integration. For example, data from tunnel segment production is often restricted to 
proprietary systems managed by manufacturers. Likewise, TBM data is stored in exclusive 
systems developed by equipment suppliers. The experience in this case study confirms the view 
of researchers and industry practitioners that challenges in fully leveraging BIM and digital twin 
are not just about the technology but span into broader organizational and structural industry 
issues (Getuli et al., 2021; Stascheit et al., 2018).
This study also highlights the need for a standardized approach to managing tunnel 
geometry and information. Tunnels feature complex and often irregular geometries, making it 
challenging to represent them accurately in IFC—particularly for nonstandard tunnel designs 
(Gong et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2022). Starting with Yabuki (2009), several efforts have aimed at 
establishing a certain degree of standardization (e.g. BuildingSmart, 2020; Lee and Kim, 2011; 
Stascheit et al., 2013). However, essential design information remains unrepresented, or the 
standard  itself  has struggled  to gain sufficient  industry  traction  for widespread  
implementation. In this case study, the original tunnel design had to be detailed and 
parametrized to meet the interoperability requirements necessary to facilitate integration with 
other data sources. This effort could have been reduced if those BIM-IFC data standards and 
requirements were set up from the very beginning of the project.
7.2 Digital twin construction – informing and controlling construction operations
Conceptually, construction with digital twin information systems consists of two main 
components: (a) intent information, encompassing as-designed and as-planned data, such as 
original BIM models and construction plans, and (b) status information, comprising as-built and 
as-performed data, including details like final segment positioning (Sacks et al., 2020). In this 
case study, these components are processed separately and eventually brought together to form 
the digital twin. This generated virtual system proactively informs the physical world, enabling 
the construction project team to compare the intended design with the actual construction in real 
time. This approach offers a more meaningful and productive outcome than traditional as-built 
models, which are typically compiled reactively at the end of the construction phase.
In terms of operational efficiency, the automation gained with the proposed solution 
contributes to reduce manual work that would be needed to produce the digital twin by manual 
means otherwise. Per tunnel ring, the cycle time to model a ring was optimized by more than 
90%. The benefits extend to further operations in the project lifecycle since the digital twin 
system has information that can be fed back to inform the ongoing fabrication level design 
detailing, part of the design intent. Once the segments are placed, the information in the status 
model is used as inputs for proactive engineering bracketry drilling design. This cyclical 
flow—integrating information from both intent and status back into the design—represents the 
core operational value provided by the developed digital twin in this study. Additionally, 
similar operational benefits can be extrapolated to upstream workflows, such as streamlining 
the bracket construction process and ensuring that relevant segment production information, 
including sensors, is linked to its precise location within the built tunnel lining for future 
maintenance and operations.
7.3 Limitations and future work
The single case study design may limit the generalizability of the findings. The parametric 
segment objects and data models developed are specifically tailored to the unique project

8. Conclusions
This study demonstrates the feasibility and benefits of implementing and using digital twin 
construction systems and methods to streamline the construction of tunnels with TBMs, as 
illustrated through a use case in a mechanized tunnelling construction project. The 
implemented algorithm achieved a 90% reduction in digital twin modelling time while 
enhancing lean design and construction workflows. By generating a dynamic representation of 
the tunnel’s physical state in short cycles, it provided the project team with timely, data-driven 
insights to support decision-making and process optimization.
A key insight derived from this experience is that defining and implementing systems with 
adequate data exchange standards to enable data interoperability among the various software 
products is essential for realizing the tangible benefits of BIM design and digital twin. Another 
is that despite imperfect data interoperability across the different systems involved, it is 
feasible to compile an accurate digital twin status model automatically using state of the art 
technology and innovative parametric design algorithms. The study also provides insights on 
how a purpose-built digital twin can support complex construction processes, such as tunnel 
segment lining and bracketry construction, thus improving the flow of tunnelling operations 
and avoiding the waste of rework by enabling error-free installation of systems. This is 
achieved by providing project construction and design teams with accurate information on 
current conditions in real-time.
Notably, the study establishes a crucial link between research and practical applications, 
building upon knowledge to formulate a novel approach grounded in a real-world project. 
Ultimately, this work contributes valuable insights for researchers and industry practitioners 
seeking to advance digital twin applications in tunnelling and offers a replicable framework 
that may be adapted for broader construction contexts. From a theoretical standpoint, it 
enhances our understanding of the positive interactions of BIM (and now DTC) and lean 
construction principles.
