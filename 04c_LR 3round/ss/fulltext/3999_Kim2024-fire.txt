A B S T R A C T
Modern large complex buildings, with their vast expanses and multipurpose usability, present diverse fire-
spread scenarios, necessitating precise awareness of fire situations. Despite numerous studies integrating
building information models with IoT and AI technologies to provide contextual information, there may
be limitations in recognizing potential risks under dynamic fire behaviors during urgent situations. This
paper presents a fire emergency management system based on an informative digital twin (IDT) to provide
instantaneous fire situational information. Utilizing IDT, the system gathers building-specific fire scenario
knowledge for immediate fire recognition. Leveraging this knowledge, the IDT assesses building fire risks and
shares safety information on egress routes and zones with fire-related applications. A simulation case study
revealed a fire recognition processing time of 0.88 s, acceptable for fire emergencies. Additionally, the IDT’s
risk assessment reduced the total evacuation time by 65 s and potential injuries by 406, demonstrating the
system’s effectiveness in fire emergency management.
1. Introduction
As modern buildings become increasingly large and complex, the
risk of fire hazards increases. Large complex buildings such as shop-
ping malls, airports, and underground shopping areas, bustling with
people, are prone to significant threats to both life and property during
fires [1]. In particular, these buildings, with their intricate structures
and varied uses across different sub-zones [ 2], present a dynamic
fire spread and potentially complicate the control and management
responses during such emergencies [ 3]. Survival rate in the event of
a fire relies heavily on fast on-site decision-making. However, building
characteristics can obscure the assessment of the situation during a fire,
increasing the difficulty for evacuees in making appropriate decisions.
This can lead to delayed evacuation and serious casualties. It is chal-
lenging for building occupants to promptly evacuate based solely on
simple fire alarms. Therefore, these buildings require an informative
fire-response management system that can provide detailed information
about the fire situation, including fire spread trends and potential risk
levels. This enables the evacuees to make informed decisions quickly
and formulate effective response strategies.

assist in identifying the source of a fire [ 9], thus minimizing delays in
the early stages of fire detection and recognition.
The problem of evacuation delays in complex buildings, owing to
their unique features, continues to be a challenge in the response phase
following recognition. Evacuees plan escape routes before evacuation
and firefighters develop strategies before conducting rescue operations.
In this context, information about the spread of fire and smoke is cru-
cial; however, because of the complex structure and various subzones
of buildings, it is challenging to predict fire-spread patterns. Current
fire scenes visualized in three-dimensional maps can assist; however,
effective response strategies must consider the future trajectory of fire

spread, which relies on individual experiences. There is a potential
deficiency in the prerequisite knowledge required to predict the dy-
namic spread of fire in large complex buildings for both evacuees
and firefighters [10]. This gap can lead to inefficient decisions and
delayed responses, potentially causing a severe loss of life [11]. Fire
simulations using computational fluid dynamics (CFD) can offer useful
information [12] but are currently limited mainly to post-incident
analysis and strategy development owing to constraints in execution
speed [13].
The structural complexity and large scale of buildings also present
challenges in decision-making during movement phases. Large complex
buildings typically have multiple exits and long and intricate escape
routes [14]. The occupants of large complex buildings, the majority
of which are often not residents, are unfamiliar with the building
layout, making it very difficult to navigate routes [15]. In the event
of a fire, evacuees must continually find and move along safe paths
until evacuation is complete. They cannot cross excessively hot areas,
which are filled with toxic gases and heavy smoke, impairing their
visibility. In other words, evacuees must identify and avoid areas
with a high potential fire risk while moving. Conversely, firefighters
may need to prioritize these high-risk areas to increase their rescue
efficiency. However, it is not easy for both groups to assess potential
risks, as this requires tracking and predicting changes in fire-related risk
factors over time, such as temperature variations and carbon monoxide
concentrations.
In summary, the characteristics of large complex buildings make
them prone to inefficient decision-making and response strategies,
potentially leading to a severe loss of life [16]. In such cases, training
within a building using various fire scenarios can aid in better decision-
making during fire emergencies. However, conducting realistic drills
in complex buildings is often challenging because of the large number
of temporary occupants and variety of factors to consider during a
fire. As mentioned, real-time predictions of fire spread and information
on potential hazards within a building can complement the knowl-
edge gained from training and help derive optimal response strategies.
However, this requires fire emergency management systems (FEMSs) to
overcome challenges related to simulation speed and to be capable of
rapidly tracking and predicting changes in fire risk factors.
As one of the technologies poised to address the aforementioned
issues, a digital twin has the potential to improve information man-
agement and decision-making in various sectors, including manufac-
turing [17], the automotive industry [18], and construction [19,20].
It overcomes the challenges of managing dynamic, large-scale data in
physical environments, which is a difficulty faced by BIM [21,22],
and augments system performance by integrating sophisticated data
analysis technologies, such as artificial intelligence (AI) [23] and sim-
ulation [24]. A pivotal aspect of digital twins is their utilization of
virtual models and environments, enabling experiments that surpass
the conventional limitations of reality, such as time, space, safety, and
cost [25]. The knowledge gained from these experiments can be used
to predict future changes and develop optimal response strategies [26].
Inspired by these capabilities, we devised a method to model and sim-
ulate various fire scenarios within virtual environments, gather critical
insights into potential fire situations, and utilize this information to
enhance decision-making in actual fire emergencies.
In this study, we proposed an informative digital twin (IDT)-based
FEMS specifically designed for large complex buildings. The system
employs fire-spread information and potential fire risk assessments to
overcome delays in fire response due to decision-making difficulties in
large complex structures.
The main contributions of this study are as follows:
(1) IDT-based Building Fire Emergency Management: We in-
troduce an IDT-based FEMS architecture capable of providing
fire-spread information and potential fire risk assessment in
large complex buildings. This architecture leverages a cloud–
edge computing framework for efficient handling of large-scale
data. The IDT, built upon object-oriented design principles, func-
tions at the edge and develops internal behavioral logic that
is essential for delivering the necessary information throughout
each phase of the fire response. It provides appropriate fire
situation information to fire-response entities and is responsible
for managing all data-processing activities.
(2) Unique Fire Knowledge Base Construction and Utilization:
We propose a mechanism using the IDT to create a building-
specific fire knowledge base, along with methods for its effective
utilization. Recognizing the computational challenges of CFD,
our system pre-simulates potential fire scenarios using the high-
resolution building model of the IDT. During fires, the system
employs a similarity comparison algorithm to compare real-time
sensor data with data extracted from the fire knowledge base,
thereby facilitating the immediate analysis of fire and smoke
propagation trends.
(3) Zone-based Risk Assessment Algorithm: We propose a zone-
based risk assessment algorithm based on the IDT designed to
support safe fire-response plans in the event of a fire. This
algorithm estimates the level of risk in different zones at specific
times, considering the dynamic changes in a fire in buildings
with complex structures and various utilized subzones. It uses
factors such as temperature and smoke and carbon monoxide
concentrations to assess risks. Evacuees can use this risk infor-
mation to safely evacuate within the available safe escape time,
thereby avoiding movement inefficiencies. Rescuers can use this
information to determine priorities for rescue operations.
2. Literature review
2.1. Building fire management systems
In the event of a fire, to ensure the safety of occupants, the to-
tal evacuation time, known as the required safe egress time (RSET),
must be shorter than the available safe egress time (ASET) [27]. This
principle applies to both general buildings and large complex build-
ings equally. The total evacuation time comprises the detection, pre-
movement, and movement times. Caution is necessary at all stages
because delays at any stage can lead to overall delays. Research has
been conducted on building fire management systems capable of pro-
viding appropriate fire situation information to assist individuals in
making prompt decisions throughout each phase of the evacuation
process.
The fire alarm system within a building provides information about
a fire at the detection stage, and with the advent of Internet of Things
(IoT) technology, it has become an essential piece of equipment within
buildings [28]. Fire alarms are critical because they serve as primary in-
dicators of fire presence, marking the beginning of emergency response
actions. However, the effectiveness of such alarms can be compromised
by frequent false alarms, which lead to public skepticism and potential
evacuation delays. To address this issue, researchers [7,8] have applied
AI to analyze patterns in fire sensor data to identify uncertainties and
reduce the likelihood of false alarms. Another approach has enhanced
the reliability of alerts by using a combination of sophisticated sensors,
including smoke detectors and cameras, and leveraging an ensemble of
sensory data to improve the detection accuracy [6].
Numerous studies have been conducted to provide information on
fire situations, even during the post-detection stage. The authors of [29]
aimed to support the development of evacuation strategies in the pre-
movement stage using a BIM model to visualize fire situations collected
from sensor data. In [30], the authors addressed the challenge of
evacuees struggling to understand a fire environment by introducing an
augmented reality (AR) device. This device facilitated awareness of the
fire situation by displaying virtual arrows and providing voice guidance
for evacuees along the shortest safe route to an exit. The research
presented in [31] revealed that utilizing virtual reality (VR)/AR devices

the cognitive ability of individuals to navigate dangerous situations

Although numerous studies have shown the potential of delivering
essential fire situation information to stakeholders during fire inci-
dents, addressing problems that may arise in large complex buildings
remains a challenge. As discussed previously, the essence of ensuring
safety during a fire lies in reducing the total evacuation time by
minimizing delays at every stage [34]. It is not merely about the
initial alarms activated by the building’s detection systems; there is
a pressing need to enhance fire safety features and offer appropriate
information about fire situations, including visual information on fire
situations, escape routes, etc. Although various research efforts have
demonstrated the feasibility of supplying such information at different
stages, a lack of systematic integration among technologies may result
in erroneous decisions propelled by potential inaccuracies and misinfor-
mation [35]. The complex nature of large buildings requires managing
a substantial volume of disaster-prevention data, which exacerbates
these challenges. The capacity of technologies such as the narrowband
IoT (NB-IoT) to support up to 200,000 devices is promising [36];
however, effectively processing the vast amounts of data generated
during a fire to derive relevant information at each stage presents a
distinct challenge.
The occurrence of complex fire patterns owing to intricate structures
and various subzones complicates decision-making for evacuees and
firefighters alike [15]. During a building fire, more than half the
occupants reported feeling deprived of the necessary data for making
informed decisions [37]. Similarly, over 83% of firefighters reported
a lack of critical decision-making information. This information short-
age regarding fire situations exacerbates decision-making challenges
in complex fire patterns. Additionally, large buildings with complex
layouts, typically featuring multiple evacuation routes, inherently ex-
perience prolonged evacuation times [38]. Ensuring the safety of any
chosen route throughout the evacuation process is difficult owing to
the changing conditions [14]. Under these circumstances, it is advan-
tageous to develop an evacuation strategy that includes the prediction
of fire and smoke spread trends based on comprehensive fire simulation
analyses.
For instance, in [39], a fire spread model based on a fire dynamics
simulator (FDS) was developed considering the complex nature of
skyscrapers and the rapid spread of fires. This model used the analyzed
data to address the challenges of fire prevention and determine evac-
uation strategies for occupants. Similarly, in the context of wildfires,
where the fire trajectory is influenced by diverse materials at the
ignition source, fire simulation results play a pivotal role in shaping
response strategies by predicting fire spread and behavior [40]. Analyz-
ing changes in temperature, smoke, and carbon monoxide during fire
situations through simulations, as shown in a series of studies, can help
identify potential hazards and develop optimal strategies. However,
CFD-based fire simulations can be time consuming, often requiring
hours or even days [13], which limits their immediate use during a
fire event. This limitation becomes more evident as the complexity of
the model increases.
2.2. Digital twins in disaster safety
A digital twin is a virtual representation that mirrors a potential
or actual physical entity spanning from the micro-atomic level to the
macro-geometrical level [41]. By utilizing data analysis technologies,
including AI, digital twins process large datasets to enhance the moni-
toring, prediction, diagnosis, and optimization of physical systems [19].
Although they may be confused with BIM models, digital twins are
distinct in their focus on replicating an existing physical entity and their
ability to represent the real-time condition of a physical system through
connectivity [21]. This real-time data integration capability supports
dynamic decision-making and optimization, distinguishing digital twins
from BIM models. The functionalities of data management and decision
support provided by digital twins present significant opportunities for
improving the efficiency of construction and asset management [42].
Furthermore, their recent deployment in disaster and safety manage-
ment, leveraging these features, has attracted significant attention [20,
43].
As information structures, digital twins excel in the lifecycle man-
agement of buildings and disaster management systems by collecting
and analyzing real-time data. This is particularly apparent in disaster
safety management, such as that of fire accident scenarios. For instance,
Khajavi et al. [44] utilized digital twins to enhance safety protocols
throughout a building’s lifecycle by focusing on fire detection and
anomaly identification. Wang et al. [45] emphasized that the effective-
ness of a digital twin lies in its capacity to faithfully represent data and
their meanings, and to harness the entirety of these data for knowledge
processing. A recent study [46] investigated the integration of digital
twins and semantic web technologies for the intelligent management of
building fire prevention systems, developing digital twins that combine
static building geometric data with dynamic sensor data for proactive
fire prevention management.
By facilitating information processing, digital twins can be inte-
grated with advanced analytical tools, such as AI and simulation, to
provide stakeholders with the analyzed information they need. Zhang
et al. [47] proposed an AI-based digital fire framework to develop a
digital twin that can provide detailed information such as temperature
profiles within buildings, fire locations, and fire sizes. This system,
named AID-Fire, is expected to be utilized for fire-trend prediction
and evacuation guidance based on AI technology, demonstrating the
potential to provide analyzed information within buildings for fire
response. Ding et al. [23] identified a lack of real-time dynamic evac-
uation information, such as the location of occupants and the status
of firefighters during the evacuation and rescue stages, as one of
the main causes of casualties in building fires. Their study explored
smart human monitoring technology based on digital twins using AI,
which employed a Convolutional Neural Network(CNN) -based evacuee
detector and multi-object tracking algorithm to assist firefighters in
accurately locating evacuees.
Simulation-based prediction and analysis are the core aspects of
digital twin technology [48,49]. Leveraging this capability, research
has been conducted to offer predictive insights into disaster safety
domains, including smart cities and wildfires. Ford et al. [50] pro-
posed a digital-twin-based smart disaster management system known
as SCDT by integrating simulation technology across various smart city
infrastructures. The authors highlighted that the SCDT could virtually
navigate the disaster management cycle using simulations, enhance
predictions of expected action outcomes, and facilitate feedback on the
ramifications of the actions implemented. Yun et al. [25] developed
a novel digital twin framework designed to enhance the precision of
disaster monitoring and prediction and mitigate the impact of nat-
ural disasters. In this framework, they introduced a hybrid disaster
model that combined a physics-based model with a data-driven error
correction model to improve disaster prediction accuracy.
Although several studies have been conducted using digital twins,
research on their use to solve fire-response scenarios is less common.
Moreover, many of these studies do not provide a comprehensive
view of the entire process but rather narrow the focus to specific
response steps [7]. Such a fragmented approach can make it difficult
to adequately provide the required information at different stages of a
fire emergency. In addition, as has been repeatedly emphasized, FEMSs
rely on intelligent tools for prediction and analysis, and to be effectively
utilized in emergency fire scenarios, they must overcome limitations in
processing speed.

2.3. Goals of the study
This study aims to explore the design and implementation of an
FEMS that leverages an IDT to provide appropriate fire situation in-
formation during the fire-response stage. The main research questions
(RQs) are as follows:
RQ1 How can we develop an FEMS that provides fire situation in-
formation to support rapid decision-making by fire stakehold-
ers throughout the steps of a fire emergency in large complex
buildings?
RQ2 What are the possible approaches to obtaining immediate in-
formation on fire and smoke spread trends during ongoing fire
situations rather than post-event?
RQ3 How can potential risk information from dynamic fire patterns
be derived to support the establishment of safe evacuation and
rescue strategies for large complex buildings?
3. IDT-based building fire emergency management
3.1. Overall architecture
The objective of the IDT-based FEMS is to assist stakeholders in
making timely and effective decisions during fire emergencies. Fig. 1
shows the architecture of the system. This system employs a strategy
of knowledge accumulation and discovery to overcome the limitations
associated with the processing speed of CFD simulations. Prior to a fire
incident, fire safety experts design and simulate various potential fire
scenarios tailored to the building. In the event of an actual fire, the
system leverages the IDT to search among these pre-simulated scenarios
for those that closely align with the ongoing fire conditions. These
selected scenarios are then used to track and predict dynamic changes
in the fire situation, enabling stakeholders to rapidly make informed
decisions.
The IDT is a comprehensive digital representation of a physical
building that plays a pivotal role in building fire emergency manage-
ment. The IDT-based system is designed to utilize cloud–edge comput-
ing, enabling the efficient processing of vast amounts of data generated
by various fire safety equipment in buildings.
(1) Cloud-computing layer. The cloud-computing layer offers com-
puting resources for processing high-cost functional entities
(FEs), handling vast fire knowledge bases, and executing build-
ing fire emergency management applications. Equipped with
high-performance computing and ample storage space, cloud-
computing servers enable fire experts to design and simulate
numerous potential building fire scenarios with high resolution.
To accommodate numerous users, FEMS applications are hosted
on the cloud for efficient access.
(2) Edge-computing layer. The IDT operates within the edge-
computing layer and processes raw sensor data to derive ap-
propriate fire situation information for applications. By prepro-
cessing the data streams generated by numerous sensors in a
building at the edge, the IDT eliminates the need to send unnec-
essary data to the cloud, thereby avoiding bandwidth waste that
could occur as the number of buildings managed by the system
increases [51,52]. The IDT follows predefined internal response
logic steps to derive the fire situation information required by
the applications. Because the IDT is responsible for executing the
necessary processes at each stage of a fire emergency, tracking
the progress and timing of the fire response is straightforward.
(3) Device layer. The device layer comprises a multimodal sens-
ing network powered by various IoT sensing devices spread
throughout a building. These devices collect critical sensor data
regarding the physical building environment, including temper-
ature, smoke density, and carbon monoxide levels. These sensor
data are relayed to the edge-computing layer and used to analyze
the building fire situation.

3.2.1. IDT attributes
IDT attributes are a collection of information that represents the
physical building status from a multidimensional perspective. These
attributes are categorized as static or dynamic. Static attributes, which
include the BIM model and sensor metadata (e.g. location and type),
remain constant unless they are manually modified. These static at-
tributes are used to design and simulate potential fire scenarios. In
contrast, dynamic attributes adjust over time in response to changes
in a building’s status and primarily emerge during a fire emergency.
Dynamic attributes are categorized into the following three parts:
• Present status. The present status represents the building’s cur-
rent status, which includes the temperature, smoke, and carbon
monoxide data observed by IoT sensor devices.
• Potential status. The potential status denotes the possible status
of the building from the present to the future. It includes vir-
tual sensor data synchronized with real sensors and fire hazard
factors representing the expansion and migration of fire products
such as gas temperature, smoke visibility, and carbon monoxide
concentration.
• Projected status. The projected status represents the potential risk
information derived from the potential status matched to a real
fire scenario. For each egress route and zone, the projected status
includes the available time, required time, and safety margin for
safe evacuation and rescue.
According to the operation of IDT behaviors during a fire emer-
gency, the present, potential, and projected statuses are linked together,
as shown in 2. Assuming that a fire ignited from location L7, the present
status attribute holds the real sensor data from the time of fire detection
to the current point in time 𝑡𝑐. The potential status attribute contains
numerous potential statuses discovered from the fire knowledge base,
each of which contains virtual sensor data from the time the virtual fire
was ignited to the end of the virtual fire scenario. Because each virtual
sensor has a position and type synchronized with a real sensor, it is
possible to discover potential statuses that match reality by comparing
the sensor data generated by each sensor. The potential status includes
fire hazard factors that vary with fire dynamics and are distributed
throughout the internal area of the building. Fire hazard factors are
represented as spatiotemporal data, including gas temperature, CO
concentration, and smoke visibility, and are utilized to assess the fire
risk in each area. From the discovered potential status, the projected
status represents the risk level at the current time and the remaining
time to the next risk level. As the aligned potential status includes
fire hazard factors in the future, the remaining time for the next risk
level can be derived. For example, at the current time 𝑡𝑐, the egress
routes from location L9 to all exits are tenable, and there is sufficient
time to prepare for movement and escape. At a point in the future
after time 𝑡𝑓 1, only one escape route is tenable; hence, it must be used
to escape as quickly as possible within a limited time. Therefore, the
preparation movement action must be performed within 𝑡𝑓 1. Similarly,
after 𝑡𝑓 1+𝑡𝑓 2, no egress routes are available; therefore, the escape action
must begin within 𝑡𝑓 2 at time 𝑡𝑐 + 𝑡𝑓 1.
3.2.2. IDT behaviors and functional entities
IDT behaviors comprise a set of processes designed to support
fire emergency management by automatically responding to dynamic
changes in the status of a physical building. These behaviors are orga-
nized into a state machine with five states: idle, detection, recognition,
response, and treatment. As the status of the physical building changes,
the IDT seamlessly transitions its behavioral states and activates the FEs
associated with the current state. FEs are computing components that
are invoked according to behavioral logic and process data according
to each function and purpose by interacting with the fire knowledge
base and IDT attributes. These FEs are strategically deployed to ensure
smooth operation of IDT behaviors. Considering the computing and
storage space requirements of each FE, some FEs with high computa-
tional costs are integrated into the cloud-computing layer, and other
FEs that have low computational costs and are forced in real time are
integrated into the IDT in the edge-computing layer.

3.2.3. IDT interfaces
IDT interfaces are communication modules that facilitate commu-
nication between an IDT and its external components. Depending on
the connection target, IDT interfaces are categorized as digital twin to
physical things (DT2PT), digital twin to functional entities (DT2FE),
and digital twin to application (DT2APP) interfaces. The DT2PT inter-
face supports connectivity between the IDT and the physical buildings.
It receives measured data from numerous IoT sensors distributed in
large complex buildings and stores the obtained sensor data in the IDT’s
present status attribute. The DT2FE interface supports information
exchange between the IDT and the high-cost FEs of the cloud-based
building FEMS. Through the DT2FE interface, the IDT invokes the
knowledge discovery FEs and receives relevant fire knowledge, and
the fire scenario modeling and simulation (M&S) FE accesses the static
attributes of the IDT. The DT2APP interface supports building fire
emergency management applications in accessing the IDT attribute
information. Through the DT2APP interface, various building fire emer-
gency management applications can access the IDT attributes and
receive appropriate information according to their purpose. A detailed
implementation of the IDT interfaces is described in Sections 4.1 and
4.2.
4. System development
4.1. Development Kubernetes/KubeEdge-based FEMS system
To implement the edge-cloud system architecture presented in Sec-
tion 3.1, we utilized Kubernetes [ 53] and KubeEdge [ 54] to construct
a cloud–edge computing environment, as illustrated in Fig. 4 . In our
environment, the cloud consisted of two servers, and the edge consisted
of one x86 PC. The IDT process, along with the low-cost FEs, runs at the
edge, whereas fire management applications and high-cost FEs operate
in the cloud. Parallel processing can be optionally performed for faster
speed enhancement in simulations and knowledge discovery when nec-
essary. The Kubernetes controller can automatically place containers
based on the resource demand and availability, thus making the parallel
processing configuration easier. The knowledge DB is managed using a
persistent volume subsystem, and the processes in the cluster can be
accessed by the DB.
Considering the real-time communication between the IDT and
high-cost FEs, processes running inside containers are interconnected
based on the Data Distribution Service (DDS) [55]. The deliverability of
data can be efficiently managed by considering quality of service (QoS)
factors, such as deadlines. IoT sensor data are collected through an
edge broker server based on the Message Queuing Telemetry Transport
(MQTT) protocol [ 56], and the processed IDT attributes are provided
to the FEMS applications through a REST API server.
4.2. Development of object-oriented informative digital twin
A UML class diagram of the developed IDT is shown in Fig. 5 (a).

To store and manage the IDT attributes data, we used SQLite [57],
which is a lightweight and server-less relational database engine that
supports efficient data management in resource-constrained edge en-
vironments. The data structure of the IDT attributes is constructed
based on the definitions of dynamic (present, potential, and projected
statuses) and static attributes. The present status continuously stores
real-time data observed from various sensors distinguished by the sen-

fire knowledge composed of virtual sensor data and fire hazard factors,
each identified by a knowledge ID. Sensor data for virtual sensor data
are designed to match the sensor data structure of the present status
for seamless similarity measurements. Fire hazard factors, represented
in two dimensions, consist of multiple two-dimensional simulation data
entries stored over time for each (x, y) coordinate. The projected status
includes zone risk information, which is distinguished from the zone
ID. Each zone risk information contains multiple route risk information
entries, which store available route egress time (ARET), required route
egress time (RRET), and safety margin (SM) data for escape routes
between specific exits (exit ID) and within the zone (zone ID), whereas
the static attribute manages sensor meta information, which includes
the location (x, y) and type of one or more sensors for designing poten-
tial fire scenarios. It also manages information on one or more egress
routes, representing the trajectories of escape routes and distances, as
well as the file path corresponding to the BIM model. Fig. 5(b) shows
the developed IDT attribute based on SQLite.
The IDT behavior was modeled using the Business Process Model
and Notation (BPMN) [58], which is an Object Management Group
(OMG) standard for specifying processes in a workflow. The IDT be-
havior contains four states, namely, detection, recognition, response,
and treatment, and invokes each FE component according to its cur-
rent state, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The detailed operation of the FE
components is presented in Section 4.3.
To implement the DT2PT, DT2FE, and DT2APP interfaces, we uti-
lized the MQTT, DDS, and REST API, respectively. In DT2PT interface,
each real sensor data is structured as {datetime, data} and col-
lected through the MQTT topic of sensordata/<sensor_id>. In
the DT2FE interface, the static attributes required to generate potential
fire scenarios on the cloud side are transmitted through the DDS
topic in the static_attribute/, and requests and responses for
discovering similar fire knowledge are transmitted via the discov-
ered_knowledge/ DDS topic. For the DT2APP interface, REST APIs
are designed to access the IDT attributes, as shown in Table 1.
4.3. Development of core FEs and fire knowledge base
4.3.1. Building fire scenario modeling and simulation
The goal of building fire scenario modeling and FE simulation is to
construct an extensive fire knowledge base for fire recognition. This
is achieved by simulating a range of potential fire scenarios within
a building, considering its physical attributes and different fire con-
ditions. By accumulating various potential fire scenarios in the fire
knowledge base, IDT systems have more opportunities to identify po-
tential fire scenarios that more closely reflect reality, thereby improving
the fire recognition accuracy.
To simulate building fire dynamics, we utilized an FDS, which
was developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) and is primarily used in the fire safety domain. In the modeling
phase, the BIM model and sensor information in the static attributes
of the IDT are used to reflect the physical properties of the building.
The development of Industry Foundation Classes (IFC), which is an
open data model standard (ISO 16739), facilitates the interoperability
between BIM and the FDS. Building fire scenario modeling is performed
using the following steps:
1. The BIM model is imported into BlenderFDS, a third-party FDS
modeling tool for converting IFC to an FDS. As a result of
the conversion, the building geometry is represented by OBST
symbols in the FDS.
2. The location and properties of the virtual sensor devices are
defined based on actual sensor information. In the FDS model,
virtual sensor devices are represented as DEVC symbols with
locations specified by XYZ coordinates and properties specified
by QUANTITY parameter.
3. The thermal properties of the building material are defined
based on the ASTM E84 Fire Standards [59]. The thermal proper-
ties are represented as MATL symbols and applied to the surface
(SURF) of the building geometry (OBST).
4. The combustion conditions of a virtual building fire are defined
by specifying the fire location and reaction. The location of the
fire is specified by the VENT symbol, and the reaction of the fire
is represented by the REAC symbol with the type of fuel.
After the modeling process is completed, slice script blocks (SLCF)
are added to the FDS model to analyze the two-dimensional spatial
distribution of the gas temperature, carbon monoxide concentration,
and smoke visibility.
In the simulation phase, the FDS software is run using the FDS
model developed above. According to script blocks DEVC and SLCF,
the FDS software output virtual sensor data and fire hazard factors, re-
spectively. The virtual sensor data are represented as one-dimensional
time-series data corresponding to each IoT sensing device, which has
measurements of temperature, smoke density, and carbon monoxide.
Conversely, the fire hazard factors are expressed as two-dimensional
spatiotemporal data, where each axis represents the x-coordinate, y-
coordinate, and time. Fig. 6 shows an example of fire hazard factors
(gas temperature, carbon monoxide concentration, and smoke visibil-
ity) at time points (a) 60 s, and (b) 300 s. The fire hazard factors are
then used to assess the fire risk level and transition time by determining
the ARET and RRET.
The fire knowledge base was constructed based on TimescaleDB, a
time-series database, to efficiently store and manage the derived time-
varying virtual sensor data and fire hazard factors. TimeScaleDB, which
is specifically optimized for time-series data, offers efficient data inges-
tion and querying performance, which are essential for dealing with
large volumes of continuously evolving data. Its automatic data par-
titioning and time-based query optimization significantly enhance the
query performance and data management. Moreover, TimeScaleDB’s
ability to scale effortlessly and its advanced data compression and
retention policies make it ideally suited for building an extensive fire
knowledge base, ensuring the efficient storage and rapid retrieval of

4.3.2. Building fire status similarity measurement

by invoking the knowledge discovery FE, a fixed amount of candi-
date fire knowledge with high similarity is discovered from the fire
knowledge base and imported to the potential status attributes. Subse-
quently, the building fire status similarity measure FE identifies the fire
knowledge with the highest similarity to the potential status attributes.
In this process, the potential status attributes act as a type of cache
memory, and the similarity measure FE can be performed with a low
computational overhead owing to the fixed-size search space.

5. Case study
To determine whether the proposed IDT-based FEMS can support
fire responses in large-scale complex buildings (to answer RQ1), we
conducted a case study focusing on an underground shopping mall. This
case study also examined the effectiveness of fire knowledge discovery
algorithms (to answer RQ2) and evaluated the performance of the
zone-based risk assessment algorithm (to answer RQ3).
5.1. Case building description
The BIM model of the large complex building adopted in this case
study was obtained from the Hoehyeon Underground Shopping Center
in Jung-gu, Seoul, Korea. As shown in Fig. 7(a), the total building area
is approximately 9031 m 2, with an average height of 2.4 m, width
of 166 m, and length of 58 m. A total of 987 IoT sensor devices,
including smoke density, carbon monoxide, and temperature sensors,
are installed in each room and hallway. In this model, 19 zones are
considered, which are connected to eight escape exits through egress
routes (light green lines).
5.2. Preparation of fire knowledge base and test scenarios
To construct a rich fire knowledge base, numerous virtual fire
scenarios were modeled and simulated considering various types of
fires, as indicated in Table 5. As shown in Fig. 7(b), the initial locations
of the virtual fires (depicted by red rectangles) were distributed in each
room (216 points) and corridor (38 points). For each fire-initiation
location, five fire classes were considered according to the NIST fire
classification: ordinary combustible materials (Class A), flammable liq-
uids (Class B), electrical equipment (Class C), combustible metals (Class
D), and cooking oils (Class K). In addition, fire sizes of 0.5 m × 0 .5 m ,
1.0 m × 1 .0 m, and 2.0 m × 2 .0 m were applied to model the virtual fire
scenarios. Accordingly, a total of 3810 (254 locations × five fire classes
× three fire sizes) virtual fire scenarios were modeled and simulated to
construct the fire knowledge base.
The experiments on the IDT-based FEMS were conducted with a
number of test scenarios, assuming that fires occurred inside rooms and
corridors, as shown by the yellow rectangle in Fig. 7(b). The detailed
settings of the test scenarios are listed in Table 6. Considering safety
and site constraints, we constructed simulation test scenarios instead
of creating actual fire situations so that the sensor data acquired from
these test scenario simulations were considered real sensor data.

6.1. Analysis of IDT-based FEMS on test scenarios
In Test Scenario A, it was assumed that a fire of Class A and size
1.0 × 1.0 initiated in Room 1, and 30 s passed since it was detected.
From the recognition state, the IDT sequentially invoked the knowledge
discovery, and building state similarity measure FEs to discover fire
knowledge similar to the current building state. This discovery process
took approximately 1 s on a Core i-9 3.5 GHz, thus satisfying the
need to perform in near real time during a fire emergency. Table 7
lists the top five similar fire knowledge derived through online DTW-
based similarity measurements. These represented the fire scenarios
that occurred in Room 1. For the smallest DTW distance of 5.99, the
most similar fire knowledge was recognized as a fire of Class A and size
1.0 × 1.0, which was identical to Test Scenario A.
In the response state, the ARET for each route was first calculated
by comparing the untenability criteria with the recognized fire hazard
factors using Table 4 and Eq. (5). Fig. 8 shows the untenable time of
the area and some examples of the calculated ARET in Zones 1 and
19. As the fire spread, starting from the top-left corner, the untenable
time showed low values near Room 1 and increased in the bottom-right
direction. Accordingly, the calculated ARETs were less than 24 s in
Zone 1, whereas they were more than 1000 s in Zone 19. For egress
routes between Zone 1 and Exits 1, 2, and 5, the ARETs were 10 s, 20
s, and 24 s, respectively, indicating that the egress routes will become
untenable in that order.
From the calculated ARETs for all egress routes, the RRET, SM, and
safest routes were calculated using Eqs. (6), (11) and (12), respectively.

potential fire scenarios is necessary.

6.2. Effect of online DTW-based fire knowledge discovery
The proposed IDT-based FEMS utilizes a knowledge accumulation
and discovery approach to leverage computationally intensive fire sim-
ulations in emergencies. To evaluate the effectiveness of Online DTW-
based fire knowledge discovery, we compared the processing times of
the fire simulation and similarity measurement algorithms, as shown in
Table 11. This evaluation was conducted on a Core i-9 3.5 GHz com-
puting system, and similarity measurement algorithms were performed
under the following conditions: 𝑘 = 3810 fire knowledge, length of real
sensor data 𝑁 = 10, and length of virtual sensor data 𝑀 = 3600.
As shown in Table 11, the processing time of the fire simulation
utilizing the FDS exceeded 8 h, indicating that conducting such sim-
ulations during a fire emergency is impractical. Although the original
DTW required a relatively low processing time (781.71 s) in the simi-
larity measurement, which is not trivial for application in time-critical
situations where fire recognition must be performed within a short
period of time after a fire is detected. However, the proposed online
DTW significantly reduced the processing time to 37.68 s by calculating
the DTW cost matrix in a cumulative manner, and the processing time
could be further minimized to 0.88 s by introducing parallel processing.
During a fire, the length 𝑁 of the real sensor data progressively in-
creased, leading to a notable expansion in the processing time disparity
between the original and online DTW, as illustrated in Fig. 10. Because
the time complexity of the original DTW was 𝑂(𝑘𝑁𝑀 ), the processing
time increased exponentially, requiring a significant time of 12 493.85
s at 𝑁 = 50. In contrast, online DTW showed a constant processing time
of 37.77 s on average when calculating the cost matrix by accumulating
a 1× 𝑀 partial cost matrix at each time instead of calculating the entire
cost matrix of 𝑁 × 𝑀. Accordingly, online DTW can support real-time
fire recognition with a consistent processing time that is not affected
by the size of 𝑁.
6.3. Impact of zone-based risk assessment
To evaluate the impact of zone-based risk assessment, this study
performed an evacuation simulation using Pathfinder [66]. The default
settings of Pathfinder were used as parameters for all personal char-
acteristics, and the change in movement speed due to fire in Eq. (7)
was applied to the entire space. Fig. 11 illustrates a comparison of
the evacuation time and injuries between escaping via the shortest and
safest routes with varying occupant capacities (500, 1000, 2000, and
3000) in Test Scenario A.
The evacuation through the shortest routes required 94.3, 136.3,
227.0, and 323.5 s, whereas evacuation through the safest routes
required shorter times of 85.8, 109.5, 175.0, and 258.5 s, respectively.
Accordingly, the selection of the safest route could save up to 65 s
when there were 3000 occupants in the building. This effect was mainly
relevant to the occupants in Zones 1–3. In evacuation via the shortest
route, occupants encountering the fire-spread path at Exit 1 experi-
enced significant delays owing to the impact of the fire, prolonging
the evacuation process. Conversely, in evacuation via the safest route,
occupants quickly completed the evacuation by avoiding the fire-spread
path as much as possible. Evacuation via the shortest route resulted
in 73, 158, 366, and 708 injuries, whereas evacuation via the safest
route resulted in 32, 73, 146, and 302 injuries, respectively. Utilizing
the safest routes rather than the shortest routes reduced the number
of injuries by up to 406. This is because the shortest route becomes
untenable in Zones 1, 2, and 3 during evacuation, whereas the safest
routes with a high ARET are relatively tenable for a long time.

and developed an IDT-based FEMS. The system is based on an IDT,
which has attributes, behaviors, and interfaces, and adopts a cloud–
edge computing structure to manage the vast data generated in the
building [51,52]. Through a case study of the Hoehyeon underground
shopping mall, we examined in detail how situational information is

Trends in fire and smoke spread provide crucial information for
fire responses. However, owing to the long processing time of CFD
simulations, this information is mainly used only in post-incident anal-

accumulate various fire scenarios through simulations and introduced
a strategy to extract and use the scenario that was most similar to
the ongoing fire situation. In this process, we proposed fire knowledge
discovery algorithms that can find a similar scenario within 0.88 s. This
overcomes the simulation execution speed issue, which requires over
8 h, enabling real-time utilization (Section 6.2). This answers the RQ2.
During a fire, safe areas at the current time can become hazardous
as the fire spreads. Large complex buildings take longer to evacuate
and rescue from than regular buildings. Therefore, potential risks must
be considered to ensure safe evacuation and prompt rescue. Based on
the guidelines in [ 61–63], we proposed a zone-based risk assessment
algorithm that considers three fire-related risk factors. This algorithm
calculates the safety margin time for each zone, enabling fire stake-
holders to respond safely by considering the potential risks. Evacuation
considering potential hazards resulted in a promising outcome, reduc-
ing the evacuation time by 65 s and decreasing the number of injuries
by 406 compared to the shortest-path-based evacuation (Section 6.3 ).
This answered RQ3.
Despite the promising results of the IDT-based FEMS, this study has
three major limitations that must be addressed in future studies.
First, as the number of buildings managed by the proposed sys-
tem increases and the number of sensors within the buildings in-
creases, there is a potential burden on computing and network re-
sources that could lead to system malfunctions [ 67]. We believe that
the issue of computing resources can be mitigated by prioritizing
critical tasks using container-based orchestrators [68], which are incor-
porated into our system design for efficient resource deployment and
management. Running an IDT at the edge can reduce resource strain
by saving network bandwidth; however, if urgent data transmission is
not guaranteed, malfunctions may still occur. Applying priority-based
networks [ 69] or time-sensitive networking technologies [ 70] to the
network infrastructure of the system is a potential solution.
Second, the accuracy of the fire and smoke spread trends derived by
the system depends on the knowledge accumulated in the knowledge
base. An underdeveloped knowledge base can lead to failure of the fire
response. We believe that future studies can construct a sufficiently
developed knowledge base to handle various fire scenarios using the
following techniques: (1) Setting a DTW distance threshold to alert
experts regarding insufficient fire scenarios, prompting the generation
of relevant scenarios, and (2) automatically generating fire scenarios
using generative AI technologies [71]. The proposed system allows for
large-scale knowledge base construction in the cloud and facilitates the
replacement and updating of IDT FEs, making it easy to adopt these
strategies and technologies.

Third, the fire risk assessment in this study has room for improve-
ment as it was conducted in limited situations. The study considered
only a limited number of fire risk factors during the risk assessment and
assumed an average speed for human movement when calculating the
RRET. This approach does not account for obstacles and blocked paths
within buildings or individual physical characteristics such as age and
disability. These limitations can be addressed by updating the fire risk
assessment FE in the proposed system. For instance, in the former case,
we can reflect the changes in building conditions perceived through
spatial monitoring [72] in the ARET calculation (Eq. (5)) or derive the
impact of state changes on the evacuation speed and incorporate it into
the RRET calculation (Eq. (7)). In the latter case, we can optimize the
evacuation and rescue plans by providing personalized risk assessment
information based on detecting and tracking evacuees [23] within the
building, considering individual differences rather than the average
speed.
8. Conclusion
This paper presented an IDT-based FEMS for fire emergency man-
agement in large complex buildings. The IDT-based FEMS seamlessly
integrates the cloud, edge, and device layers to ensure immediate access
to fire information during emergencies. The IDT of the edge layer, built
with an object-oriented design, efficiently gathers and processes data
on the physical state of a building measured from the device layer.
This process adheres to behavioral logic and encompasses detection,
recognition, response, and treatment. These are executed using FEs that
provide the necessary fire information for each phase of emergency
management. For real-time fire recognition, the IDT-based FEMS uses a
strategic approach for knowledge accumulation and discovery. In this
approach, computationally intensive fire modeling and simulations can
be executed in the cloud layer of the FEMS. Simultaneously, the edge-
based IDT discerns similarities in the simulated fire knowledge relevant
to the current building status. The similarity measurement between the
real and simulated virtual sensor data employs an efficient online DTW
algorithm. The IDT utilizes discovered fire knowledge to evaluate fire
risks in various zones within a building by introducing metrics such as
ARET, RRET, and SM between specific zones and exits. This enables
the IDT to provide information on potential risks by zone, along with
fire and smoke spread trends. We expect that the proposed IDT-based
FEMS will significantly enhance building fire emergency management
by providing crucial fire information during emergencies.

Appendix A. FEMS applications
Fig. A.12 shows the FEMS application provided by the IDT-based
FEMS. To construct a fire knowledge base, fire experts model poten-
tial fire scenarios using Blender, a fire-scenario design application, as
shown in Fig. A.12(a). Blender is an open-source three-dimensional
modeling software that supports BIM model import in IFC format,
FDS modeling, and FDS file export by applying the BlenderBIM and
BlenderFDS plugins. When the fire scenario modeling is completed, the
fire experts send the modeled FDS extension file to the Building Fire
Emergency Manager through FDS Upload Web, as shown in the bottom-
right corner of Fig. A.12(a). The Fire Scenario M&S FEs then run the
FDS software using the FDS file and store the simulation results in the
fire knowledge base.
Existing building fire-monitoring applications primarily provide vi-
sualized information by overlaying sensor data collected in real time
on a virtual building. However, this only shows the current state of
fire spread, making it difficult to preemptively understand the complex
fire-spread patterns in complex buildings. Fig. A.12(b) shows an FEMS
application that can be used in the fire recognition and response
stages, visualizing the two-dimensional fire hazard factor information
of the most similar fire knowledge through the web. In a fire situation,
evacuees and rescuers can use the scrollbar at the bottom of the image
to understand future fire spread trends in advance, allowing them to
derive an optimal response strategy.
Fig. A.12(c) shows a structure support FEMS application utilizing
IDT-based risk assessment results. Based on the risk assessment, the
application displays the risk level of each area to help firefighters
establish an efficient rescue strategy during the response and treatment
stages. The risk level of each zone, derived through each area’s safety
margin, is categorized into three levels, ‘‘E1’’, ‘‘E2’’, and ‘‘E3’’, and is
marked in the zones with red, yellow, and green labels, respectively.
Firefighters can use this to prioritize areas with higher risk and proceed
with rescue operations accordingly.
Fig. A.12(d) shows an FEMS application for evacuees that uses IDT-
based risk assessment results. Similarly, the risk level information of
each zone, derived from each area’s safety margin, is used to calculate
safe evacuation routes for evacuees. The AR application running on
the smartphone displays the current location of the evacuee and the
calculated safe route.
