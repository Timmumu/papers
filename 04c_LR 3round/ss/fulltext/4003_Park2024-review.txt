ABSTRACT  
The recent advancement in digital twin technology, which creates virtual replicas of real-world processes, offers 
an interactive testbed for understanding and predicting environmental changes. As pluvial flood damage esca -
lates globally in urban areas, there remains a gap in understanding the most effective collaboration between 
governments and local residents for sustainable flood risk management. To address this gap, we develop a 
participatory framework for urban pluvial flood modeling, incorporating open source software, virtual reality, 
minimum viable product, and gamification components. This framework engages citizens in every phase of the 
participatory modeling process, from input data preparation, through hydrological model construction, to model 
verification, and experiments. We present a case study on the recurring pluvial flood damages in South Korea ’ s 
Gangnam region, demonstrating the practical implications of an interactive, web-based crowdsourcing platform 
to leverage community engagement and local knowledge. The results underscore the evidence of a proactive role 
for citizens, not merely as recipients of disaster information but as key contributors collaborating with a range of 
stakeholders in stormwater management and modeling. Combining digital twin technology with citizen partic -
ipation can empower informed decision-making and collective actions in the evolving digital era, leading to a 
disaster-resilient community.   
1. Introduction 
A Digital Twin, defined as a virtual replica of physical entities, pro -
cesses, and systems in the real world, has burgeoned with the rapid 
advancement of environmental monitoring and computing power over 
the last decade ( Batty, 2018 ). The digital twin allows us to visualize, 
analyze, and simulate how the physical counterpart would behave by 
assimilating the physical and digital world ( Tao et al., 2019 ). One of the 
best ways to leverage this technology is to predict future environmental 
changes and to explore optimal interventions (i.e., new designs, tech -
nologies, or policies) in advance ( Fuller et al., 2020 ). This concept is 
widely accepted in areas ranging from manufactured products to 
buildings, cities, and even ecosystems ( Qi et al., 2021 ). Recently, there 
have been many attempts to employ digital twins at the city scale to 
assess adaptive strategies for climate change-induced disasters, 
especially in areas where actual social experiments are not feasible due 
to economic and moral concerns ( Weil et al., 2023 ; Xia et al., 2022 ). In 
the field of stormwater management, especially with the potential for 
significant flood risk reduction through government-citizen collabora -
tion, the call for implementing digital twin platforms is on the rise ( Weil 
et al., 2023 ). 
As extreme heavy rainfall events increase due to climate change, not 
only the flood-prone areas along rivers but also inland urban areas are 
facing the risk of pluvial flooding, which occurs when the intensity of 
rainfall exceeds the capacity of urban drainage systems ( Lin et al., 2022 ; 
Rosenzweig et al., 2018 ). Urban pluvial flood modeling has been con -
ducted primarily from the supply side, involving entities such as the 
government, academia, and industry ( Azizi et al., 2022 ). This top-down 
approach has mainly focused on advancing the detailed methodology, 
spatial and temporal resolution, and accuracy of the hydrological model

( Bulti & Abebe, 2020 ; Lim et al., 2023 ). While this conventional 
modeling approach was suitable in the past when data was generated 
and utilized by a few entities, its high cost and ’ black-box model ’ 
nature — where citizens only receive the result of flood risk map without 
understanding the underlying mechanisms — hinder social support and 
consensus ( Gebremedhin et al., 2020 ). Recently, there has been an 
emphasis on involving citizens directly in urban pluvial flood modeling 
from the demand side of flood control ( Assumpç ˜ao et al., 2018 ). This 
bottom-up approach uses participatory modeling to socialize the costs, 
allowing consecutive model updates in the public domain and validation 
using volunteered geographic information (VGI), like social media im -
ages ( Haworth, 2016 ; Lin, 2022 ; Vermiglio et al., 2022 ). The partici -
patory modeling approach can be effective in enhancing the granularity 
of large-scale urban modeling ( Zhang et al., 2019 ). 
Pluvial floods can occur not only in low-lying areas but also in any 
urban area experiencing structural or functional failures of the drainage 
system, whereas fluvial floods are mainly associated with specific flood- 
prone areas ( Douglas et al., 2010 ; Lin et al., 2021 ). This imposes greater 
spatial uncertainty on pluvial flood risk, with the risk becoming even 
higher in densely populated areas that have undergone rapid urbani -
zation ( Di Salvo et al., 2018 ; Lin et al., 2023 ). Therefore, while it is 
necessary to construct pluvial flood modeling across the entire urban 
area, the high cost of a top-down approach underscores the need for 
citizen participation ( Assumpç ˜ao et al., 2018 ). By engaging the partic -
ipation of local residents, it is possible to incorporate diverse local 
knowledge into the modeling, such as historical changes in the hydro -
logical regime, water courses, flood patterns, and socio-demographics 
( Haworth, 2016 ; Hendricks et al., 2018 ; Wehn et al., 2015 ). Enhanced 
citizen ownership and transparency in participatory modeling lead to 
community engagement, empowering individuals to have a voice in 
decision-making and thereby promoting accountable and sustainable 
disaster management ( Gray et al., 2018 ; Zhang et al., 2019 ). 
Urban pluvial flooding, which encompasses both surface runoff and 
combined sewer overflows, represents a challenge intertwined with 
complex natural and human systems ( Liu et al., 2007 ). In light of this, 
there has been an increasing call for a paradigm shift towards partici -
patory stormwater management, emphasizing collective actions ( Bouz -
guenda et al., 2019 ; Dhakal & Chevalier, 2016 ; Lim et al., 2023 ). By 
taking measures such as sand-bagging, installing temporary water bar -
riers, and cleaning inlets (e.g., drain grates and curbs) or trenches, cit -
izens can play an instrumental role in mitigating pluvial floods ( Ren ´e 
et al., 2018 ). As such, a bottom-up approach to stormwater management 
is pivotal, where citizens are actively involved in every phase of the 
process, from building pluvial flood models to analysis, 
decision-making, evaluation, and feedback ( Barreteau, Bots, & Daniell, 
2010 ; White et al., 2021 ). By doing so, citizens are encouraged to 
brainstorm and implement preventive measures at both the individual 
and community levels ( Lehtola et al., 2022 ). Consequently, a platform 
that provides prompt feedback on the anticipated effects of those actions 
is vital. However, there is a lack of comprehensive and user-friendly 
guidelines for participatory pluvial flood modeling, while each neces -
sary technical component has already been developed as open-source 
( White et al., 2023 ). To address the gap, we propose a framework for 
interactive and public-driven rapid urban pluvial flood modeling, along 
with a step-by-step case study that walks through the entire process. 
This paper discusses four key components essential for participatory 
urban pluvial flood modeling: Open Source Software (OSS), Virtual 
Reality (VR), Minimum Viable Product (MVP), and Gamification 
( Fig. 2 ). First, OSS liberalizes access to urban flood modeling by elimi -
nating software costs and enabling seamless integration with different 
OSS tools ( White et al., 2023 ). OSS platforms enable continuous model 
improvement, making them more responsive to public needs ( Zhang 
et al., 2022 ). Second, VR is identified as a pivotal tool for effective 
communication, providing immersive and realistic visualizations 
( Portman et al., 2015 ). VR enhances situational awareness of urban 
flooding, allowing laypersons to explore the expected impact of floods in 
various scenarios ( Bakhtiari et al., 2023 ; Fan et al., 2020 ). Third, by 
adopting an MVP approach, the web-based participatory model aims to 
accelerate computational speed while still capturing essential physical 
processes, considering both budget and data constraints ( Bartos & Ker -
kez, 2021 ). This parsimonious approach can enable near real-time flood 
assessments and early warning systems, keeping the platform versatile 
and straightforward ( Sermet et al., 2020 ). Fourth, gamification in -
corporates the behavioral aspects of citizens to encourage public 
participation ( Baruch et al., 2016 ). Even a non-monetary reward system 
can enhance both the quality and quantity of crowdsourced data ( R I 
Ogie et al., 2019 ). This integrates well with a sanctions system to deter 
the spread of incorrect information and facilitates citizens acting as both 
users and providers of data ( Ostrom, 1990 ). Collectively, these four 
components work synergistically to establish a robust, participatory 
modeling to foster grassroots initiatives for sustainable stormwater 
management in the digital twin era. 
We provide a framework for participatory urban pluvial flood 
modeling that involves a multi-step process, starting with preparing 
input data for constructing and calibrating hydrological models. The 
framework couples a one-dimensional stormwater model with a rapid 
flood spreading model (RFSM) to offer a simplified yet effective tool for 
rapid flood risk estimation ( Jamali et al., 2018 ). Model verification re -
lies on a range of data sources, such as flood inundation maps, flood 
marks, sewer level data, social media images, and anecdotal reports 
from local residents ( Fohringer et al., 2015 ; Le Coz et al., 2016 ; Wang 
et al., 2018 ). Additionally, the framework is tailored to explore various 
flood mitigation strategies, including both conventional ’ grey ’ infra -
structure solutions like retrofitting drainage systems, as well as ’ green ’ 
infrastructure like installing various low impact development practices 
( Hendricks & Dowtin, 2023 ). The framework also incorporates the role 
of community engagement and future climate change scenarios to 
explore solutions for contributing to urban flood resilience and climate 
change adaptation. We demonstrate this framework with a case study on 
recurring pluvial flooding in the Gangnam region of Seoul, South Korea, 
using popular open-source software like EPA SWMM and QGIS. The 
framework aims to provide a participatory and process-based method -
ology for developing near real-time urban flood models, offering 
actionable strategies and immediate feedback to support various 
stakeholders, including local residents, engineers, researchers, and 
policymakers ( Geertman & Stillwell, 2004 ). 
The remainder of this paper unfolds in the following order. Section 2 
provides an overview of the theoretical background of the four com -
ponents for participatory urban pluvial flood modeling. In Section 3 , we 
outline the framework for modeling, which includes input data prepa -
ration, hydrological model construction, model verification, and model 
experiments. In Section 4 , we showcase detailed guidelines on essential 
techniques for each modeling framework, only without recruiting actual 
human participants from the study area. This case study utilizes open 
source software through every modeling process. In the concluding 
sections, we highlight the primary insights and implications of our 
framework in the era of the digital twin. Before we begin, note that the 
purpose of our model is not to develop and publish the actual working 
repository or archive for the participatory urban pluvial flood modeling. 
Rather, we aim to enable general readers to construct and analyze 
interactive, web-based 3D rapid urban pluvial flood modeling from 
scratch, following the framework without using any proprietary tools. 
Our work can contribute to developing a digital twin platform and 
mobilizing citizen participation around the globe for adapting to urban 
pluvial flooding intensified by climate change. Consequently, this paper 
advocates for a shift in perspective, where citizens can play a significant 
role beyond merely receiving emergency information, by actively 
participating in data provision, model development and verification, 
and decision-making processes.

2. Components for participatory urban flood modeling and 
digital twins 
Participatory modeling utilizing digital twins can be conceptualized 
as revolving around two interconnected axes: the technical aspect and 
the social aspect ( Fig. 1 ). In the technical aspect, the Level of Detail 
(LoD) is primarily used to represent the granularity and complexity of 
virtual reality in relation to its physical counterpart ( Lehtola et al., 
2022 ). The LoD is usually divided into 4 to 5 levels (i.e., LoD 0-4). The 
higher the level of detail, the more accurate and precise the simulation 
of reality, making the highest LoD ultimately the desired target of a 
digital twin. However, building a higher LoD requires an extensive 
amount of input data and often specialized proprietary software, hence 
significantly boosting the computing power required for simulations 
( Kikuchi et al., 2022 ). 
From the corresponding social aspect, we propose citizen participa -
tion as a solution that can effectively address the technical limitations of 
a digital twin. Voluntary citizen participation can yield a large amount 
of detailed and localized input data by socializing the cost of data 
acquisition ( Hendricks et al., 2018 ). By using open-source software, we 
can significantly reduce modeling costs by involving citizens throughout 
the entire modeling process — including model construction, verifica -
tion, and experiments. Additionally, making data and decision-making 
open to the public throughout all stages of the modeling processes can 
bolster the model ’ s reliability and transparency. However, as the 
required LoD of the model increases, the task difficulty may become too 
high to expect voluntary participation from the general public. There -
fore, within this trade-off relationship, striking an appropriate balance 
between the technical aspects of the digital twin and the social aspects of 
citizen participation is crucial for optimal participatory modeling 
( Rodríguez-Bolívar et al., 2018 ). To address this challenge, we break 
down participatory modeling into four components and their relevant 
attributes within the context of urban flood modeling ( Fig. 2 ). 
2.1. Open source software 
To encourage citizen participation in urban pluvial flood modeling, 
the use of open source software (OSS) is imperative. One of the major 
obstacles impeding active public involvement in participatory modeling 
is the high licensing costs of specialized hydrology software ( White 
et al., 2023 ). Recently, OSS has become virtually indistinguishable from 
proprietary software in terms of functionality, and its user-friendliness 
has also significantly improved ( Namgyal et al., 2023 ). Additionally, 
the rapid expansion of online communities among OSS users is creating 
a virtuous cycle that further promotes the development of OSS. Thus, 
OSS is a public-driven, flexible platform that permits continuous model 
refinement and adaptive modifications in response to public demand 
without relying on a limited number of developer groups ( Zhang et al., 
2022 ). 
Above all, there is the advantage of improving interoperability 
through integration with different OSS. Specifically, in the realm of 
urban flood models, the coupling of stormwater models with overland 
flow models is essential, and OSS can facilitate this process more 
seamlessly ( Sadler et al., 2019 ). The scalability of such OSS allows it to 
be easily transplanted and customized in areas with restricted budgets to 
build their digital twins, or in countries vulnerable to climate disasters 
( Nkwunonwo et al., 2020 ). This contributes to improving environmental 
justice and equity by opening access to the source code and data of flood 
models to the public ( Dixon et al., 2021 ; Hendricks et al., 2018 ). Also, 
OSS offers transparency in its architecture and algorithms, which can be 
openly audited and validated by anyone. Thus, this transparency can 
increase reliability in the modeling product and enhance the sense of 
community and trust among users, thereby aiding in the improvement of 
social capital for a more resilient disaster response ( Aldrich & Meyer, 
2015 ; Hasala, Supak, & Rivers, 2020 ; Weil et al., 2023 ). 
As diverse stakeholder groups (including local community members, 
civil societies, researchers, and policymakers) become more engaged 
and familiar with these open-source tools, they can enhance the social 
consensus on emergency responses and collective actions related to the 
risk of pluvial flooding ( R I Ogie et al., 2019 ). In other words, one per -
son ’ s output can be used as another person ’ s input, creating a linked 
model chain and crowdsourcing platform. Such collective action and 
intelligence from the grassroots level are fundamental for proactive 
stormwater management and pluvial flood mitigation. 
2.2. Virtual reality 
Virtual reality (VR) is a multifaceted technology which can facilitate 
realistic visualization and also work as an effective means of commu -
nication across various domains ( Portman et al., 2015 ). Ordinary people 
may find it difficult to comprehensively assess the situation by looking at 
a traditional 2D flood map with cross-sections representing flood depth 
( Bakhtiari et al., 2023 ). Accordingly, VR is a key technology that can 
improve the situational awareness of the general public in disaster sit -
uations by providing an immersive experience similar to the real world 
in a digital twin ( Fan et al., 2020 ). Additionally, experts from various 
fields such as urban planning, hydrology, transportation engineering, 
law enforcement, architecture, and policy making, can virtually explore 
different flood scenarios. By doing so, they can compare the expected 
results promptly, allowing them to identify the best strategies through 
iterative scenario analysis and parametric studies. 
Recently, many platforms have been developed that integrate 
geographic information systems (GIS) with VR to visualize analysis re -
sults in real-time ( Tobias et al., 2016 ). The dynamic representation of 
flood inundation over time facilitates the development and evaluation of 
emergency response strategies for public safety, including flood pro -
tection, evacuation processes, and traffic control ( Wolf et al., 2022 ). For 
instance, VR simulation of flood inundation extent and depth can

2.3. Minimum viable product 
The participatory modeling essentially needs to be a web-based 
platform accessible to everyone at all times ( Ma et al., 2024 ; Wolf 
et al., 2022 ). Therefore, it is necessary to ensure sufficiently fast 
computational processing under the technical constraints of 
web-hosting to maintain reliable services, especially during emergencies 
( Bartos & Kerkez, 2021 ; Voinov et al., 2018 ). Prior research suggests 
that the public ’ s average patience for waiting for a website to respond is 
only 4-8 seconds ( Galletta et al., 2004 ), implying that a rapid response is 
essential for active citizen participation. The urban flood model typically 
takes longer in computational speed because it requires the integration 
of both the stormwater model and the overland flow model ( Namgyal 
et al., 2023 ; Sadler et al., 2019 ). The computation speed varies widely 
for both the stormwater model (depending on the drainage network 
complexity) and the overland flow model (depending on the dimension 
of flood modeling methods: rapid flood spreading, 1D, 2D), but it 
roughly ranges from a few minutes to several hours ( Bulti & Abebe, 
2020 ). 
If an urban flood model takes longer than, say, tens of minutes for a 
single simulation, it would be challenging for the platform to actually be 
used by the general public. Given the nature of disaster information 
platforms that do not yield revenue, there are budget constraints on 
expanding the server ’ s hardware capacity. Therefore, every possible 
method to increase computational speed should be utilized as much as 
possible on the software side. For example, there are methods such as 
using lower dimensions of flood modeling methods and employing pre- 
defined flood volume-depth curves for the target area to drastically 
reduce computation time ( Rahman et al., 2016 ). 
To get out of a black box model, merely sharing the open-source code 
transparently (as discussed in Section 2.1 ) is not sufficient. If the model 
is too specialized and intricate, and only a few experts can understand it, 
it will still remain a black box model to the general public. Therefore, for 
participatory modeling, it is essential to adopt a minimally viable model 
that is intuitively understandable by the general public while still styl -
izing and encapsulating the core physical processes ( Zhang et al., 2022 ). 
By being stylized, the model can prioritize the influential factors 
affecting urban pluvial flooding, allowing the general public to focus on 
primary concerns without getting overwhelmed by minute details. 
Therefore, from the standpoint of the general public, a framework is 
desired to produce a minimum viable product which is easy and light, 
suitable for real-time rapid flood assessment, early warning, and emer -
gency operations ( Sermet et al., 2020 ). 
2.4. Gamifications 
For spontaneous participation from citizens, it is essential to have a 
two-way feedback platform that facilitates interaction between citizens, 
as they are both users and providers of participatory modeling ( Luo 
et al., 2022 ). For instance, a system should inform citizens about 
anticipated pluvial flood risks in specific areas and allows them to 
contribute observational data for model validation, effectively acting as 
a bilateral crowdsourcing platform.

Given the nature of urban pluvial floods, which can emerge and 
disappear in a short time in unspecified areas, there is often a lack of 
data on inundation areas or depth ( Manfreda et al., 2014 ). Volunteered 
geographic information (VGI) can provide near real-time data on flood 
inundation extent, depth, and flow directions ( Cariolet et al., 2019 ; Yao 
& Wang, 2020 ). Compared to traditional physical or remote sensing 
methods, VGI offers a wider spatial coverage with a high-resolution ( Li 
et al., 2018 ). Additionally, for the same period and location, 
on-the-ground observations from multiple participants allow for 
cross-checking ( Y. Li, Osei, Hu, & Stein, 2023 ). Flood marks or flood 
scene images can be used to validate the results of the pluvial flood 
model and enhance its accuracy ( Ren ´e et al., 2018 ). Furthermore, 
non-imagery crowdsourced data such as anecdotal evidence and his -
torical flood patterns can be qualitatively analyzed ( Rollason et al., 
2018 ). 
However, verifying the reliability of VGI and ensuring data security 
and privacy are necessary. A significant problem in utilizing VGI for 
emergency management is the propagation of incorrect information, 
whether maliciously or by mistake ( Wang et al., 2018 ). During actual 
flood disasters, there are instances where flood scene images are 
incorrectly geo-tagged, so it is preferable to have photos that also show 
surrounding geographical features for reference. Moreover, there is also 
an issue where past flood scene images from the same area are redis -
tributed, causing social confusion. In such cases, it is necessary to 
determine whether the photo is the same as one previously published by 
filtering using the photo ’ s metadata ( Yao & Wang, 2020 ). VGI may 
inadvertently expose sensitive information, such as locations, de -
mographics, or identities ( Li et al., 2023 ). These security and privacy 
vulnerabilities necessitate robust measures, including personal infor -
mation protection, anonymity protocols, and clear privacy policies, to 
ensure the trustworthiness and ethical use of VGI ( Yan et al., 2024 ). 
Balancing the quality of VGI with privacy and security issues is crucial 
for maintaining responsive and sustainable public participation 
( Rodríguez-Bolívar et al., 2018 ). 
To facilitate spontaneous citizen participation, a reward system for 
those providing information is vital. The reward does not necessarily 
have to be a monetary payoff; it could be non-monetary payoffs like 
reputation, social connections (e.g., number of followers), virtual goods, 
or influences, which give a sense of warm glow or accomplishment 
( Brown et al., 2016 ; Ogie et al., 2019 ). For the reward system to work 
effectively, a personalized virtual identity that can receive credit is 
preferred over a completely anonymous identification ( Daniel et al., 
2018 ). When people receive immediate rewards for their actions, they 
become more motivated to participate. This concept, known as gamifi -
cation, is widely adopted in behavioral experiments and crowdsourcing 
platforms ( Baruch et al., 2016 ). Simultaneously, sanctions are also 
necessary for users who continuously provide incorrect information, to 
prevent ‘the tragedy of the commons ’ in the virtual world as well ( Ogie 
et al., 2017 ; Ostrom, 1990 ). Therefore, by leveraging gamification for 
educational purposes, we can empower voluntary and responsible citi -
zen engagement, ultimately achieving sustainable participatory 
modeling through a learning-by-doing approach. 
3. Participatory framework for urban pluvial flood modeling 
3.1. Input data preparation 
As the first step, the preparation of various input data is necessary for 
participatory urban pluvial flood modeling ( Fig. 3 ). The input data can 
be broadly categorized into data for both model construction and model 
calibration and validation. For hydrological model construction, input 
data such as rainfall data, terrain data, building data, land use and land 
cover (LULC), soil data, and urban drainage network map are needed at 
a minimum. 
In terms of terrain data, not only the digital terrain model (DTM) is 
needed, but also a digital surface model (DSM) that takes into account 
the heights of buildings is required for more accurate overland flow 
modeling ( Huang et al., 2024 ). Using GIS, one can calculate the average 
slope according to the desired spatial scale ( Lin & Li, 2019 ). For rainfall 
data, continuous precipitation data from within or near the target area is 
needed. Given the characteristics of urban pluvial floods, which have 
short times of concentration and flood durations, a rainfall monitoring

interval of ideally less than 20 minutes is preferred ( Blanc et al., 2012 ). 
Nonetheless, to minimize computational demands and given that the 
typical precipitation monitoring interval is hourly, the temporal reso -

hourly ( Blanc et al., 2012 ). 
To estimate surface runoff, we chose an empirical and data-driven 
method: the Natural Resources Conservation Service (formerly called 
as Soil Conservation Service) Curve Number (NRCS-CN) method 
( USDA-SCS, 1986 ). This is because the NRCS-CN method requires less 
data, specifically LULC and hydrologic soil group data, compared to 
process-based rainfall-runoff models that demand high-resolution and 
extensive data. Therefore, we considered the NRCS-CN method more 
suitable for participatory modeling, as it facilitates greater public 
engagement through its simplicity and minimum data requirements. 
With the aforementioned data, a generic rainfall-runoff model can be 
constructed. 
However, to adequately capture the characteristics of urban pluvial 
floods that occur due to insufficient capacity of the urban drainage 
system, geometrical data on the urban drainage system for stormwater 
modeling is needed. Specifically, geographical information about the 
location or depth of manholes, drain grates, and outlets, as well as 
conduit diameter and length, is required ( Dai et al., 2018 ; Mohebbi 
et al., 2020 ). In most cities, this information is restricted from public 
access and is classified as confidential data ( Blumensaat et al., 2012 ; Dai 
et al., 2020 ; Du et al., 2019 ; Li et al., 2023 ). While a drainage network 
model incorporating every real-world element may require significant 
computational resources, it does not automatically guarantee greater 
accuracy ( Dobson et al., 2022 ; Krebs et al., 2014 ). To save computa -
tional power, the majority of prior research focuses on modeling key 
junctions and often omits detailed real-world inlet components like 
grates and curb openings in their stylized drainage network model 
( Dong et al., 2023 ; Leit ˜ao et al., 2010 ). Therefore, if input data for the 
actual urban drainage system is unavailable, an alternative is to 
construct a stylized drainage network model based on the local sewer 
design standards and manuals (for detailed methods, refer to Section 
3.2 .). 
3.2. Hydrological model construction 
In order to construct urban pluvial flood modeling, we need to utilize 
both the stormwater model and the overland flow model. For the sake of 
model simplicity, we suggest coupling a one-dimensional stormwater 
model and a rapid flood spreading model (1D-RFSM) for this framework. 
For the stormwater model part, essential inputs include rainfall data, 
terrain data, LULC, hydrologic soil group, and the urban drainage 
network. The output of the stormwater model consists of flood volume, 
flood duration, flooding rate, and flooded nodes, which are then used as 
inputs for the overland flow model part. 
First, it is essential to construct a model of the drainage network. If 
geographical data for the drainage system in the target area is already 
available, it can be used directly. Alternatively, for a minimum viable 
model, users can consolidate local or tributary sewers with the same 
flow direction to build a stylized drainage network model that empha -
sizes the trunk or intercepting sewers ( Davidsen et al., 2017 ; Leit ˜ao 
et al., 2010 ). If geographical data for the drainage system in the target 
area is unavailable, a stylized drainage network model can be con -
structed based on the principles outlined in the local sewer design 
manual. As a good rule of thumb, junctions were positioned at every 
node and at points where the conduits curved. In straight sections, the 
spacing between junctions should not exceed the maximum interval 
distance, defined by the sewer design standards of the local government. 
Since most urban drainage systems transport sewage by gravity, it is 
necessary to know the slope of the conduit, specifically the depth (invert 
elevation) of its starting and ending junctions, to calculate storm drain 
flow ( Schmitt et al., 2004 ). However, if the invert elevation data for each 
junction is unknown, the drainage network model can be completed by 
applying the pipe design slope according to the conduit diameter, as 
provided in the sewer design standards of the local government. 
Secondly, to estimate surface runoff from rainfall data, the initial 
step is to delineate subcatchments according to the drainage network 
map (if available) within the target area. However, if a drainage network 
map is not available, subcatchments can be delineated based on the grid 
zone or road network in urban areas, since public sewers are often 
installed under the road layout ( Amaguchi et al., 2012 ). Using GIS, the 
surface slope and imperviousness of each subcatchments can be derived 
based on terrain data. Subsequently, the NRCS-CN method is employed 
to estimate rainfall-induced runoff or infiltration, commonly used in 
ungauged watersheds. Although it lacks a connection to real-world hy -
drological processes compared to physically distributed models, its use 
of a few parameters allows for faster and sometimes even more accurate 
results ( Sitterson et al., 2017 ). Using Table S.1, the NRCS-CN values can 
be derived through LULC as well as hydrologic soil groups. 
Third, to calculate the combined sewer inflow into the target area, 
identify the junctions where external inflows come from the corre -
sponding adjacent subcatchments. External inflow from adjacent sub -
catchments refers to the base sanitary flow (dry weather inflow) on clear 
days, and during rainfall, it means the sum of that base sanitary flow and 
the rainfall-induced surface runoff (wet weather inflow). For the base 
sanitary flow coming from neighboring subcatchments, the estimation 
was performed by taking the product of the conduit ’ s cross-sectional 
area (m
2
), the average sewage carrying capacity (%), and the flow ve -
locity (m/s). If the flow velocity cannot be obtained, the design flow 
velocity can be used instead. The rainfall-induced surface runoff from 
each adjacent subcatchment is estimated using the modified rational 
method, which is widely used for rapid flood assessment and deter -
mining the design capacity of urban drainage systems ( Aron & Kibler, 
1990 ). It is posited that the surface runoff from the outlet of each 
adjacent subcatchment all flows into the target area ’ s urban drainage 
network. 
Fourthly, run a 1D stormwater model simulation. The governing 
equation for routing both sewer flow and unsteady surface flow is the 
Saint-Venant equation, solved through the application of the finite dif -
ference method ( Schmitt et al., 2004 ). As input, a rainfall hyetograph is 
used, and intermediate results for surcharged overflow from each node 
are generated, such as total flood volume, flood duration, and peak 
flooding rate and time. 
For the overland flow modeling, we suggest using the simplest flood 
modeling approach, the Rapid Flood Spreading Model (RFSM), as part of 
this framework. RFSM only takes the DSM and the surcharged overflow 
volume derived from the stormwater model as its inputs, and yields final 
outputs such as flood inundation extent and depth ( Bernini & Franchini, 
2013 ). Despite its limitations, such as not considering overland flow 
velocity and being only capable of ascertaining the boundaries of the 
flood inundation area, RFSM shows acceptable accuracy for simulating 
an individual rainfall event compared to more complex flood modeling 
approaches, like 1D or 2D models ( Jamali et al., 2019 ). Therefore, RFSM 
is commonly used in areas where rapid flood assessment is required for 
real-time forecasting, early warning, and emergency operations, as well 
as in regions where the available input data is not enough for conducting 
higher-dimensional flood models ( Bulti & Abebe, 2020 ). The procedure 
of flood spreading begins by filling local depressions and then proceeds 
to overflow floodwater into adjacent grid cells until no more floodwater 
is left ( Jamali et al., 2018 ). We can determine the surcharge-induced 
inundation depth by adding up the flood volume on the DSM of the 
target area. By repeating this process for multiple flood events, the 
relationship between total flood volume and flood inundation depth can 
be derived through nonlinear regression analysis, resulting in a 
pre-defined flood volume-depth curve. Using this curve can dramatically 
reduce computational times to a matter of seconds, enabling immediate 
near real-time responses. For specific parameter values and model set -
tings, please refer to Section 4.2 .

3.3. Model verification 
As previously discussed, due to the lack of observed data on the 
dynamic change of urban pluvial flooding extent or depth, we conduct 
model calibration and validation using multifaceted data and methods. 
To verify the stormwater model, data about the sewer depth or flow rate 
in the conduit is required. This is possible only if the sewer management 
entity is conducting regular monitoring. However, the overland flow 
model can be validated through various means such as historical flood 
extents, flood scene images, flood marks, or anecdotal evidence. Pho -
tographs of in situ flood situations can often be obtained through social 
media, while historical flood extents and flood marks are usually pro -
vided by local governments or accredited surveyors ( Le Coz et al., 2016 ). 
Anecdotal evidence, such as how high the water rose or which roads 
were inundated, can be gathered through interviews with residents or 
posts on social media ( Wang et al., 2018 ). 
The calibration process is conducted for the parameter sets related to 
the characteristics of the urban drainage system, within the ranges 
confined by previous studies outlined in Table 1 . The optimum param -
eter values were calibrated in small increments (i.e., 0.001 unit) to 
minimize the error between the observed values and the model output. 
The goodness-of-fit measure for calibration and validation varies 
depending on the target variable and observed data.

historical flood extents, which are geographic data represented by 
polygon features. In order to verify the geometric similarity between two 
polygons, the Lee-Sallee index is often used in hydrology and flood

explicit measure provides a value that ranges between 0 and 1, with 
closer to 1 meaning that A and B completely coincide ( Clarke & Gaydos,

3.4.2. Grey infrastructure strategy 
Secondly, another approach involves retrofitting traditional grey 
infrastructure. This approach focuses on engineering solutions like 
enhancing the carrying capacity of the existing drainage system, such as 
expanding the diameter of pipes and increasing the pipe slope. Addi -
tionally, new conduits can be added for better stormwater management. 
A notable example would be the deep tunnel sewer systems that are 
being adopted in many mega-cities worldwide ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Park 
et al., 2024 ). These systems serve as an advanced engineering solution 
for managing large volumes of stormwater and wastewater. By exploring 
various network configurations of urban drainage system, users can 
identify the optimal measures to alleviate the bottleneck and minimize 
the surcharged overflow volume. 
3.4.3. Collective action strategy 
The third strategy focuses on enhancing a community ’ s flood resil -
ience through collective action, such as the voluntary organization and 
cooperation among citizens ( McEwen et al., 2018 ; Mees et al., 2016 ). 
This can involve installing water barriers or temporary sandbags, as well 
as cleaning the inlets of the urban drainage system to ensure they remain 
unclogged. Or, sharing information about flooded roads to control traffic 
around dangerous areas and to identify detour routes can help vulner -
able road users evacuate safely. On a personal level, individuals can be 
motivated by immediately seeing how and to what extent their efforts 
contribute to reducing flood damage. On the community level, scientific 
analysis can determine the number of participants needed for effective 
stormwater management, thereby aiding in the organization and 
mobilization of resources. 
3.4.4. Climate change scenarios 
Lastly, users can explore and compare above stormwater manage -
ment strategies by applying various climate change scenarios to predict 
the impact of uncertain heavy rains in future. Additionally, by testing 
different return periods of design rainfall under climate change sce -
narios, they can identify the tipping points for each urban pluvial flood 
mitigation strategy. The method for determining design rainfall entails 
aligning historical precipitation data with a probability distribution 
function, followed by generating an Intensity-Duration-Frequency (IDF) 
curve ( Esteves, 2013 ). We estimated the volume of rainfall expected for 
the selected return period, taking into account various climate change 
models and scenarios. To generate hydrographs based on that interest 
design rainfall, we opted to use the alternating block method within this 
framework ( Gersonius et al., 2012 ). 
4. Case study 
In this section, we selected the Gangnam region of Seoul, South 
Korea, as a case study to showcase the participatory flood modeling 
framework. We used popular open source software and provided 
detailed guidelines on the essential techniques step-by-step, with the 
exception for real human participant recruitment from the study area. 
The Gangnam region is one of the largest central business districts and 
the most expensive residential areas in South Korea. In the Gangnam 
region, we developed a urban pluvial flood model for Seocho 2-dong, 
Seocho-gu. This case study site covers an area of 1.02 km
2 
and has 
experienced pluvial flooding biennially for the last decade. In 2022, the 
study area experienced a 100-year heavy rainfall with a maximum in -
tensity of 92 mm/h, leading to the most severe flood damage ever 
recorded in Seoul, South Korea. The target area has a U-shaped topog -
raphy that is lower than its surroundings. Therefore, the runoff from 
adjacent regions initially gathers in the target area before being dis -
charged into the Han River via a northwestern tributary ( Fig. 4 ). How -
ever, the recurring disasters are not solely due to the vulnerable 
conditions of topography, but also to the insufficient capacity and ill- 
designed drainage systems of the target area ( Park et al., 2024 ). In 
this case study, we used EPA SWMM 5.2 for the rainfall-runoff and sewer 
flow simulation, and QGIS 3.28.10 for the rapid flood spreading simu -
lation; both are leading open source software.

Secondly, for the double validation of the 2022 flood event, we 
performed a formal goodness-of-fit test to quantitatively compare the 
observed sewer level data with the simulated sewer depth, an interme -
diate output of the stormwater model before coupling with the overland 
flow model. The monitored sewer level data for the study area has been 
available every minute in a few specific conduits since 2012 from the 
Seoul City public database ( https://data.seoul.go.kr ). Among them, we 
used the observed values from a conduit near one of the study area ’ s 
outlets (J9-Out2), as shown in Fig. 9 , and conducted quantitative vali -
dation with the 2022 flood rainfall, which resulted in the most signifi -
cant flood damage ever recorded. We evaluated two measures of 
goodness-of-fit, specifically NSE and RSR, broadly utilized in the field 
of hydrology. 
The NSE, a measure of model accuracy compared to the observed 
data, ranges from negative infinity to 1. The higher the NSE value (closer 
to 1), the better it captures the actual behavior observed in the real 
world. Moriasi and colleagues (2007) (D. N. Moriasi et al., 2007 ) set 
forth general performance benchmarks for hydrological models with 
monthly time steps, categorizing an NSE greater than 0.75 as “ Very 
Good ” , those between 0.65 and 0.75 as “ Good ” , and those above 0.50 as 
“ Satisfactory ” . However, our model operates on a minute-by-minute 
basis, implying that the performance criteria designed for monthly in -
tervals might be overly strict, given that model accuracy generally di -
minishes with shorter time steps ( Engel et al., 2007 ). As far as we are 
aware, there are no established performance benchmarks for models 
operating on a minute-by-minute basis, prompting us to follow the 
standards for monthly models. Consequently, we have cautiously 
assumed that our model ’ s performance is on par with, or superior to, 
that of monthly models for the same index value. Using Eq. 2 , the NSE 
value of the stormwater model for the minute-based sewer level data is 
evaluated as 0.52, which is considered satisfactory or better. 
The RSR, a normalized indicator of error variance, varies from 0 to 
positive infinity. The closer the RSR value is to 0, the fewer errors there 
are in the model simulation. According to Moriasi et al. (2007) , the 
suggested performance benchmarks for the RSR with monthly time steps 
classify an RSR less than 0.5 as “ Very Good ” , those between 0.5 and 0.6 
as “ Good ” , and those between 0.6 and 0.7 as “ Satisfactory ” . Again, we 
have conservatively adopted the criteria for monthly time steps to assess 
the performance of our minute-by-minute model. Using Eq. 3 , the 
derived RSR value is 0.69, indicating that the model performance is at 
least satisfactory in terms of the residual variation of sewer level data. In 
summary, both goodness-of-fit measures demonstrate the performance 
of the stormwater model as above satisfactory.

4.4. Model experiment 
We demonstrate experiments for green infrastructure, grey infra -
structure, and collective action strategies using the constructed urban 
pluvial flood model, based on extreme rainfall events driven by climate 
change scenarios. 
4.4.1. Green infrastructure strategy 
Using the 2010 flood rainfall as a case study, we applied three types 
of LID practice scenarios — namely, permeable pavement, bio-retention, 
and green roofs — to 50% of the area of impervious surfaces, green 
spaces, and buildings, respectively. The parameter values for LID 
implemented in the SWMM model can be found in Table S.5. Fig. 10 
displays the dynamic retention and detention effects of each LID practice 
in runoff reduction. Fig. 10. A illustrates that the majority of direct runoff 
from or in-situ rainfall on LID practices could have been effectively 
retained through the use of combined permeable pavement and bio- 
retention. Additionally, Fig. 10. B shows that the water-holding capac -
ity of green roofs varied over time in two distinct sections: first, in the 
surface and soil layers where ponded water was fully drained within 3 
weeks, and second, in the drainage mat, where the detained water was 
progressively released over a span of 40 days. In terms of the total 
volume of retained or detained stormwater, permeable pavement 
contributed to 74% and bio-retention made up 15% for the retention 
volume, as indicated by the total area under the infiltration loss rate 
curve for each LID practice in Fig. 10. A. The detention volume from 
green roofs accounted for 11%, as shown by the sum of the maximum 
ponded and detained water volume in Fig. 10. B. In conclusion, LIDs 
achieved an 8.4% reduction in total flood volume of the 2010 flood 
event compared to the baseline without the application of LID practices. 
4.4.2. Grey infrastructure strategy 
As a strategy to improve grey infrastructure, we devised scenarios 
that double the conduit capacity in sections of the existing drainage 
network that frequently experience bottlenecks. We then compared their 
effectiveness in reducing flood risks using the rainfall data from the 
2010 flood ( Table 3 ). Comparing the Grey1 and Grey3 scenarios, Grey1 
reduced the flood volume by 35.8% by enlarging the conduit capacity of 
only two links, whereas Grey3, despite improving a longer section of 
four links, had a negligible effect on reducing flood volume (-0.4%). 
Accordingly, it can be seen that focusing on critical bottleneck sections, 
regardless of the overall length upgraded, is the most effective strategy 
for mitigating urban flood risks. In this case study, the J10-Out1 section 
serves as the leverage point. Likewise, the Grey2 scenario, which in -
cludes the J10-Out1 section and extends upstream to encompass a total 
of four sections for diameter enlargement, is analyzed to potentially 
reduce flood volume by up to 40.3% compared to the baseline. In 
summary, if efficiency in terms of flood reduction per link is considered, 
the Grey1 scenario would be the optimal strategy, and if the goal is to 
minimize the total flood volume, the Grey2 scenario would be the better 
option. Based on the results of scenario analysis, the public can engage 
in discussions with various stakeholders about policies for retrofitting 
the most effective sections of the drainage network. 
4.4.3. Collective action strategy 
Through participatory urban flood modeling, citizens can find areas 
where they can voluntarily contribute to flood risk reduction. For 
instance, as discussed in Section 3.4.3 , one way to avoid flood damage in 
the community is to install water barriers or temporary sandbags 
collectively. Participatory modeling can help them to identify the 
pluvial flood-prone area and analyze the required length and height of 
water barriers or sandbags. We conducted a case study on collective 
action strategies utilizing the 2022 flood event, which featured a 
building that actually avoided flooding with water barriers.

5. Discussion 
5.1. Digital twins with bottom-up approach 
In the face of the growing threat of pluvial flooding in urban envi -
ronments, we raise the urgent need for an innovative solution that not 
only embraces modern technology but also actively involves citizen 
participation. To adaptively response to the spatial uncertainty associ -
ated with urban pluvial flooding, we need digital twin technology with a 
bottom-up approach that is capable of detailed, real-time analysis across 
the city scale. This approach encourages citizens, especially those most 
affected, to have a voice and actively engage in all stages, including the 
creation and analysis of urban pluvial flood models, and to contribute to 
decision-making, evaluation, and feedback processes. Incorporating 
citizens into this process enhances both the data reliability and the 
plausibility of the modeling (Oti et al., 2019), while also democratizing 
the decision-making process for flood risk management (Gharaibeh 
et al., 2021; Rodríguez-Bolívar et al., 2018). This represents an impor -
tant paradigm shift: citizens can be active contributors, rather than just 
passive recipients of disaster information, leading to more sustainable 
and resilient communities in flood management (Bouzguenda et al., 
2019). 
The proposed framework highlights four essential components for 
participatory flood modeling and offers guidance on how various tech -
nologies should synergize and operate collaboratively. To recap, Open 
Source Software liberalizes access (Mahajan et al., 2022), Virtual Reality 
enhances situational awareness (Bakhtiari et al., 2023), Minimum 
Viable Product ensures real-time feedback (Xia et al., 2022), and 
Gamification facilitates engagement and quality (Ligorio et al., 2023). 
Each of these components contributes to making flood modeling more 
inclusive and actionable for both experts and the general public. This 
framework can help model a physical environment in real-time, allow -
ing for rapid assessment of natural disaster situations. During emer -
gencies such as floods, droughts, heat and cold waves, and 
environmental pollution, digital twins can be used to anticipate the 
trajectory and impact, enabling prompt and effective emergency 
responses. 
Constructing these models involves coupling the stormwater and 
overland flow models, leveraging a 1D-RFSM model to enhance effi -
ciency and streamline processes. In regions where dynamic observa -
tional data are scarce, utilizing multifaceted data sources and novel 
methods facilitates comprehensive model verification, empowering in -
dividuals and communities to devise and implement proactive and 
resilient stormwater management strategies. The final product of this 
model is a flood inundation map, and it is also possible to estimate the 
flood volume, flood duration, and peak flooding rate for each flooded 
node. Using a concrete case study in the Gangnam region of South Korea, 
the results showcase versatile applications to enhance urban flood 
resilience against climate change scenarios, encompassing both green 
and grey infrastructure along with collective action. 
In terms of the future applicability of the model outputs, an online 
interactive platform or crowdsourcing archive for the participatory 
model is needed, as exemplified by the case of ‘SWATShare’ (Rajib et al., 
2016). Firstly, the results can be utilized to create a 3D interactive flood 
risk map that displays inundation risks for individual buildings. Using 
this, an early warning and evacuation system can be established. 
Additionally, local authorities can use it for road traffic control in areas 
anticipated to be inundated, thus minimizing loss of life and property 
damage. Secondly, by integrating real-time data, this framework can aid 
in logistical planning and organizing resources during emergencies, such 
as rescue operations and disaster relief distribution (Zou et al., 2023). It 
facilitates collective decision-making by pinpointing the most accessible 
routes for relief delivery and identifying areas in urgent need for rescue. 
Lastly, to support collective action for flood control, this framework can 
also determine which heights and lengths of water barriers would divert 
surface runoff and influence flooding depths in flood-prone areas. This 
provides scientific evidence that can be used to advocate for necessary 
infrastructure improvement or to determine the required amount of 
human and material resources for collective action. 
Given these points, digital twins offer a tool for a wide range of ap -
plications, especially in real-time monitoring and decision-making sce -
narios where there is a need to evaluate the potential impact of disaster 
mitigation strategies. This framework effectively articulates the impor -
tance for a bottom-up approach to supplement the traditional top-down 
strategies in urban flood management, emphasizing the community’s 
role in addressing flood risks. As a caveat, the construction and main -
tenance costs for green infrastructure and grey infrastructure were not 
factored into this stylized exploratory model. The drainage network 
model in this study assumed a free outfall condition, and the conveyance 
capacity of the waterbodies was not considered when discharging runoff 
into them. Subsequent studies would need to incorporate these factors to 
facilitate more effective flood adaptation policies and collective action. 
By leveraging cutting edge technologies with knowledge sharing, the 
proposed framework offers a promising pathway to more resilient and 
adaptive urban communities in the face of climate change (Chang et al.,

5.2. Participatory urban flood modeling and sustainable cities 
Participatory urban flood modeling can contribute to achieving the 
SDG targets through each of its components ( Fig. 13 ). However, when 
the four components work together in synergy with a digital twin model, 
they can have an even greater impact on achieving the SDGs. 
Specifically, OSS can promote inclusive participation across all ages, 
genders, races, and socio-economic statuses (Target 10.2: Promote 
universal social, economic and political inclusion), leveraging its 
license-free, economically affordable nature and the collective contri -
butions from individuals of diverse backgrounds ( Rodríguez Bolívar 
et al., 2023 ). Moreover, OSS makes its source code and data openly and 
equally available to all, allowing anyone to conduct audits and 
verification freely. This approach boosts social capital and environ -
mental equity, ensuring the public has access to information (Target 
16.10: Ensure public access to information and protect fundamental 
freedoms). OSS exhibits flexible behavior through continuous model 
modifications and integration with other software in response to public 
demands. Consequently, this self-organizing feature of OSS advances 
participatory environmental modeling, which can be transferred and 
adapted in areas with restricted budgets and those vulnerable to 
climate-related disasters (Target 17.7: Promote and transfer sustainable 
technologies to developing countries). 
VR can serve as a tool that helps the general public more easily ac -
cess, intuitively understand, and communicate disaster-related infor -
mation (Target 9.C: Universal and affordable access to information and 
communications technology). By offering an immersive experience that 
mirrors the real world, it enhances situational awareness about disaster 
situations, thereby increasing the adaptive capacity of individuals and 
communities to climate-related disasters. Additionally, citizens and ex -
perts can share and disseminate accumulated knowledge through 
repeated scenario analysis for decision-making on best management 
practices (Target 13.3: Improve education, awareness-raising, and 
human and institutional capacity). VR, combined with engaging tech -
nologies like GIS, the Internet of Things, and the metaverse, can induce 
high usability and active citizen participation, leading to collective ac -
tions such as the installation of temporary flood barriers in scientifically 
informed specifications and locations (Target 17.6: Knowledge sharing 
and cooperation for access to science, technology, and innovation). 
For rapid flood assessment and early warning, a simple and rapid 
MVP is an technically important attribute of participatory urban flood 
modeling (Target 3.D: Improve early warning systems for global health 
risks). Given the technical and budgetary constraints on hardware for 
real-time emergency response systems, focusing on streamlining soft -
ware operations in participatory flood modeling offers the most effective 
approach. This involves reducing computational power while still being 
able to reliably represent core physical processes with an MVP (Target 
9.5: Enhance scientific research and upgrade the technological capa -
bilities of industrial sectors). Integrating participatory modeling enables 
the creation of easily adaptable and stylized archetype collections for 
each Level of Detail of digital twins, specifically tailored to areas 
requiring disaster response (Target 17.8: Operationalize the technology 
bank and science, technology, and innovation capacity for least devel -
oped countries). 
VGI can be used to obtain a large amount of high-quality, real-time 
data on urban pluvial floods at a low cost for flood model validation. 
However, to keep VGI responsive and reliable, gamification methods 
that combine reward (i.e., non-monetary payoff) and sanction systems 
need to be incorporated (Target 17.18: Enhance availability of high- 
quality, timely and reliable data). Gamification also supports and 
strengthens local community engagement, becoming a foundation in 
sustainable stormwater management by providing implicit knowledge, 
the community ’ s memories, and past experiences with floods (Target 6. 
B: Support local engagement in water and sanitation management). 
When citizens are motivated by gamification to contribute to any pro -
cess of participatory modeling, they learn how individual voluntary 
cooperation can affect community disaster resilience. This hands-on 
approach not only fosters a deeper understanding of collective action 
but also serves an educational role in promoting the SDGs as global 
citizens ( Ligorio et al., 2023 ; Meyer et al., 2018 ) (Target 4.7: Education 
for sustainable development and global citizenship). 
When these four components of participatory urban flood modeling 
work together through digital twins with a bottom-up approach, they 
can significantly contribute to achieving the following six SDG targets: 
Target 1.5, 6.5, 11.5, 11.B, 13.1, and 16.7. 
Participatory urban flood modeling empowers communities by 
involving them throughout the entire process. This approach not only 
enables them to identify pluvial flood risks but also to collectively 
devise, verify, and implement strategies that mitigate the social-

6. Conclusion 
To address the escalating threats of urban pluvial flooding, it is 
required to leverage digital twin technology with a bottom-up approach, 
enhancing disaster preparedness and response at both individual and 
community levels. This paper presents a participatory modeling frame -
work by highlighting four pivotal components — open source software, 
virtual reality, a minimum viable product, and gamification — 
embodying the essence of the digital twin era. Firstly, our novel 
framework encompasses key processes of input data preparation, hy -
drological model construction, model verification, and experimentation. 
Secondly, this framework was applied in the Gangnam region of Seoul, 
South Korea, by coupling a one-dimensional stormwater model with a 
rapid flood spreading model. The case study demonstrated that the 
model achieved plausible accuracy when evaluated using double vali -
dation, which combines quantitative (i.e., Lee-Sallee index = 0.89, NSE 
= 0.52, and RSR = 0.69) and qualitative (i.e., visual comparison with 
social media images) verification methods. Through participatory 
modeling, citizens can examine the pluvial flood mitigation effects of 
both green and grey infrastructure strategies, as well as collective action, 
under various climate change scenarios. As a result, grey infrastructure 
more effectively reduces flood volume than green infrastructure (49.9% 
vs. 9.9% on average) across various climate change scenarios, but 
combining them can maximize the flood reduction (58.8%). So as to 
ensure consistent and reliable performance amidst climate change un -
certainty, adopting coupled green-grey infrastructure and collective 
flood mitigation strategies is crucial for the study area. This framework 
promotes a paradigm shift by transforming citizens ’ roles from passive 
receivers to proactive contributors in making cities and society more 
sustainable against climate-related disasters. Ultimately, this framework 
embarks on paving a pathway towards fostering more informed 
decision-making, armed with the digital twin technology and commu -
nity engagement, ready to confront extreme weather-induced 
challenges.
