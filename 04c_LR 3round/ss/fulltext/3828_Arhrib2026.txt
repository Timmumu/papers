Abstract
While infrastructure asset management increasingly relies on high-resolution drone im-
agery, existing workflows suffer from fragmented information management and depen-
dence on costly local processing infrastructure. This paper addresses these limitations by
using a cloud-native spatial intelligence hub that converts raw inspection imagery into
an interactive and queryable three-dimensional information layer. The system integrates
a timeout-resilient orchestration layer for photogrammetry pipelines, a multi-user three-
dimensional environment for collaborative review, and a PostGIS-backed spatial database
that stores defects as georeferenced anchors. We further introduce a spatial anchoring
workflow mapping three-dimensional interactions to world coordinates, retrieving context-
relevant images via frustum-based visibility scoring. Evaluated on real inspection datasets,
the serverless architecture achieved end-to-end reconstruction in under one hour with
sub-25 ms query latency. Results indicate that acquisition geometry, particularly oblique
convergent viewpoints, is a stronger predictor of reconstruction complexity than image
count. This work establishes a reproducible reference architecture, enabling a transition
from file-centric documentation to traceable, spatially indexed evidence management for
infrastructure Digital Twins.
Keywords: spatial intelligence; serverless computing; infrastructure inspection; Digital
Twins; photogrammetry; web-based 3D visualization; cloud-native architecture; semantic
spatial anchoring
1. Introduction
The Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (AEC) sector has made significant
progress in digitizing design and delivery phases, largely through Building Information
Modeling (BIM). However, the Operation and Maintenance (O&M) phase, while constitut-
ing approximately 60–80% of an asset’s total lifecycle cost, remains plagued by systemic
inefficiencies rooted in data fragmentation [1–3].
Infrastructure asset management relies heavily on inspection workflows that generate
vast quantities of heterogeneous data, including high-resolution images, unstructured pdf
reports, and tabular defect logs stored in isolated spreadsheets [ 4,5]. The fundamental
problem facing facility management is not a lack of data, but a lack of context. In current
practice, a structural engineer might identify a shear crack in a raw drone image (File A),
log its width in an Excel spreadsheet (File B), and describe its severity in a formal report
(File C). As illustrated in Figure 1, this fragmentation results in “dead data”: information

trapped in disconnected silos, severing the semantic link between a defect’s visual evidence

Empirical studies indicate that while Common Data Environments (CDEs) were
intended to solve this via ISO 19650 standards [7–9], current implementations often function
merely as “digital filing cabinets”, [10] managing files rather than granular information.
This disconnect prevents organizations from achieving portfolio-level visibility and inhibits
the transition from reactive repairs to predictive maintenance [11,12].
The urgency of this transition is particularly acute in emerging markets, such as
Morocco and the broader North African region, where rapid urbanization creates a pressing
need to maintain both legacy and modern infrastructure. However, the adoption of high-
fidelity Digital Twin technologies in these regions faces an upfront economic barrier: the
Capital Expenditure (CapEx) required for local processing hardware.
Traditional photogrammetric workflows and “Scan-to-BIM” processes are computa-
tionally punishing. They necessitate high-end local workstations equipped with enterprise-
grade GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA RTX series) and expensive perpetual software licenses for
Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in emerging markets; this upfront investment
is prohibitive [ 2,13,14]. Furthermore, the workload of an inspection firm is inherently
“bursty”; intense data processing is required immediately after a drone flight, followed by
periods where expensive hardware sits idle, rendering the fixed-cost workstation model
economically inefficient [15–17].
To overcome these hardware limitations, this research posits that emerging markets have
a unique opportunity for “Technological Leapfrogging”. Just as mobile telephony in Africa
bypassed landlines [18,19], construction firms can bypass the “desktop software” phase entirely
by adopting cloud-native platforms [20]. By moving from local, file-based workflows to a
serverless cloud architecture [21], we can decouple inspection capabilities from local hardware
constraints, converting prohibitive CapEx into flexible Operational Expenditure (OpEx).
To enable this transition, we propose the concept of a “Spatial Intelligence Hub”. Un-
like traditional “Level 1” Digital Twins, which often serve as passive 3D visualization[3,22],
this architecture treats the 3D reconstruction as a “spatial organizing substrate” [ 23–25].
In this paradigm, the distinction matters operationally. When a defect is recorded as an
unstructured text description (“north façade, third floor, near expansion joint”), matching
it to its physical location requires human interpretation at every subsequent access.

Conversely, when the defect is recorded as a three-dimensional coordinate anchored
to a georeferenced mesh surface, the same defect can be retrieved by any spatial query
that includes that coordinate regardless of who wrote the original description or when.
This approach creates a “Semantic Digital Twin” where geometry and business logic are
integrated into a unified system [26]. Consequently, this allows for bidirectional information
flow: users can query the model to find defects or query the database to locate them in 3D
space [24].
The mathematical foundations of recovering 3D structure from 2D images are well-
established through Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [27,28], and Multi-View Stereo (MVS)
algorithms [29]. Open-source toolkits such as COLMAP have democratized access to these
algorithms, allowing for the generation of high-fidelity sparse and dense point clouds from
unordered image collections. While recent advances in Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)
and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) offer superior visual fidelity for rendering, traditional
SfM/MVS remains the gold standard for engineering applications requiring explicit geo-
metric accuracy and metric measurements [30–32]. However, these tools typically operate
as monolithic software stacks designed for vertical scaling, necessitating high-end local
workstations equipped with enterprise-grade GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA RTX series) and signifi-
cant RAM. This hardware dependency creates a “Digital Divide”, particularly in emerging
markets where the high Capital Expenditure (CapEx) for such workstations is prohibitive
for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) [2]. Furthermore, because inspection workloads
are inherently bursty, requiring intense processing immediately after a flight followed
by idle periods, the traditional workstation model represents an economically inefficient
allocation of resources [15,16].
While the industry has moved toward Common Data Environments (CDEs) to comply
with ISO 19650 standards, empirical studies indicate that current implementations often
function merely as digital filing cabinets, managing files rather than granular information.
This file-centric approach results in dead data, where defect logs, PDF reports, and 2D
images remain semantically disconnected from the geometric reality of the asset.
Although Building Information Modeling (BIM) offers semantic richness, it is funda-
mentally design-centric, assuming data flows from design to operations. This paradigm is
poorly suited for post-construction inspection, where as-built reality diverges from design
intent and requires costly Scan-to-BIM manual modeling to reconcile.
Consequently, most infrastructure Digital Twins remain at level 1 maturity serving
as passive visual replicas that lack the spatial intelligence to support complex queries or
automated maintenance planning [3,33].
Cloud computing offers a potential solution to hardware constraints; however, tradi-
tional lift-and-shift strategies migrating virtual machines to the cloud fail to address the
cost inefficiencies of idle hardware [15]. Serverless computing (e.g., AWS Lambda, Google
Cloud Run) offers a superior pay-for-value model but introduces significant architectural
challenges for photogrammetry [16].
Standard serverless containers are stateless and impose strict execution time limits
(timeouts) [34], making them historically unsuitable for long-running SfM/MVS pipelines
that can take hours to process. While recent work has explored serverless architectures
for lightweight geospatial tasks like tile serving, there remains a paucity of research on
orchestrating heavy, stateful reconstruction pipelines in a serverless environment [35–37].
An additional gap concerns workload estimation. Existing deployments often approximate
processing cost using simple input metadata such as image count or file size, despite pho-
togrammetric literature demonstrating that the computational complexity of Multi-View
Stereo is strongly influenced by acquisition geometry, particularly viewing angle diver-
sity and baseline-to-depth ratio, rather than image count or Ground Sampling Distance

Many existing implementations rely on monolithic architectures or persistent virtual
machines to handle resource-intensive Structure-from-Motion (SfM) and Multi-View Stereo
(MVS) tasks. This centralized approach often leads to severe data fragmentation, processing
bottlenecks, and high idle infrastructure costs, which limit the deployment of continuous
monitoring platforms. Furthermore, the existing literature lacks comprehensive frame-
works that seamlessly integrate stateless, serverless computational pipelines with precise
spatial anchoring. To address these gaps, this paper proposes a fully decoupled cloud-
native architecture that eliminates continuous infrastructure overhead while maintaining
geometric and semantic integrity during 3D reconstruction.
Unlike pure computer vision studies that focus narrowly on defect detection algo-

The primary contributions of this paper are:
1. Serverless reference architecture coupling timeout-resilient photogrammetric orches-
tration with spatial evidence management, validated on real UAV inspection datasets.
2. Formalized spatial anchoring model that binds defect records to georeferenced 3D
mesh coordinates via raycasting, frustum-based image retrieval, and PostGIS spa-

reconstructions into queryable evidence substrates.
3. Consumption-based economic model that converts capital-intensive photogrammetry
infrastructure to pay-per-use Operational Expenditure, lowering the adoption barrier
for SMEs in emerging markets.
4. Open-source release of the complete implementation as a reproducible reference
architecture for construction informatics research.
5. An empirical demonstration that acquisition geometry, not image count, is the primary
driver of MVS complexity in a serverless context, with practical implications for cloud
job scheduling and resource allocation.
To guide the development and evaluation of this proposed architecture, this study
addresses three primary research questions (RQs):
RQ1 (System Architecture and Scalability). How can resource-intensive, stateful photogram-

2. Materials and Methods
This section details the technical implementation of the spatial intelligence hub, orga-
nized into three subsystems: the serverless reconstruction pipeline, the spatial anchoring
mechanism, and the collaborative information management layer.
2.1. Architectural Overview and Design Principles
The system architecture adheres to three fundamental design principles that distin-
guish it from traditional desktop photogrammetry workflows:
Principle 1: Decoupling of Compute and State
Traditional photogrammetry applications tightly couple user interface, data manage-
ment, and computational processing within monolithic desktop executables. This coupling
creates resource contention where heavy reconstruction tasks monopolize workstation
resources, preventing concurrent user interaction.
The proposed architecture strictly separates stateful components (user data, project
metadata, camera poses) managed by lightweight web services from stateless computa-
tional tasks (SfM processing, mesh generation) executed in ephemeral containers. This
separation enables the user interface to remain responsive during long-running reconstruc-
tions and allows computational resources to scale independently of user load.
Principle 2: Event-Driven Asynchrony with Durable Execution
Photogrammetric reconstruction inherently involves long-running processes that
exceed typical HTTP timeout limits. Rather than maintaining persistent connections or
implementing complex polling mechanisms, the proposed architecture employs an event-
driven architecture where state transitions trigger asynchronous workflows.
Crucially, we utilize durable execution semantics where workflow progress is check-
pointed, enabling automatic recovery from transient failures without restarting entire
reconstruction pipelines [41]. This approach transforms the traditional “keep the worksta-
tion running overnight” workflow into “upload and receive notification when complete”.
Principle 3: API-First Interoperability
To combat the data fragmentation problem identified in the Introduction, the system
exposes all functionality through documented REST APIs. Geometric data is served via
OGC-standard 3D Tiles, defect records are queryable through GeoJSON endpoints, and
camera poses are accessible as structured JSON. This API-first design enables integration
with enterprise asset management systems, GIS platforms, and custom analytical tools
without vendor lock-in.

“Inngest (v3, Inngest Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA) durable execution model” solves
the fundamental challenge of long-running photogrammetry in serverless contexts. Tra-
ditional serverless platforms impose strict timeout limits (typically 15 min for AWS
Lambda [44], 60 min for Cloud Run [45]). A complete SfM reconstruction of 500+ images
may require several hours. Inngest decomposes workflows into discrete steps with explicit
dependencies, automatically handling retries, timeouts, and state persistence. Each step
executes within timeout bounds, while the overall workflow can span arbitrary durations.
“Google Cloud Run (Gen 2, Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA)” provides server-
less container execution with several advantages over function-based platforms. Containers
encapsulate the complete toolchain including GDAL, PDAL, and native binaries, avoiding
the “dependency packaging” challenges of AWS Lambda. Cloud Run’s automatic scaling
from zero to hundreds of concurrent instances enables handling simultaneous reconstruc-
tion requests without manual capacity planning. The pay-per-use billing model aligns costs
directly with computational work performed [45].
“Supabase’s (v2, Supabase Pte. Ltd., Singapore) PostGIS integration” transforms
the PostgreSQL (v15, PostgreSQL Global Development Group, Berkeley, CA, USA)
database into a spatial database management system capable of storing and indexing three-
dimensional geometries. This is foundational to the spatial anchoring mechanism described
in the spatial anchor mechanism section. Row-Level Security policies enforce multi-tenant
data isolation at the database layer, providing defense-in-depth against application-level
security vulnerabilities.

Stage 1: Image Ingestion and Validation
Users upload drone imagery through the browser interface, which performs client-
side validation checking for GPS EXIF tags and minimum image resolution requirements.
Images are streamed directly to S3 using pre-signed URLs, bypassing application servers to
avoid bandwidth bottlenecks. Upon the successful upload images, the uploaded event is
published to Inngest’s event bus.
Stage 2: Feature Extraction
The event triggers a Cloud Run container executing a feature extraction module. For
each image, SIFT (Scale-Invariant Feature Transform) key points are detected and described,
typically identifying 2000–10,000 features per image depending on scene texture [ 46].
Features are persisted to Google Cloud Storage as serialized descriptor files. Processing
parallelizes across images with each container handling a batch of 10–20 images, enabling
linear scaling with dataset size.
Stage 3: Feature Matching and Geometric Verification
A subsequent workflow step loads all feature descriptors and performs pairwise
matching using an approximate nearest neighbor search (FLANN) [47]. Putative matches
undergo geometric verification through RANSAC-estimated fundamental matrices, fil-
tering spurious correspondences caused by repetitive textures or moving objects [ 48].
The output is a sparse graph where nodes represent images and edges represent verified
geometric relationships with sufficient overlap for reconstruction [49].
Stage 4: Incremental Structure-from-Motion
The core SfM reconstruction employs incremental bundle adjustment. The algorithm
selects an initial image pair with strong baseline separation, triangulates matched features
to initialize a sparse 3D point cloud, then iteratively registers additional images by solving
the Perspective-n-Point problem [27]. After each image registration, global bundle adjust-
ment refines all camera parameters and 3D point locations simultaneously, minimizing
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 9 of 32
reprojection error across the entire dataset. This stage outputs camera-intrinsic (focal length,
principal point, distortion coefficients), camera-extrinsic (position and orientation for each
image), and a sparse point cloud. Critically, camera extrinsic parameters are serialized to a
JSON format and stored in S3, enabling the spatial anchoring mechanism.
Stage 5: Multi-View Stereo Densification
The sparse reconstruction is densified through patch-based MVS [ 50]. Depth hy-
potheses are propagated and refined through iterative PatchMatch search, with each pixel
storing a depth value and surface normal defining a slanted plane hypothesis. Multi-view
photometric consistency, measured via normalized cross-correlation across neighboring
views, drives hypothesis refinement. Depth maps are subsequently fused through geomet-
ric consistency filtering, rejecting estimates that are not corroborated across a minimum
number of views. The output dense point cloud typically contains 10–100 million points
depending on image resolution and scene complexity.
Stage 6: Mesh Generation and Web Optimization
The dense point cloud is converted to a textured mesh through Screened Poisson
surface reconstruction, which treats meshing as solving a spatial indicator function whose
gradient best approximates point normal [51]. The resulting watertight mesh undergoes
decimation targeting 500k triangles for web rendering.
Texture generation employs view-dependent blending following the multi-view tex-
turing approach of Waechter et al. [ 52], compositing image contributions weighted by
viewing angle and distance. Finally, the mesh is tiled into an OGC 3D Tiles format with a
hierarchical level-of-detail, enabling progressive streaming in CesiumJS (v1.115, Cesium
GS, Inc., Philadelphia, PA, USA). The tileset is uploaded to S3 with public read access, and
the project status is updated in Supabase, triggering a notification to the user.
The event-driven architecture provides robust failure handling. If feature matching
fails due to insufficient image overlap, the workflow automatically retries with relaxed
matching thresholds. If bundle adjustment fails to converge (often caused by poor camera
calibration or extreme scene geometry), the system falls back to sequential SfM with
incremental validation. Each failure mode is logged with diagnostic information enabling
manual intervention if automated recovery fails. The durable execution model ensures
that successful intermediate stages (e.g., feature extraction) are not discarded, avoiding
redundant computation upon retry.
A recognized architectural trade-off of stateless serverless execution is the I/O over-
head associated with persisting and reloading intermediate reconstruction artifacts, such
as feature descriptors, sparse point clouds, and depth maps, between pipeline stages. Be-
cause each stage executes within an ephemeral container, the intermediate state cannot be
retained across stage boundaries and must therefore be externalized to object storage.
Several design decisions mitigate this overhead. First, intermediate artifacts are stored in
compact binary formats, including compressed numerical arrays, reducing storage require-
ments compared with verbose text-based representations. Second, object storage access is
restricted to stage boundaries, whereby each processing stage performs a single read operation
at startup and a single write operation upon completion, avoiding repeated transfers during
execution. Third, compute services and object storage are deployed within the same cloud
region, minimizing network latency and eliminating cross-region transfer costs.
Although this design introduces additional data movement relative to monolithic
architectures, it enables fault-tolerant checkpointing, independent stage recovery, and
elastic scaling. For the inspection-oriented workloads evaluated in this study, the resulting
I/O overhead remained operationally acceptable and did not offset the economic and
deployment advantages of the serverless architecture.

Algorithm 1. Frustum-based image retrieval for a 3D anchor point
Input:
Anchor point p on the 3D mesh
Set of cameras C = {ci}, with known poses and intrinsics
Integer k, number of images to return
Output:
Ranked list of top-k inspection images Ik
Procedure:
Initialize an empty list of candidate images S.
For each camera ci in C:
Project point p into the camera’s local coordinate system. If the point lies
behind the camera or outside the 2D image boundaries, discard the
camera and continue.
Perform a raycast from the camera center to p. If the ray intersects the
mesh before reaching p, discard the camera (point is occluded).
Calculate the composite score Scomposite based on the camera’s Euclidean
distance to p and the incidence angle against the surface normal.
Append the camera and its score to S.
Sort S in descending order of score.

3. Results
3.1. Experimental Setup
To validate the performance and feasibility of the proposed serverless architecture,
we conducted experiments using two distinct datasets representing typical infrastructure
inspection scenarios.
Dataset A comprises 167 UAV images of The Mill building in London, acquired
using a mapping-oriented flight configuration combining predominantly nadir imagery
with a single oblique pass. The building is characterized by a pitched roof with two
dominant planar slopes intersecting along a ridge line. Images were captured using a
Hasselblad L1D-20c camera (DJI, Shenzhen, China.) at approximately 21.3 m altitude with
80% forward overlap and 80% lateral overlap, yielding a Ground Sampling Distance (GSD)
of approximately 0.97 cm/pixel.
This nadir-dominated viewing geometry provides strong intersection angles for hori-
zontal roof surfaces but produces weak network configuration for vertical façade elements,
reflecting a common limitation in urban aerial survey workflows where façades are not the
primary acquisition target.
Dataset B consists of 85 images of a building roof structure at EPFL (Swiss Federal
Institute of Technology, Lausanne, Switzerland), acquired at an altitude of 30 m AGL by
a Phantom FC330 drone (DJI, Shenzhen, China.) using a circular oblique survey pattern,
providing a Ground Sampling Distance (GSD)of approximately 1.67 cm/px. Despite
the coarser spatial resolution compared to Dataset A, the oblique convergent viewing
geometry creates diverse triangulation angles and high baseline-to-depth ratios across the
image network. The scene contains photovoltaic panels, skylights, chimneys, and service
installations that introduce significant local depth variation and rich, non-repetitive texture.
The selection of two geometrically distinct datasets was deliberate rather than op-
portunistic, as illustrated in the survey flight configurations of Figure 7. Dataset A and
Dataset B were chosen to represent opposite ends of the acquisition geometry spectrum
encountered in practice: nadir-dominated grid surveys, which dominate commercial in-
spection workflows due to their simplicity, and circular oblique surveys, which represent
best-practice photogrammetric network design for complex rooftop structures. This ge-
ometric contrast allows the evaluation to probe the system’s behavior under conditions
where the primary driver of computational complexity as established in photogrammetric
literature shifts from image count to network geometry [38–40]. A homogeneous sample of
similarly acquired datasets would obscure precisely the architectural hypothesis being eval-
uated, namely that checkpoint-based orchestration is necessary because MVS complexity is
geometry-driven and therefore unpredictable from input metadata alone. The two-dataset
design thus constitutes a controlled contrast rather than a statistical sample, consistent with
the paper’s goal of demonstrating architectural feasibility and identifying the dominant
complexity driver rather than establishing population-level performance bounds.
This configuration generates high parallax and strong multi-view intersection geom-
etry, making the dataset suitable for evaluating fine-scale surface reconstruction under
optimal network conditions. Both datasets were processed without ground control points;
camera positions were initialized from GPS EXIF metadata embedded at capture.

3.4. Spatial Anchoring Validation
3.4.1. Image Retrieval Accuracy
Manual functional evaluation was conducted across 30 randomly sampled anchor
points distributed to ensure spatial coverage: 12 points on vertical façade surfaces, 11 points
on roof planes, and seven points at geometrically complex zones including building corners,
dormers, and roof-to-wall transitions. For each anchor, the top-ranked image returned by
the frustum-based scoring mechanism was assessed against two criteria: (1) the anchor
point must be visible within the image frame without occlusion, and (2) the viewing angle
must be sufficient to resolve surface texture at a scale appropriate for defect inspection.
The mechanism demonstrated correct retrieval in 28 of the 30 cases, corresponding to a
top-1 accuracy of 93.3%. For a binomial proportion of 28 successes in 30 trials, the 95%
Wilson confidence interval was [77.9%, 98.8%], reflecting the wide uncertainty inherent
in a 30-sample evaluation. This interval should be interpreted as indicative rather than
definitive; a minimum sample of 100–150 anchor points across diverse building typologies
would be required to bound top-1 accuracy within ±5 percentage points at 95% confidence.
The current evaluation is therefore sufficient to demonstrate functional correctness of the
mechanism but insufficient to establish operational performance guarantees.
The two failure cases occurred exclusively at building corners where grazing viewing
angles caused the highest-scored image to capture the anchor point at the extreme image
periphery, satisfying the frustum containment condition geometrically while providing
insufficient surface resolution for inspection purposes. This failure mode is predictable
from first principles: the composite scoring function penalizes large incidence angles via the
normal-direction dot product term n·vi, but does not explicitly penalize high eccentricity
within the image frame. A straightforward mitigation adding an image-plane centrality
term to the scoring function is identified as a targeted improvement for future work.
Critically, both failure cases were concentrated at a single geometric category (exterior
corners) that represents a known limitation of nadir-dominated acquisition geometry
rather than a deficiency of the retrieval mechanism itself; re-acquisition with a targeted
oblique pass at these locations would eliminate the failure mode at the data collection stage.
These results confirm the functional correctness of the frustum-based approach across the
dominant surface categories encountered in building inspection.
3.4.2. Spatial Query Performance
We populated the defects table with 500 synthetic test records distributed across the
reconstructed building. Spatial proximity queries using PostGIS’s ST_DWithin function
executed in 15–25 ms with the GIST spatial index, compared to 800–1200 ms without
indexing (sequential scan). This confirms that the spatial indexing layer supports interactive
query response times across large defect inventories, validating the architecture’s suitability
for portfolio-wide spatial queries within an operational inspection interface.
To contextualize these results operationally, sub-25 ms query latency places spatial
defect retrieval within the same response-time budget as standard database lookups in
enterprise asset management systems, which typically target under 100 ms for interactive
workflows [12]. The 40–50× speedup provided by GIST spatial indexing over sequential
scan is consistent with theoretical expectations for spatial index structures on point ge-
ometries, where query complexity reduces from O(n) to O(log n) [24]. More significantly,
the measured latency was achieved on a dataset of 500 records distributed across a single
building footprint. For portfolio-scale deployments spanning hundreds of buildings with
tens of thousands of defect records, query performance will depend on the spatial distri-
bution of anchors relative to the query geometry and the selectivity of the ST_DWithin
radius parameter. The PostGIS geography cast used in the implementation ensures that
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 20 of 32
distance calculations account for Earth’s curvature, maintaining metric accuracy for cross-
building queries spanning geographic distances where planar approximations introduce
unacceptable error. These characteristics establish that the spatial indexing layer is architec-
turally appropriate for operational deployment at the building-portfolio scale targeted by
the platform.
The 500-record evaluation confirms interactive latency for a single-building deploy-
ment. For portfolio-scale scenarios, 200 buildings, each with 500–1000 defect records,
totaling 100,000–200,000 spatial records, query performance will depend on the selectivity
of the ST_DWithin radius and the geographic extent of the portfolio. At this scale, standard
GIST indexing on a single PostgreSQL instance is anticipated to maintain sub-100 ms
latency for well-parameterized proximity queries, based on published PostGIS benchmarks
for point geometry datasets of comparable size. For portfolios exceeding ~1 million records
or spanning multiple geographic regions, partitioning strategies, either range-based par-
titioning by building identifier or geographic partitioning by bounding box, would be
required to prevent full-index scans. The current single-instance Supabase deployment is
appropriate for the pilot-scale use cases targeted in this work; distributed scaling via read
replicas or Citus-based sharding represents a natural extension for enterprise deployment.
4. Discussion
4.1. Architectural Contribution to Construction Informatics
This work demonstrates that thoughtful system architecture addressing workflow
integration can deliver substantial practical value independent of algorithmic sophistica-
tion. While much academic attention focuses on improving defect detection accuracy or
reconstruction fidelity, practitioners struggle with more fundamental challenges around
data organization, multi-stakeholder collaboration, and portfolio-scale visibility. The spatial
intelligence hub addresses these practical needs through three architectural patterns:
Event-Driven Decomposition for Heavy Compute. The photogrammetry pipeline
demonstrates that computationally intensive geospatial workflows can be successfully
disaggregated into event-triggered microservices without sacrificing reliability. This pattern
is generalizable beyond photogrammetry to other construction monitoring applications
including BIM model synchronization, clash detection, and energy simulation.
Geometry as Database Interface. By treating the 3D reconstruction as a spatial orga-
nizing substrate rather than passive visualization, we enable a fundamentally different
interaction paradigm. Users navigate spatial reality (the 3D model) rather than abstract file
structure, and the system automatically maintains bidirectional linkage between geometric
locations and business records. This pattern could be applied to other asset management
domains including MEP system maintenance and historic preservation.
While the proposed architecture relies on a traditional Structure-from-Motion (SfM)
and Multi-View Stereo (MVS) pipeline to generate this geometric interface, a theoretical
comparison with emerging view-synthesis paradigms, such as Neural Radiance Fields
(NeRF) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), highlights critical operational trade-offs. NeRF
and 3DGS have gained significant academic attention for their ability to render highly pho-
torealistic, continuous representations of complex scenes from sparse imagery. However,
these implicit and explicit radiance field methods are optimized primarily for visual fidelity
rather than discrete geometric engineering metrics. For infrastructure inspection applica-
tions requiring explicit metric geometry, traditional MVS remains better aligned with the
requirements of the proposed spatial anchoring workflow because it reconstructs explicit,
geometrically verifiable polygonal meshes and point clouds with a true metric scale.
This explicit geometry is mathematically required for raycasting-based coordinate
extraction and PostGIS spatial indexing. Furthermore, training NeRFs or optimizing
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 21 of 32
millions of 3D Gaussians introduces immense computational overhead, often requiring
high-end, persistent GPU infrastructure that directly conflicts with the low-cost, stateless,
CPU-friendly serverless economics demonstrated on this platform. Consequently, while
view-synthesis methods excel at visual walkthroughs, the SfM/MVS paradigm remains
the essential mathematical substrate for queryable, spatially anchored asset intelligence.
Serverless Democratization of Specialized Tools. The economic analysis demonstrates
that converting capital-intensive desktop applications into serverless cloud services can reduce
adoption barriers by an order of magnitude. This democratization is particularly impactful
in emerging markets where SMEs lack capital for high-end workstations but can afford
modest per-project processing fees. This pattern represents a potential strategy for digitally
transforming other AEC workflows that currently require expensive specialized software.
Acquisition Geometry as a Cloud Provisioning Signal. The comparative reconstruction
experiment surfaces an implication for serverless system design. While it is well established
in photogrammetry that MVS complexity scales with network geometry rather than image
count, the finding is that oblique convergent surveys produce denser, more computationally
demanding reconstructions than nadir-dominated surveys of an equivalent or larger size.
Future work should investigate lightweight geometric complexity estimators, derivable
from image network topology prior to execution that would enable dynamic container
provisioning without a pre-processing run.
The empirical results reported in Section 3 provide concrete grounding for several
claims that have been asserted but not previously measured in the context of serverless
cloud deployment. Remondino et al. [39] established theoretically that dense image match-
ing complexity scales with viewing angle diversity and baseline-to-depth ratio rather than
image resolution, and Haala and Rothermel [38] demonstrated this effect experimentally
in batch desktop processing. The results presented here extend these findings to a server-
less, pay-per-use execution context where the cost implications are direct and immediate:
Dataset B required 38% more MVS time despite containing 49% fewer images, a cost inver-
sion that a naive image-count-based resource allocation model would mishandle in both
directions simultaneously, over-provisioning Dataset A and under-provisioning Dataset B.
This finding has a concrete implication for cloud cost management that has not been
previously articulated: acquisition geometry metadata, specifically whether a survey uses
nadir-dominated or oblique convergent flight patterns, should be treated as a first-class
input to serverless job scheduling, not merely as a photogrammetric quality parameter. The
checkpoint-based orchestration design adopted in this work is a pragmatic response to this
uncertainty; future work on lightweight geometry complexity estimators, as proposed in
Section 5, represents a path toward proactive rather than reactive resource allocation.
4.2. Technological Leapfrogging and Economic De-Risking
The results give some empirical grounding to the leapfrogging argument made in the
introduction. In the traditional workstation paradigm, the requirement to purchase high-
performance hardware creates a “sunk cost” barrier: organizations must commit capital

creates a chicken-and-egg problem for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs), particularly
in emerging markets like Morocco.
For a firm in Casablanca or Fez, a high-end workstation represents a significant
percentage of an SME’s discretionary budget, requiring executive approval and multi-year
depreciation schedules. By transitioning to a serverless, consumption-based model, the
proposed platform resolves this paradox. It converts a high-barrier Capital Expenditure
(CapEx) into a scalable, low-risk Operational Expenditure (OpEx).

4.3. Integration with Existing BIM Ecosystems
A frequent question is how the spatial intelligence hub relates to existing Building
Information Modeling (BIM) platforms. We emphasize that the system is complementary
rather than competitive. BIM excels at design-phase information management where building
geometry is authored through parametric modeling and enriched with semantic specifications.
This design-centric information flows forward through construction for coordination.
However, BIM platforms assume information originates during design and struggle
with post-construction scenarios where as-built reality must be captured through sensor
data. While scan-to-BIM workflows exist for converting point clouds to parametric models,
these require substantial manual effort (20–40 h of modeling per building) and often
discard high-frequency geometric detail present in photogrammetric reconstructions. The
spatial intelligence hub addresses this gap by accepting that post-construction reality often
diverges from design intent and therefore requires direct geometric capture rather than
parametric reconstruction.
The photogrammetric mesh serves as a bottom-up representation of as-built conditions
onto which inspection data is anchored. This as-built Digital Twin complements top-down
BIM models where they exist and provides comparable spatial organization benefits for

4.4. Limitations and Boundary Conditions
Several technical limitations constrain the system’s applicability:
Photogrammetric Reconstruction Requirements. Structure-from-Motion algorithms
require sufficient visual texture variation for feature matching. Surfaces with uniform
appearance (e.g., blank concrete panels, monotone metal cladding) or highly repetitive
patterns (e.g., grid facades, brick coursing) can challenge reconstruction. Highly reflective
or transparent materials (glass curtain walls, polished stone) violate brightness constancy
assumptions underlying photometric stereo. These failure modes can be mitigated through
targeted data collection strategies (e.g., adding artificial texture markers, capturing multiple
exposures) but represent inherent constraints of vision-based reconstruction.
Beyond complete reconstruction failure, partial data quality degradation presents a
more common operational risk. Low-contrast or motion-blurred images reduce SIFT key
point repeatability, increasing the proportion of images that fail geometric verification and
are excluded from the reconstruction graph. The system’s event-driven retry logic handles
this gracefully at the feature-matching stage by relaxing match thresholds, but does not
currently detect or report which images were excluded, leaving the inspector unaware of
coverage gaps. A coverage gap visualization overlaying the reconstructed mesh with a
per-region confidence map derived from the number of contributing source images has
been identified as a necessary addition before operational deployment.
Network Connectivity Dependency. The cloud-native architecture assumes reliable
internet connectivity for uploading imagery and accessing reconstructions. In remote
locations or during field work in areas with poor cellular coverage, this dependency creates
workflow friction. Hybrid architectures incorporating local caching or offline-capable
mobile applications could address this limitation but would increase implementation
complexity. For the target use case of urban infrastructure inspection in emerging markets
with expanding 4G/5G coverage, this limitation is increasingly manageable.
Scalability Ceilings. While the serverless architecture scales effectively for moderate
workloads (dozens of concurrent projects), theoretical limits exist. Database query perfor-
mance degrades for extremely large portfolios (hundreds of thousands of defect records)
without careful index tuning and query optimization. Long-term archival storage costs for
high-resolution imagery could become significant for organizations managing hundreds
of buildings inspected repeatedly over decades. Intelligent data lifecycle management
strategies (automated down-sampling of historical imagery, migration to archival storage
tiers, aggregation of temporal data) would be necessary for enterprise-scale deployment.
Absence of Automated Defect Detection. The current implementation requires human
inspectors to manually create defect annotations after visually examining reconstructions
and source imagery. While this manual workflow reflects standard practice in current
inspection operations and was a deliberate design choice, robust spatial anchoring in-
frastructure must precede AI integration, as a detection model is only as useful as the
information substrate to which it writes; it represents the most consequential bottleneck for
scaling inspection throughput. The spatially anchored defect database generated by human
experts through this platform constitutes precisely the kind of georeferenced, evidence-
linked ground truth dataset that supervised defect detection models require but rarely
have access to in structured form. Existing defect detection research typically operates
on unstructured image collections without spatial context, limiting the ability to train
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 25 of 32
models that reason about defect location, progression, or spatial relationship to struc-
tural elements [5,32]. The present system’s output defects annotated with 3D coordinates,
severity classifications, linked source images, and supporting documentation are natively
structured for training spatially aware detection models. This creates a self-reinforcing
improvement cycle: human-in-the-loop annotation through the platform generates training
data; training data enables automated pre-annotation; automated pre-annotation reduces
inspector workload while maintaining human verification. The integration of automated
detection is therefore identified not merely as a feature addition but as an architectural
inevitability that the spatial information infrastructure of the present system is designed
to support.
Parameter Sensitivity and Hyperparameter Optimization. The current evaluation
establishes the end-to-end functional feasibility of the architecture using baseline algorith-
mic parameters. However, we have not yet conducted a formal sensitivity analysis on
the hyperparameter configurations governing the spatial anchoring and reconstruction
engines. For instance, the α parameter within the frustum-based image retrieval scoring
function (Algorithm 1), which balances the weight of surface normal incidence against
Euclidean distance, was empirically fixed rather than systematically optimized. Similarly,
the PatchMatch Multi-View Stereo configuration (e.g., the number of depth hypotheses
and propagation iterations) was held constant across both datasets to isolate the impact of
acquisition geometry on compute time. Because modifying these variables directly affects
both the accuracy of spatial queries and cloud resource consumption, a comprehensive
ablation study and hyperparameter sensitivity analysis represent a critical next step to
mathematically optimize the platform’s operational efficiency.
4.5. Threats to Validity
The measured end-to-end runtime depends on cloud execution conditions such as cold
starts and multi-tenant resource contention. Although the orchestration design mitigates
timeouts, execution time may vary across deployments.
Construct validity: Image retrieval functional correctness is evaluated by manual
verification across 30 sampled anchor points, which may introduce subjectivity in border-
line cases such as corners and repetitive textures. The evaluation sample is sufficient to
demonstrate functional correctness of the mechanism but insufficient to establish statistical
performance bounds for operational deployment. A top-k evaluation across a larger and
more diverse sample is identified as necessary future work to support claims of retrieval
reliability across varying building typologies.
External validity: To properly contextualize the empirical evaluation, the system’s
performance bounds must be evaluated within the scope of system architecture design
rather than a population benchmark study. Accordingly, we satisfied the validation criteria
by clearly defining our selection method as a controlled, purposeful contrast rather than a
statistical population sample. The two-dataset configuration was specifically engineered
to capture opposite ends of the geometric spectrum (nadir-dominated grid vs. circular
oblique convergent networks) to evaluate our dynamic serverless resource-provisioning
hypotheses. However, this deliberate design choice inherently limits broad generalizability
claims, as the system has not yet been stress-tested on asset classes characterized by
challenging photogrammetric boundary conditions, such as glass curtain walls, open-frame
steel structures or underground infrastructure. Validating the architecture across at least
two additional complex building typologies, specifically a masonry heritage structure and
a steel-frame industrial facility, has been established as an immediate follow-on roadmap
item to systematically map external operational bounds.

4.6. Generalizability to Other Construction Monitoring Applications
The architectural patterns demonstrated in this work extend beyond building facade
inspection to other construction monitoring scenarios requiring spatial organization of
heterogeneous data:
Progress-Tracking During Construction. Periodic drone surveys of active construction
sites could be anchored to a common reference geometry, enabling automated change
detection and schedule deviation analysis. The same frustum culling mechanism could
retrieve source images documenting specific construction activities for verification against
approved shop drawings.
Cultural Heritage Documentation. Historic preservation workflows require detailed con-
dition monitoring of irreplaceable structures. The spatial intelligence hub could organize
multi-modal sensor data (photogrammetry , thermal imagery , ground-penetrating radar) around
a unified 3D baseline, supporting conservation planning and disaster recovery preparation.
Infrastructure Resilience Monitoring. Critical infrastructure (bridges, dams, tunnels)
subjected to periodic inspections could accumulate temporal layers of spatially anchored
condition data. Change detection across inspection campaigns would reveal degradation
trends and inform predictive maintenance models, transitioning from reactive repairs to
proactive intervention strategies.
Post-Disaster Damage Assessment. Following earthquakes, floods, or other disasters,
rapid deployment of drone surveys could populate spatial databases with damage annota-
tions. The portfolio-scale query capabilities would enable prioritizing emergency response
based on structural criticality and damage severity, supporting evidence-based resource
allocation decisions.
The common thread across these applications is the same problem this paper addresses:
sensor data that generates observations about physical objects, where the observations
need to be organized by their spatial relationship to those objects rather than by the file
system structure in which they were stored.
5. Conclusions
This paper has presented the design, implementation, and validation of a cloud-native
spatial intelligence hub that addresses data fragmentation in infrastructure inspection
through serverless 3D reconstruction and context-aware information management. The
work makes five principal contributions to construction informatics scholarship:
First, we demonstrate that computationally intensive photogrammetry pipelines can
be successfully decomposed into event-driven serverless workflows, eliminating the need
for dedicated hardware infrastructure. The Inngest-orchestrated architecture handles
the inherent timeout challenges of long-running SfM/MVS processing while providing
robust failure recovery and cost efficiency. Processing cost per typical inspection project
represents an order-of-magnitude reduction compared to workstation depreciation and
operational costs.
Second, we formalize and implement the spatial anchor mechanism that creates bidi-
rectional linkage between database records and three-dimensional geometric locations. The
combination of raycasting for coordinate extraction, frustum culling for image retrieval, and
PostGIS spatial indexing for portfolio-wide queries transforms passive 3D visualizations
into active information-management substrates. This architectural pattern is generalizable
beyond facade inspection to other construction monitoring applications requiring spatial
organization of heterogeneous data.
Third, we demonstrate how cloud-native serverless architectures fundamentally
transform the economic model of infrastructure inspection from capital-intensive to
consumption-based deployment. By eliminating requirements for specialized hardware
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 27 of 32
and perpetual software licenses, the architecture lowers barriers to entry for SMEs in
emerging markets. This shift from upfront Capital Expenditure to pay-per-use Operational
Expenditure represents a technological leapfrogging opportunity, enabling organizations
to adopt advanced photogrammetry capabilities aligned with actual project revenue rather
than speculative infrastructure investment.
Fourth, we release the complete implementation as open-source software, providing a
reproducible reference architecture that other researchers and practitioners can validate,
extend, and adapt to domain-specific requirements. This contribution to open construc-
tion informatics enables collaborative development and prevents vendor lock-in that has
historically fragmented the AEC software ecosystem.
Fifth, we provide an empirical characterization of acquisition geometry as the major
driver of MVS complexity in a serverless cloud context, demonstrating that a dataset
with fewer images and coarser Ground Sampling Distance can require significantly more
processing time than a larger, higher-resolution dataset when oblique convergent geometry
produces higher baseline-to-depth ratios. While this finding is consistent with established
photogrammetric theory, its implications for cloud-native reconstruction pipelines have
received little attention; thus, it provides empirical grounding for the checkpoint-based
orchestration design and establishes acquisition geometry metadata as a necessary input to
cloud resource allocation decisions.
The spatial intelligence hub enables several organizational capabilities for resource-
constrained engineering firms:
Portfolio-Scale Asset Intelligence. By standardizing spatial data schemas across all
inspected buildings, facility managers can execute analytical queries spanning entire port-
folios. Questions like “which building cohorts exhibit similar crack propagation pat-
terns suggesting systemic construction defects” or “where should we prioritize structural
strengthening given limited capital budgets” become answerable through database queries
rather than requiring manual correlation across disconnected reports.
Evidence-Based Maintenance Planning. The persistent spatial anchoring of all inspec-
tion findings creates an evolving historical record of asset condition. Temporal change
detection across repeated inspection campaigns reveals deterioration rates and validates
maintenance intervention effectiveness. This evidence base supports transitioning from
reactive repairs (responding to visible failures) to predictive maintenance (intervening
before failures occur based on degradation trends).
Collaborative Multi-Stakeholder Workflows. Real-time synchronization of defect
annotations and document attachments enables distributed teams to collaborate effectively
without geographic collocation. Field inspectors, structural engineers, facility managers,
and external consultants can jointly review inspection findings, annotate observations, and
make decisions within a shared spatial interface. This reduces coordination overhead and
eliminates information loss from handoffs between stakeholders.
Reduced Barrier to Digital Adoption. The zero-installation browser-based interface
and pay-per-project pricing model make advanced photogrammetry capabilities accessible
to organizations that would never justify purchasing desktop software licenses or high-end
workstations. This democratization is particularly impactful for SMEs in emerging markets,
potentially catalyzing broader digital transformation of regional construction sectors.
Several research directions emerge from this work that would extend capabilities
while maintaining the core architectural principles:
• Automated defect detection: The spatially anchored defect database generated by
human inspectors provides a natural ground truth corpus for training computer vision
models. Future work should explore integrating automated pathology detection into
the platform, using both source imagery and 3D geometric context as inputs. Robust
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 28 of 32
spatial information infrastructure must precede AI integration; the present system
provides that foundation.
• Temporal change detection: Anchoring repeated inspection campaigns to a common
reference geometry would enable automated monitoring of structural deterioration
over time. This would support a transition from reactive maintenance to predictive
intervention, with degradation trends derived from accumulated spatial observations
rather than periodic manual comparison of disconnected reports.
• Advanced visualization with 3D Gaussian Splatting: While SfM meshes provide
the geometric reliability required for spatial anchoring, 3D Gaussian Splatting offers
superior photorealistic rendering that could improve the inspection review experience.
A hybrid architecture using the SfM mesh as the geometric substrate for raycasting
while rendering through a Gaussian Splatting layer would preserve spatial accuracy
while significantly enhancing visual quality.
• Mobile and offline capabilities: The current architecture assumes reliable internet
connectivity, which may be limited at remote inspection sites. Offline-capable mobile
applications that cache project data locally and synchronize when connectivity is
restored would extend the platform’s reach to field environments where the cloud-
native model currently creates friction.
• Standardization and interoperability: The spatial anchor concept could inform de-
velopment of open data exchange standards for inspection information, in collabora-
tion with bodies such as ISO, buildingSMART, and OGC. Standardized schemas for
spatially anchored defect records and documented API specifications would enable
interoperability across platforms and prevent the vendor lock-in that has historically
fragmented construction software ecosystems.
While the current architecture establishes a foundational spatial intelligence hub,
future work should focus on integrating three advanced capabilities into the serverless
pipeline. First, automated defect detection should be integrated by deploying asynchronous
AI inference containers triggered immediately following the final densification stage. The
primary challenge here lies in managing the cold-start latency of loading heavy vision-
model weights within stateless environments. Second, enabling temporal change monitor-
ing will require the pipeline to automatically co-register independent, multi-temporal drone
flights. This introduces the challenge of establishing stable 3D invariant coordinate frames
to accurately calculate mesh deformation over time. Finally, the integration of 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS) has been proposed to enhance photorealistic visualization. Because native
3DGS lacks the explicit polygonal geometry required for the frustum raycasting described
in this study, the proposed integration scheme involves a hybrid rendering architecture:
utilizing the explicit SfM mesh as an invisible structural layer for coordinate calculations,
while overlaying a real-time rasterized 3DGS layer for user visualization.
It is important to characterize precisely what this work demonstrates and what it
does not. The experimental evaluation validates end-to-end feasibility of the proposed
architecture on two real UAV inspection datasets representing geometrically contrasting
acquisition conditions, and confirms sub-second spatial query performance and greater-
than-90% image retrieval accuracy at the building scale. These results establish that the
system works correctly under the tested conditions. They do not establish statistical perfor-
mance bounds across diverse building typologies, acquisition equipment, or geographic
conditions; claims of that nature would require a benchmark study across a substantially
larger and more varied dataset, which is identified as the primary direction for future em-
pirical work. The contribution of this paper is therefore best characterized as a reproducible
reference architecture with demonstrated feasibility, empirically grounded design rationale,
and a clearly defined evaluation roadmap. This framing is consistent with the role of
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16122433
Buildings 2026, 16, 2433 29 of 32
system architecture papers in construction informatics, which establish the design space
and demonstrate viability before large-scale benchmark studies become tractable [3,33].
The fragmentation of inspection data represents a systemic risk to infrastructure
safety and operational efficiency. While computer vision research pursues increasingly
sophisticated detection algorithms and rendering techniques advance toward photorealistic
virtual reality, practitioners struggle with more fundamental challenges around information
organization, multi-stakeholder collaboration, and portfolio-scale asset intelligence.
This work demonstrates that thoughtful system architecture addressing these work-
flow challenges can deliver substantial practical value independent of algorithmic sophisti-
cation. By making 3D Digital Twins accessible through serverless economics and usable
through spatial information management, the spatial intelligence hub charts a path toward
portfolio-scale infrastructure intelligence in emerging markets.
As construction organizations in regions like Morocco and the broader Global South
continue, digital transformation journeys, the choice between replicating developed-market
software accumulation patterns and leapfrogging to integrated cloud-native platforms
will have lasting implications. The spatial intelligence hub represents one instantiation of
the leapfrogging strategy, and we hope this work encourages broader reflection on how
construction informatics can evolve to serve resource-constrained contexts rather than
merely transplanting approaches optimized for capital-rich markets. The open-source
release accompanying this paper invites the research community to validate, critique, and
extend this work.
Construction informatics advances not through isolated algorithmic breakthroughs
but through collective development of robust architectural patterns, standard protocols,
and open implementations that enable ecosystem growth. We welcome collaboration
toward that shared goal.
