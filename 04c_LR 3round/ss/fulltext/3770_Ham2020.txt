Participatory Sensing and Digital Twin City:
Updating Virtual City Models for
Enhanced Risk-Informed Decision-Making
Youngjib Ham, Ph.D., A.M.ASCE 1; and Jaeyoon Kim, S.M.ASCE 2
Abstract: The benefits of a digital twin city have been assessed based on real-time data collected from preinstalled Internet of Things (IoT)
sensors (e.g., traffic, energy use, air pollution, water quality) for managing the complex systems of cities, but the sensor-based reality
information is likely insufficient to provide dynamic spatiotemporal information about physical vulnerabilities. Understanding cities ’ current
states of physical vulnerability can support city decision makers in analyzing associated potential risk in urban areas for data-driven infra-
structure management in extreme weather events. As a step toward creating a digital twin city for effective risk-informed decision-making,
this paper proposes a new framework to bring crowdsourced visual data-based reality information into a three-dimensional (3D) virtual city
for a model update with interactive and immersive visualization. Unstructured visual data are collected from participatory sensing and an-
alyzed to estimate the geospatial information of vulnerable objects in the distance representing physical vulnerability in cities. The crowd-
sourced visual data –based reality information of physical vulnerability in a given region is then integrated with a 3D virtual city model, and
the updated 3D city model is fed into a computer-aided virtual environment (CA VE) for immersive visualization to enable users to navigate
the intersection of reality and virtuality. To test the proposed framework, case studies were conducted on Houston. The outcomes demonstrate
that the proposed method has the potential to make the virtual city model live in terms of local vulnerability. The digital twin city building on
crowdsourced visual data is expected to contribute to risk-informed decision-making for infrastructure management in cities and help analyze
various what-if scenarios in disaster situations with increased visibility of hazard and city interactions. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)ME.1943-

Introduction
The proportion of the world ’s population who live in cities is in-
creasing rapidly, from 54% in 2014 to an estimate of almost 80% by
2050 (United Nations 2014). As a result, city decision makers must
consider how to keep the sustainable and resilient connectivity be-
tween humans, infrastructure, and technologies that is essential for
community functioning in cities. Decision makers continually
struggle with various challenges posed by rapid urbanization and
need to become more strategic in responding to these challenges.
Many cities are increasingly implementing technological develop-
ment for a data-driven approach, which can facilitate effective
decision-making in complex systems of cities. Recently, many
cities have started to create and leverage a virtual replica of a city
(i.e., a digital version of a real city) at the intersection of reality and
virtuality (Mohammadi and Taylor 2017). This new technology has
an important characteristic: real-time data using sensors such as
Internet of Things (IoT) devices to bridge between the real world
and its virtual counterpart and understand relations among essential
constituents of a city, with increased visualization of human –
infrastructure–technology interactions, to facilitate decision-making.
For example, the city of Rennes, in the Brittany region of France,
has carried out the Virtual Rennes project, building a three-
dimensional (3D) virtual twin of the actual city to establish a
new vision of the city, accommodate the growing population, and
design new planning and city management in terms of transportation,
energy consumption, and environment (Cooper 2018). A municipal-
ity of Amsterdam has also developed a digital twin of the Amsterdam
Southeast area for managing energy transition. As a medium of citi-
zen participation, the virtual city enables decision makers to share
creative ideas with others in a dynamic way as well as to devise
potential solutions in a virtual game environment ( Geodan 2018 ;
Oud 2018).
Because the challenges in effective decision-making in such
complex systems are likely to be exacerbated in extreme situations,
the digital twin city, integrated with real-time data collected from
city monitoring systems, has been leveraged to facilitate risk-
informed decision-making given the increasing numbers and im-
pacts of disasters. For example, the Virtual Singapore project has
created a 3D city model with a variety of data sources, such as
geospatial information from government agencies and real-time
data from IoT sensors around the city ( Dassault Systèmes 2018 ;
Holstein 2018 ; Virtual Singapore 2018 ). In this project, the real-
time data recorded from various sensors were brought into contex-
tualized 3D city models to understand potential disaster risk, such
as electrical network failures, and then analyze evacuation models
to optimize evacuation plans and minimize possible structural
damage and loss of life. As such, understanding the states of spa-

In spite of the benefits of the digital twin city with real-time data
collected from preinstalled IoT sensors for monitoring energy use,
air pollution, water quality, noise pollution, and traffic in cities, the
sensor-based reality information is not sufficient to monitor the ex-
tent to which urban environments are vulnerable to potential natural
disasters for both disaster preparedness and hazard mitigation.
Although live data obtained from preinstalled city monitoring sen-
sors enable governmental agencies in major cities to monitor asso-
ciated real-time information in extreme weather events (e.g., current
conditions of electric power systems or major transportation-related
infrastructure), it is not feasible or straightforward to know the
high-resolution contextualized spatiotemporal data about physical
vulnerabilities—widely distributed in cities—that are likely to be af-
fected by extreme weather events. Physical vulnerabilities in cities
(vulnerable objects or potential wind-borne debris around critical in-
frastructure, neighborhood physical disorder, etc.) potentially lead to
primary or secondary hazards in extreme weather events, which may
result in economic, environmental, and social damage to commun-
ities and neighboring interdependent critical infrastructure systems.
A readily available source of such data would likely allow better
identification and understanding of current states of physical vulner-
abilities, which would help predict future behaviors on which such
physical vulnerabilities would have negative impact during fore-
casted extreme weather events, affecting communities and neighbor-
ing critical infrastructure.
To address challenges in preinstalled sensor-based approaches
for in-depth city monitoring, participatory sensing has recently
emerged as one way to document the state of built environments
for disaster management with little incremental cost ( Goldman
et al. 2009 ; Christin et al. 2011 ; Xue et al. 2018 ). Although prior
studies provide encouraging proof-of-concept supporting the effec-
tiveness of crowdsourced data in the context of disaster situations,
they have limitations: (1) they have mostly concentrated on social
media messages in the form of text, and in a few studies using pho-
tos; app users are asked to manually input detailed information on
current damage states shown in photos and associated risks; and
(2) they have primarily attempted to directly link “georeferenced
social media activity ” to “disaster areas ” with the assumption that
problems occur at the location where original photos are taken.
Consequently, objects in the distance of photos that have the
potential for causing cascading risks to cities (e.g., potential wind-
borne debris beside roads or critical facilities, vulnerable cranes on
urban construction sites surrounded by many utility poles) have not
been fully investigated in participatory sensing. Because there are
likely to be many cases in which people report vulnerable objects in
the distance before disaster situations, it is essential to reliably
localize such distant objects that may be vulnerable to extreme
weather events and map their spatiotemporal information (their
geospatial location at the time of the data collector ’s report). This
will help understand the potential risk of physical vulnerabilities in
urban areas, which can support effective risk-informed decision-
making.
To overcome these challenges, and as a step toward creating a
digital twin city for effective risk-informed decision-making, this
paper aims to propose a new participatory-sensing-to-digital twin
city framework. The proposed method leverages unstructured
crowdsourced visual data to identify and localize vulnerable objects
in urban areas, and the resulting spatiotemporal information of
physical vulnerability is integrated with a 3D virtual city model.
The updated 3D city model, reflecting the spatiotemporal informa-
tion of physical vulnerabilities, is then fed into an interactive
computer-aided virtual environment (CA VE) for interactive visuali-
zation of participatory sensing –based reality information in a vir-
tual environment.
The primary contributions of this paper can be summarized as
follows: (1) leveraging unstructured crowdsourced visual data to bet-
ter understand spatiotemporal information of physical vulnerabilities
in cities, enhancing modeling accuracy of cities by reducing the gap
between a real city and its digital replica; and (2) bringing an addi-
tional context dimension into current IoT-based digital twin city
environments by providing an interactive visualization of spatiotem-
poral information of physical vulnerabilities, enabling users to inter-
act with the updated 3D city model in the CA VE at the intersection
of humans–infrastructure–technology. This study contributes to the
body of knowledge of data-driven infrastructure management by
providing a novel participatory sensing-to-digital twin city frame-
work. This framework can help decision makers better understand
the as-is condition of city infrastructure in terms of vulnerability
to anticipated extreme weather events, which can greatly assist
data-driven infrastructure disaster management.
Eventually, the proposed digital twin city models can be used to
simulate various what-if scenarios based on contextualized geospa-
tial and semantic information. This approach can become a basis of
effective risk-informed decision-making for infrastructure manage-
ment in terms of disaster response and recovery, as well as disaster
preparedness and hazard mitigation against potential extreme
weather conditions. In the following sections, previous works on a
digital twin city based on sensor data, participatory sensing for dis-
aster management, and distant object localization are reviewed, and
their challenges are discussed. Then, the proposed methods are pre-
sented in detail. Finally, experimental results of case studies on
Houston, the implications of this research, and open research chal-
lenges are discussed.
Related Works
Digital Twin City and Participatory Sensing in the
Context of Disaster Management
The term “digital twin ” can broadly be defined as a creation of a
dynamic virtual representation of a physical system, along with
connectivity and analytical capabilities using IoT ( National
Infrastructure Commission 2017 ). In particular, in regard to urban
risk management, a digital twin city informed by sensor-based real-
time data has the potential to better understand the spatiotemporal
information in cities, supporting effective risk-informed decision-
making. For instance, a gas leak was simulated in a contextualized
3D city model with reality data to identify potentially affected areas
and inform city dwellers in those areas of escape routes ( Coletta
et al. 2019). As another example, Singapore ’s security forces could
use a digital copy of Singapore to simulate a terror threat at a sports
stadium to prepare appropriate reactions ( Geddie and Aravindan
2018). Similarly, the city of Newcastle has performed a flood sim-
ulation in a computer replica based on abundant real-time data from
sensors installed all around the city ( White 2018 ).
Although live data obtained from monitoring sensors have the
significant potential to improve risk-informed decision-making,
there is still a gap in the current digital twin city, as it is not trivial
to identify and understand physical vulnerability by using prein-
stalled sensors. Critical to disaster response efforts relevant to urban
environments during or after an incident is gathering and making
sense of information. Decision makers in most regions, especially
major cities, already have systems to gather near-real-time informa-
tion about major critical infrastructure (e.g., which bridges are

However, inspecting numerous vulnerable objects in cities is time-

state of built environments during or after disasters [e.g., rainfall

Twitter messages ( Wang et al. 2018 ), rapid inundation mapping
from photos posted in Twitter ( Fohringer et al. 2015 )]. The prior
works assumed that problems occur at the reporting location (where
photos are taken) and thus simply linked “georeferenced social
media activity ” to “disaster areas ” even though in disaster situa-
tions, people likely report vulnerable objects in the distance. Such
distant objects at risk typically have the potential for causing pri-
mary or secondary hazards in cities and require special attention
for disaster preparedness and hazard mitigation. Thus, for effective
risk-informed decision-making through participatory sensing, it is
necessary to understand the spatiotemporal information of distant
objects vulnerable to extreme events.
Geospatial Localization of Distant Objects
Geospatial localization denotes the process of localizing objects or
areas of interest in geographic coordinate systems. In geospatial
localization, the spatial relationship between a sensing device
and an object of interest should be estimated in connection with
an absolute location. Generally, the distance between a sensing de-
vice and an object of interest is directly measured by using active
sensors such as laser scanners. Without active sensors, one alter-
native to measure the distance is to infer the spatial relationship
of an object and a sensing device based on 2D images containing
the object and the geographic location of the sensing device. For
this purpose, intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of cameras are iden-
tified to estimate the 3D geometric information of 2D image con-
tents in connection with the real world ( Piasco et al. 2018 ).
Previous studies on computer vision have presented various
methods to localize distant objects or indoor locations in construc-
tion sites and built environments (such as Ha et al. 2018 ; Ham and
Y oon 2018). However, their applicability could be limited in urban
areas because of the necessity of supplementary information
(e.g., building information models) or a certain monitoring condi-
tion (e.g., additional cameras required to infer the spatial relation-
ship). In recent years, geospatial localization methods for urban
environments have been presented using smartphone sensor data.
Manweiler et al. ( 2012) localized a distant object using the spatial
relationship of 3D point clouds reconstructed from a Structure-
from-Motion, a global positioning system (GPS) location, and a
compass bearing. Ouyang et al. ( 2013) and Chen et al. ( 2016) used
crowdsourcing-based approaches to localize an event in outdoor
environments using shooting directions and GPS locations of
multiple users. However, the prior works yielded unstable locali-
zation accuracies [e.g., distance errors ranging from 5 to 150 m
(Manweiler et al. 2012 )] that would be insufficient to differentiate
objects that are close to each other in urban environments, so the
methods still have room for improvement.

Localizing Distant Objects Vulnerable to Potential
Natural Disasters Using Crowdsourced Visual Data
We first identify the geospatial location of potentially vulnerable
objects in the distance based on mobile sensor data, including the
geographic location of a data collector, a compass bearing toward
a target object, and a target object image using commodity-level
smartphones. With this information, we formulate lines connecting
data collectors to a distant object of interest and then determine the
location of the distant object based on the intersection points of those
lines. With citizen participation, data collection for distant objects is
performed through the two steps as shown in Fig. 2.
For data collection, it is assumed that participants take a photo-
graph of a distant object and point the upper part of smartphone
toward the distant object while leveling it to the ground to report
a compass bearing in a dedicated mobile application program.
Through the data filtering process, we verify whether the photo col-
lected involves a target object of interest. In our experiments, it was
observed that the data collection generally takes about 10 s per in-
stance, which does not result in significant resistance or discomfort
to participants and thus has the potential to be leveraged at scale.
In participatory sensing –based geospatial localization, the data
collected are preprocessed to project a geographic location repre-
sented in spherical coordinates (latitude, longitude, and altitude)
onto the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system.
As a result, the geographic location is represented as 2D Cartesian
coordinates—easting and northing —without altitude information.
This projection facilitates the localization of a distant object in
the proposed method, because the intersection point of multiple line
equations can be more efficiently found in a 2D space than locali-
zation in a 3D space. Moreover, the data accuracy of altitude is not
typically reliable in urban areas because of the interference of
nearby trees and buildings, so to improve localization accuracy,
it is better not to include such unreliable information in the geo-
spatial localization process. If two or more data points for the same
distant object are collected, the geospatial localization method can
promptly identify its location. However, because multiple observa-
tions for different distant objects can be reported simultaneously in
a certain urban area, it is necessary to categorize data points by each
distant object. To that end, we use a two-stage filtering process,
consisting of location-based clustering and object presence –based
filtering.
Location-based clustering divides entire data points into sepa-
rate clusters based on the proximity between contiguous data
points. With this process, data points in a cluster are likely to cor-
respond to the same object of interest, thereby having the potential
to improve the reliability of the localization. Data points are cat-
egorized into separate clusters building on mean-shift clustering
(Cheng 1995 ) by optimizing a parameter of window size. With
the specified window size, data points can be grouped into various
numbers of clusters in accordance with their locations and proxim-
ity to adjacent data points. Nonetheless, a cluster may still involve
irrelevant data points with respect to the distant object of that cluster.
For example, although most data points in one cluster correspond to
a vulnerable tower crane, some of them may correspond to a leaning
utility pole nearby. In such cases, localization accuracy may be sig-
nificantly degraded because of an erroneous compass bearing of
irrelevant data points.
To prevent such undesirable cases, the second filtering stage,
object presence–based filtering, builds on a region-based fully con-
volutional network (R-FCN) ( Dai et al. 2016 ) for object detection
to filter out data sets involving image frames without a target object.
R-FCN is a kind of deep convolutional network to detect objects
in the form of bounding boxes in images, based on a feature
extraction network, a region proposal network, and a position-
sensitive region of interest (RoI) pooling layers. To robustly detect
objects of interest in images, the 50-layer residual network ( He
et al. 2016 ), which has been validated as one of the best convolu-
tional networks to extract hierarchical visual features, is leveraged
as a feature extractor in R-FCN. Based on extracted visual feature
maps, the following convolutional layers —a region proposal net-
work and a position-sensitive RoI pooling layers—identify whether
they contain a target object to localize. When an image does not
involve a target object, the geographic data and compass bearing
associated with that image frame are discarded in the process,
because it is likely to have wrong information with respect to an
object of interest.
Before geospatial localization, it is necessary to consider the
magnetic declination at different locations to adjust compass
bearings. The compass bearings are measured by referencing the
Earth’s magnetic north as indicated by a magnetic sensor; however,
because the north of geographic coordinate systems is based on true
north, the angle difference (magnetic declination) between mag-
netic north and true north causes a compass bearing error. To adjust
for such deviation, the International Geomagnetic Reference Field
Model ( National Centers for Environmental Information 2018
)
finds a degree of magnetic declination at a certain location.
Once the compass bearing is adjusted, a line equation is formu-
lated on each data point in the UTM coordinate system, connecting
a data collector ’s location and a corresponding target object. In
cities, it is likely to have multiple observations by citizens for a
single object of interest. In that case, hundreds or thousands of pos-
sible intersection points among multiple lines could exist due to
errors in data collection and inaccurate sensors. After converting
multiple line equations into a linear system of equations in a matrix
form, a unique solution for the location of a distant object (i.e., inter-
section of multiple lines) is derived, building on the Moore–Penrose
generalized inverse approach ( Serre 2010 ), which builds on a
pseudo-inverse matrix of a singular or nonsquare matrix.

Although the unique location of a distant object can be obtained
by this inverse approach with all data points in a cluster, errors of
geospatial localization may vary significantly depending on the ac-
curacy of collected data points. To determine the accurate location
of a distant object, the 2D distribution of possible locations of a
distant object is examined. To this end, the possible locations are
estimated by selecting n number of data points ( n is the same as or
less than the total number of data points), formulating k number of
line equations ( k is the same as or less than n), and estimating a
unique intersection point in each case. With possible combinations
of n and k, numerous locations of distant objects are estimated. The
final location of a distant object is determined through mean-shift
clustering on the estimated locations. A cluster with the largest
number of data points is selected, and its cluster center location
is regarded as the final location of a distant object.
3D Virtual City Modeling, Its Update Based on
Participatory Sensing –Driven Reality Information of
Physical Vulnerabilities, and Interactive Visualization
in a CAVE
Once the spatiotemporal information of potentially vulnerable
objects in the distance is identified in cities, we automatically
stream unstructured crowdsourced visual data –driven reality infor-
mation into a 3D city model with the increased visibility toward an
updated 3D virtual city model (Fig. 3).
A 3D virtual city model refers to a digital representation of
urban areas that includes 2D or 3D geometry of earth surfaces,
buildings, vegetation, and infrastructure, as well as related objects
belonging to cities ( Biljecki et al. 2015 ). In this paper, the 3D vir-
tual city model consists of 3D building models, a 2D geographic
information system (GIS) map, and critical infrastructure in the
form of a set of geometric elements such as points, lines, and poly-
gons in a virtual environment. The geometry of 3D buildings is
modeled in the extensible markup language (XML) schema to de-
scribe the surfaces, boundaries, and differentiated roof structures of
the buildings based on geometric meshes using vertices, edges, and
faces. The XML schema is made up of a set of media assets that are
individual entities of digital media, including synthetic and photo-
graphic bitmaps, 3D mesh models, shaders, animation data, and
video samples. In the XML schema, the level of detail (LoD)
(i.e., the degree of abstraction of the real world according to com-
putational and economical facets as well as users ’ needs) must be
considered when describing the details of the geometry of build-
ing models. In our case studies, we leveraged 11,457 geometric
3D building models to describe parts of Houston in a virtual
space, with the level of Detail 2 (LoD2) having detailed shapes
of buildings and differentiated roof structures for interactive visu-
alization in a virtual environment.
To map the 3D building models onto a 2D GIS map, the spheri-
cal coordinate in the XML schema is used to specify the location of
each 3D building model on a 2D GIS map by pairing with the
corresponding element ID in the XML schema. A 2D GIS map,
one of the components of a 3D virtual city model, is made up
of 2D aerial images that represent a flat surface to show the size
and shape of areas, locations of features, and distances between
multiple points in space with geospatial and semantic information.
In this research, the 2D GIS map, consisting of the geospatial data
of infrastructures such as highways and railways from the Open-
StreetMap (OpenStreetMap 2019), is overlaid with high-resolution
satellite imagery, which represents the semantic information for
detailed characterization of geometric objects in given regions.
OpenStreetMap is an open data repository to provide map images
and all underlying map data built by a community of contributors
who maintain and update data about roads, railways, buildings, and
so on. It includes not only boundary conditions to determine the
size of a 3D city model, but also geometric networks involving
the terrain, roads, and railways in the areas of interest to model
a series of interconnected infrastructures. In this paper, satellite
imagery from Microsoft Bing Maps is draped over the surface
of a 2D GIS map based on the spatial data of OpenStreetMap
and National Elevation Dataset (NED) ( United States Geological
Survey 2019), such as digital elevation and geographic information
of roads and railways. The area on which 3D building models were
mapped (parts of Downtown Houston and midtown, museum park,
and Texas medical center) was 26.2 km

We have discussed the information of geospatial locations of
vulnerable objects from participatory sensing; next, we focus on
the geospatial information of critical infrastructure (transportation
systems, electric power transmission, water supply, medical care
facilities, and emergency facilities). As a proof-of-concept, we
mapped critical infrastructure (e.g., bus routes, metro railway, sta-
tions, utility poles, schools, hospitals, and storm sewers) around
vulnerable objects recognized from participatory sensing in
Houston. To gather such critical infrastructure information, we
leveraged the GIS data from the City of Houston Geographic
Information System (COHGIS) that provides spatial and semantic
information in Houston. Here, the critical infrastructure is modeled
in the Keyhole Markup Language (KML) schema, which is based
on XML standard, for expressing geographic annotation and visu-
alization. The KML schema, using a tag-based structure with ele-
ments and attributes, typically displays geographic data by using
placemarks, ground overlays, paths, and polygons in web-based
2D or 3D platforms. The KML schema specifies a set of elements
and attributes (e.g., placemark, path, polygon, description, ground
overlay) with spherical coordinates that can be fed into a 2D GIS
map. In this paper, the geographic location of critical infrastructure
in the COHGIS is registered into a 2D GIS map in the form of
vector data (i.e., point, line string, or polygon) or a 3D mesh model.
In addition to the COHGIS, the spatial information of utility poles
was captured at street level from a birds-eye perspective in Google
Earth. Fig. 4 illustrates parts of the KML schema and shows an
example of mapping the geographic location data of critical infra-
structure and vulnerable objects on a 2D GIS map.
The geographic location of vulnerable objects is calculated from
unstructured crowdsourced visual data, and then the resulting
spatiotemporal information is used for a model update to bring
an additional context dimension into a 3D virtual city model.
As a proof-of-concept, we mapped vulnerable objects recognized
from participatory sensing in Houston (e.g., leaning utility poles,
broken fences, vulnerable high-rise cranes, potential wind-borne
debris such as temporary signs) to better explore spatial information
of vulnerable objects and understand the impact of their failures on
neighboring critical infrastructure. The outcomes of the geospatial
localization are a set of points representing the estimated geospatial
locations of vulnerable objects in 2D Cartesian coordinates within
the UTM zone. The 2D Cartesian coordinates (easting/northing in
meters) of the geospatial locations of physical vulnerability are con-
verted into the spherical coordinate (latitude/longitude in degrees) to
import an additional context dimension (in the form of vector data
or 3D mesh models) into a 3D virtual city model. After conversion of
the coordinate system, the set of geospatial localization results of
vulnerable objects in the KML schema is fed into a 3D virtual city
for a model update. In other words, we update a 3D virtual city
model based on a set of vulnerable objects recognized from unstruc-
tured crowdsourced visual data analytics. Fig. 5 illustrates an exam-
ple of streaming vulnerable objects as a set of points into a virtual
city model.
A CA VE is a dedicated system to create and navigate a virtual
world in which users can walk through and be immersed in 3D
models rather than just looking on a screen or a 2D drawing. In this
paper, case studies were conducted on visualizing and exploring the
updated digital twin city of Houston in the CA VE facility at Texas
A&M University. The system is composed of a 180° curved wall
of 36 monitors in a 9-by-4 configuration to make a large seamless
display and a computer with a quad-core 3.7 GHz CPU, 16 GB
RAM, and a 8 GB graphics processing unit (GPU). The digital twin
city of Houston was fed into the CA VE to integrate real-virtual
states of spatiotemporal information in urban environments, which
helps users interact with the spatial information of vulnerable ob-
jects and neighboring critical infrastructures. Here, the proposed
framework leverages the real-world data, including visual data col-
lected from smartphones of city dwellers through the server, and
spatial and semantic information of critical infrastructures in Hous-
ton from the open database (COHGIS). The KML schema contain-
ing (1) geospatial information of vulnerable objects obtained from
the proposed localization; and (2) geospatial and semantic informa-
tion of critical infrastructures provided by COHGIS, and the XML

Experimental Results and Discussion
For evaluation, case studies were conducted in Houston. Vulnerable
objects were randomly selected by data collectors. Before the
experiments, participants were instructed to be aware of the defi-
nition of vulnerable objects in this paper and the data collection
process (i.e., how to take photos involving target objects and record
the direction information using a dedicated smartphone application
while pointing the smartphone toward the objects). In our experi-
ments, as a proof-of-concept, 135 photos were collected from five
participants using smartphones (the data collection at scale is dis-
cussed in Conclusions), and the collected data involve 11 objects in
six categories ranging from structural defects (e.g., leaning poles)
to elements at risk (e.g., potential wind-borne debris). In our experi-
ments, the period during which visual data containing the objects
of interest were captured represents the temporal dimension. The
participant-to-object distances were calculated based on the loca-
tion where each photo was taken for each object and the ground-
truth location of the object. Measured compass bearings were
adjusted through the magnetic declination at each location based
on the International Geomagnetic Reference Field Model.

In our experiments, it was observed that target object #3, with
nine data collections at an average distance of 23.8 m, had an
average error of 11.47 m, the largest error. The main reason for
such a huge error is the type of target object for localization
(broken fences, in this case). When localizing such objects involv-
ing large surface areas, localization performance is often degraded
because of unreliable compass bearings: it is not trivial to deter-
mine a single point of interest for such objects (in this case, de-
termining a single point representing broken fences) involving
compass bearing measurements.
To address issues such as this, data collection should be im-
proved by instructing participants in more detail on exactly how
to point the smartphone at the object of interest. If the shape of
a target object is a point, it can be easy to obtain exact direction
information, because there is only one way to orient the smartphone
to record direction information. However, most target objects are
not in the form of a point, which may lead to collecting inconsistent
direction information for target objects, even though data collectors
look at the same target from the same position. Thus, for better
localization, participants need detailed instruction in recording con-
sistent compass bearings toward target objects.
In our case studies, geospatial localization using unstructured
visual data demonstrated the potential for localizing distant objects
reported by citizens ’ smartphones in urban areas. To the best of the
authors’ knowledge, it is hard to find tolerable error ranges regard-
ing the geographic location of distant objects that can be universally
applied. Rather, the proposed method was compared with prior
works on localizing distant objects using the sensors embedded
in smartphones. Prior works ( Chen et al. 2016 ; Qin et al. 2015 ;
Ouyang et al. 2013; Manweiler et al. 2012) have focused on similar
applications: finding the geographic location of distant objects us-
ing smartphones (e.g., develop a reporting system to tag objects in
the augmented reality (AR) system so that users can interact and
communicate with others in the virtual environment). Compared
with the performance of such prior studies [e.g., distance errors of
about 5–150 m when the distance to the object is about 28 –160 m
(Manweiler et al. 2012 ); distance errors of about 3 –19 m when the
distance to the object is about 20 –100 m (Qin et al. 2015 ); distance
errors of about 8 –35 m when the distance to the object is about
2–50 m ( Chen et al. 2016 ); distance errors of 9 –37 m in the same
experimental conditions ( Ouyang et al. 2013 )], the experimental
results of our case study indicate that the proposed framework pro-
duces a reasonable localization performance, recording average
distance errors of 4.49 m when the distances to the objects at
risk were 18.6–38.7 m. The main reason for the slight performance
improvement in localization may be that the mean-shift cluster-
ing on hundreds of estimated locations with errors removes outliers
and helps determine the reliable final location of each target
object.
The purpose of geospatial localization in this paper is to help site
inspectors to better identify vulnerable objects based on reported
locations in the disaster preparedness stage. Regarding the benefits
of the proposed framework, the results of the comparison with prior
works show that the proposed localization method has the potential
to reduce errors when finding the geographic location of distant
objects. In this context, in the authors ’ opinion, the localization er-
ror range seems to be acceptable for identifying vulnerable objects
(when considering human range of sight) and taking appropriate
actions (or establishing action plans) to prevent extreme weather
events from causing catastrophic damage to given regions. Despite
the significant potential of localizing vulnerable objects for risk-
informed decision making, it is noted that localizing distant objects
using only the sensors in smartphones, without dedicated ranging
sensors, is still challenging, which is an open research problem.
In the matter of transportation-related infrastructure that can
impact the daily lives of commuters, businesses, and visitors, as
well as the ability of products to move more quickly, cheaply, and
efficiently in urban environments, the city of Houston launched
the Houston 311 Service in August 2001, a web system to post
city emergency or nonemergency information recognized from
citizens’ participation such as phone calls, an online website, and
smartphone applications, to make effective risk-informed decision-
making. Here, city emergency information such as geospatial
locations of vulnerable objects that may have negative impacts on
city transportation systems are classified by municipal agents and
scripted in the web system. As a result, reporting of vulnerable ob-
jects associated with city transportation systems is shown in a 2D
map as a dot containing reporting date, type of complaint, address
information, status, and departments that are responsible for solv-
ing the issue. In the process of mapping emergency-related infor-
mation on a 2D map through the city of Houston 311 service,
governmental or municipal agents require accurate geographic lo-
cations of vulnerable objects; however, the geospatial information
recognized from citizens ’ reporting via phone call or online texting
is sometimes not enough to determine the location of vulnerable
objects, because most city dwellers rely on a street address to ex-
plain an issue location to agents. Furthermore, if city dwellers are at
a distance from vulnerable objects, it is not trivial to recognize the

objects from unstructured crowdsourced visual data, automatically
estimates the geospatial location of such vulnerable objects without
dedicated investigation, and maps them into a 3D virtual city
model. The proposed method can facilitate participatory sensing,
because city dwellers do not need to report the issue locations
explicitly; rather, they just take a photo of potentially vulnerable
objects in the distance. Thus, the proposed method has the potential
to enhance the localization of vulnerable objects compared with the
web-based reporting system via phone call or email.

management, the proposed method cannot be a single solution to
disaster risk management, because the geospatial information of
vulnerable objects would not be enough for effective risk-informed
decision-making. Thus, it is noted that the details about each issue
provided by citizens would complement the proposed framework.

Conclusion
This paper presents a new framework to build a digital twin city
with participatory sensing to better understand spatiotemporal
information of physical vulnerabilities for effective risk-informed
decision-making. The proposed approach consists of (1) geospatial
localization of vulnerable objects in the distance recognized from
participatory sensing; and (2) a model update based on unstructured
crowdsourced visual data analytics to better understand the spatio-
temporal information of physical vulnerabilities with respect to
neighboring critical infrastructure and interactive 3D visualization
in the CA VE. To test the framework, case studies were conducted
on Houston. The experimental results indicate that participatory
sensing–based spatial information of vulnerable objects enables
users to better understand the situational awareness of the extent
to which given urban areas are vulnerable to anticipated disaster
situations, as well as how such vulnerable objects likely affect neigh-
boring critical infrastructure if they fail during extreme weather
events.
Public opinion, such as risk perception of infrastructure regarding
disaster situations, is increasingly used for associated infrastructure
management (V alentin et al. 2018). For example, public awareness of
flood risk on infrastructure could be a valuable insight to assist de-
cision makers in establishing a city resilience strategy in the context
of infrastructure disaster management (Al-Amin et al. 2019 ). In ad-
dition, after extreme weather events such as hurricanes, information
from crowds regarding damaged infrastructure (e.g., geospatial infor-
mation) can be used to facilitate transportation-related infrastructure
management for disaster recovery ( Dashti et al. 2014 ; Zhang et al.
2019). In this sense, research has been done on how to efficiently
collect and analyze public opinion ( Jiang et al. 2016 ; Tsujikawa
et al. 2016 ; Zhang et al. 2019 ), and surveys have been most widely
used for obtaining information from crowds about infrastructure reli-
ability and risk awareness (Taeby and Zhang 2019; Tsujikawa et al.
2016). Despite the benefits of conventional questionnaires, there is a
benefit from collecting and analyzing infrastructure-related data from
crowds, as discussed in Kirilenko et al. ( 2015) and Kirilenko and
Stepchenkova (2014), which can enhance data-driven infrastructure
management. Here, the proposed digital twin city platform, building
on crowdsourced visual data from participatory sensing, can be used
as a support tool to collect and analyze infrastructure-related data

Although this paper shows the potential of the proposed method
for creating a digital twin city for effective risk-informed decision-
making, open research challenges still remain. (1) The geospatial
information of vulnerable objects from crowdsourced data would
not be enough to build a digital twin city for effective risk-informed
decision making: there is a need to better characterize as-is city
conditions, such as infrastructure defects or neighborhood disorder,
in a virtual environment to understand the extent to which given
urban areas are susceptible to anticipated extreme weather events.
Integrating various sources of information (e.g., textual/audio infor-
mation from city dwellers regarding vulnerable objects or events,
real-time data from existing IoT sensors) into the proposed digital
twin city and the VR modeling platform would be a great way to
reduce the gap between a real city and its digital replica, especially
in the context of physical vulnerabilities in given areas, which can
ultimately improve risk-informed decision making. (2) Participatory
sensing can be powerful, by leveraging city dwellers ’ smartphones
with mobility, and has significant potential to collect huge amounts
of information at high spatiotemporal resolution. Despite the pos-
sible benefits, the success completely relies on the willingness and
motivation of participants to conduct sensing tasks, and thus it is
important to consider how we can ensure that city dwellers are willing
to participate in data collection. One approach is to leverage crowd-
sourcing photos along with publicly available dataset such as Google
Street view, as introduced in Cheng et al. ( 2018); Najafizadeh and
Froehlich (2018); and Wang et al. ( 2018). (3) It is possible that the
locations of vulnerable objects change infrequently, and such tem-
poral variations also refer to the degree of changes in physical vul-
nerability associated with the vulnerable objects. In the proposed
approach, it is technically possible that the localization of a vulner-
able object can be dynamically updated based on additional visual
data containing the same object. In this case, only the latest version
of the localized vulnerability will be modeled and visualized in
the virtual environment, as the old one will be replaced with the
updated one. More case studies are needed to better evaluate the
updating ability of the proposed digital twin city approach by tak-
ing into account spatiotemporal fluctuations of local vulnerabilities.
Addressing these limitations and implementing future works are
currently being explored as part of ongoing research.
