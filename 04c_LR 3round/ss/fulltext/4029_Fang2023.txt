ABSTRACT  
Efforts to address unsafe behaviors of construction workers have predominantly focused on identifying causative 
factors, unraveling formation mechanisms, and devising pre-emptive control strategies. Nonetheless, these 
studies have inadequately examined the reasons behind workers ’ engagement in unsafe behavior from a 
behavioral psychology perspective, specifically the collision reaction between impulsivity and self-control in the 
decision-making process, as well as the lack of exploration of psychological processes and behavioral response 
mechanisms of workers under different management strategies on construction sites. This paper describes an 
explanatory closed-loop structured model to simulate the safety performance of workers ’ behavior under 
different intervention strategies, considering the behavioral decision process and interactions between workers 
and managerial environment. The hybrid simulation model for worker ’ s unsafe behavior consists of three parts: 
the control panel, the proactive management panel, and the reactive panel. The model provides valuable in -
formation for explaining the causation of workers ’ unsafe behaviors and finding efficient management strategies. 
Through simulation experiments, results show that proactive management has more advantages in maintaining 
workers ’ behavioral safety levels over a long period of time than reactive management. This research provides 
valuable information for policy analysis and decision-making for project managers.   
1. Introduction 
The concept of a ’ Digital Twin ’ encapsulates a symbiotic relationship 
between the tangible physical universe and its abstract digital counter -
part [1] . Within the sphere of construction activities, the very essence of 
a work site is distilled into core components, namely: the workforce, 
material resources, infrastructural facilities, equipment, and the 
encompassing landscape [2] . These constituents play a pivotal role not 
only in the physical world but also significantly contribute to creating 
digital models. The digital model is established by abstracting the 
characteristics and behaviors of these critical elements from the physical 
world and using computer technology for simulation [3] . This process 
typically includes the collection of actual data relating to these com -
ponents, worker productivity, equipment performance parameters, 
material physical properties, facility layout, and geographical features 
of the landscape, and then converting this data into digital representa -
tion through data analysis techniques [4] . This digital model serves as a 
powerful tool to elucidate and delve into the root causes behind real- 
world phenomena, thereby providing valuable insights to aid strategic 
decision-making for managing real-world scenarios [5] . 
Extensive research has already been undertaken within the con -
struction realm, concentrating on digital twins of various elements such 
as Building Information Modeling (BIM) for site facilities [6] , 
Geographic Information Systems (GIS) for landscapes [7] , and System 
Information Modeling (SIM) for electrical equipment [8] . However, it is 
essential to recognize that workers, who form the backbone of con -
struction activities and significantly influence the efficiency and safety 
of project execution, have been comparatively less studied in terms of 
their digital twin representation, particularly in the interaction mecha -
nisms between management strategies and worker behavior. Given their 
actions can potentially lead to accidents [9] , focusing research efforts on 
their digital representation is of paramount importance. According to 
Heinrich ’ s study, it was discovered that unsafe practices were the main 
culprits behind 88% of industrial accidents in a sample of nearly 75,000 
accidents [10] . Further supporting this notion, A study by Salminen and 
Tallberg [11] found that 84 – 94% of accidents resulting in fatal

These insights underscore the heightened importance of addressing 
worker safety in the construction sector. According to a report from the 
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics in 2019, construction-related fatalities 
accounted for over a fifth of the total 5,333 recorded workplace fatalities

attributable to falls, slips, and trips stood at 31.4 per 10,000 full-time

need to prioritize digital modeling research on workers ’ unsafe behav -

effective solutions through modeling. As shown in Fig. 1 , an Agent- 
Based model (ABM) of workers ’ unsafe behaviors is proposed to simu -
late and explain their behavioral responses and decision-making under 
varying work environment conditions in this paper. This modeling

the implementation of more stringent safety regulations or the 
enhancement of worker training programs. 
Many studies have focused on the causation of workers ’ unsafe be -
haviors and the corresponding effective countermeasures. However, 
most of the existing studies neglected the complex adaptive nature of 
construction workers ’ unsafe behaviors. In other words, previous studies 
analyzed the causations of workers ’ unsafe behaviors from a static and 
macro perspective. For example, Fang (2008) [14] and Dodoo (2019) 
[15] et al. investigated the factors driving construction workers to 
engage in unsafe behavior. Wang et al. (2022) [16] demonstrates a clear 
connection between construction accident prevention and unsafe 
behaviors. 
Although some scholars have begun to explore the causal mechanism 
of workers ’ unsafe behaviors based on the system dynamic methods 
[17] , few studies have investigated the dynamic and micro mechanisms 
at the root of workers ’ personal decisions to engage in unsafe behavior. 
In other words, the causation of workers ’ unsafe behaviors involves 
management effects, a psychological process and a decision-making 
mechanism. The existing research lacks a complete perspective to sort 
out the relationship between the above three aspects. 
Actually, the management of workers ’ unsafe behaviors and their 
subsequent reactions to the management measures can be regarded as a 
dynamic and feedback loop process. First, human behaviors can be 
viewed as a response to external stimuli [18] , and it is not static or 
without provocation. External factors, including the stimulation of the 
poor working environment and management measures, are usually the 
direct causes of unsafe behaviors of workers. Second, given external 
stimulation, human behavior has a complex psychological process, i.e., 
weighing the pros and cons and consequences of different behavior 
choices [19] . The final behavior choice is determined under the psy -
chological fighting of different motives. Third, by observing the 
behavior of workers on the production site, site managers can analyze 
the management performance of different management policies. 
Furthermore, management staff continually adapt their management 
strategies to maintain the site ’ s safety performance within a reasonable 
range [20] . 
Based on the above assumptions, this study presents a closed-loop 
structured model to understand and explain workers ’ unsafe behav -
iors, as shown in the digital model in Fig. 1 . In this model, emphasis will 
be placed on a dynamic feedback process: Site management staff observe 
the behavioral safety performance of the entire site and carry out sta -
tistical analyses. If the management personnel find that the behavior 
safety performance of the current construction site is too poor, they will 
put forward strict management disciplines to avoid safety accidents. As 
part of rigorous management measures, unsafe worker behaviors will be 
reduced due to high pressures, judge and weigh. As a result, the safety 
performance of the construction site will be improved and maintained 
for a period of time. To gain more management knowledge about this 
process, hybrid simulation is an effective approach to obtaining insights. 
Workers ’ unsafe behavior is an interdisciplinary field that encom -
passes various aspects of civil engineering construction, including spe -
cific management measures and the working environment [21] . 
However, its foundation can be traced back to the realm of behavioral 
psychology [22] . Previous research has inadequately addressed the 
reasons behind workers ’ engagement in unsafe behavior from a behav -
ioral psychology perspective, particularly the collision reaction between 
impulsivity and self-control in the psychological decision-making pro -
cess that leads to such behavior [23] . In addition, there is a lack of 
exploration on the psychological processes and behavioral response 
mechanisms of workers under different management strategies on con -
struction sites. To fill this research gap, our study integrates various 
behavioral psychology theories and employs an agent-based composite 
simulation model, which enhances the interpretability of results and 
facilitates the exploration of multiple scenarios. By integrating simula -
tion with real-world data, we provide valuable insights into the factors 
influencing unsafe behaviors among construction workers. The findings 
underscore the significance of proactive safety management and offer 
practical implications for improving safety performance and developing 
effective safety management strategies. 
This paper builds upon existing ABM research on worker behavior 
and further incorporates classic theories of behavioral psychology into 
the worker psychological decision-making process, enhancing the 
theoretical foundation of relevant research. Additionally, the computa -
tional experimental results of this paper explain the phenomenon of 
differences in behavior safety performance under different management 
strategies, providing project managers with a highly interpretable sce -
nario simulation tool that is conducive to the development of safety 
management strategies.

2. Related research in construction unsafe behavior 
management 
2.1. Behavior-Based safety (BBS) theory 
Behavior-Based Safety (BBS) [24] emphasizes the procedure from 
behavior observation to safety management. The BBS is a process of 
educating management and employees on the overall safety of the work 
place through behavior observation [25]. To improve workers’ behav -
ioral safety performance, BBS is a common method that attracts great 
attention. This is a management process, where supervisors were 
responsible for monitoring worker behavior, providing feedback, and 
implementing positive or negative interventions. 
The procedure of BBS consists of four parts [25], i.e., objective 
identification (what should workers not do?), behavior observation 
(what are workers doing?), causation analysis (why workers do it?), and 
safety communication (intervention strategy to improve what workers 
do) [26]. During the implementation of the BBS, a checklist of unsafe 
behaviors to be observed in the field should be established first. Em -
ployees who have received on-site safety review training are selected as 
observers. They will review and supervise other employees, including 
paying attention to their safety or unsafe behaviors and making records. 
Based on the observation records, the Antecedent-Behavior- 
Consequence (ABC) model is generally used for causation analysis 
[27]. This process involves tracing and inferring observed behaviors, 
attempting to re-establish the causes of unsafe behaviors, and specu -
lating on their potential outcomes, as a basis for safety education. Given 
the observational and analytical data, safety communication and inter -
vention strategies will be implemented, including the positive and 
negative reinforcement stimulus. 
The BBS management, as an important pre-control means of safety 
management, was largely concerned and discussed in scientific research 
and engineering practice. One of the most important issues facing BBS 
management is that once intervention actions are removed, the effect of 
improved behaviors is difficult to sustain [28]. 
2.2. Influencing factors 
The BBS pays greater attention to behavioral observation, and many 
other studies explore the relationship between safety management and 
unsafe behaviors. 
Clarke et al. (2000) [29] clarifies the term safety culture (or named 
safety climate), and points out that organizational safety culture has an 
indirect but far-reaching impact on workers’ unsafe behaviors. The 
development of safety culture on the construction site includes three 
levels from shallow to deep, including the norms and artefacts (surface 
level), the beliefs and values (intermediate level), and the core as -
sumptions (deepest level, i.e., understanding that safety is the overriding 
priority). Choudhry et al. (2006) [30] discussed how safety culture 
improves workers’ behaviors through increasing their safety knowledge, 
motivation, compliance, and participation. A good safety culture posi -
tively impacts safety managers (senior managers, line managers, and 
supervisors) and frontline production staff. 
In discussing the relationships between the unsafe behavior and 
safety management, some researchers have developed a causation 
model to explain the impacts of safety management on workers’ unsafe 
behaviors. Jiang et al. (2015) [17] presents cause-effect relationships 
between management conditions on safety and workers’ individual 
conditions, in which workers’ safety awareness, safety knowledge, 
safety attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control are 
considered. Guo et al. (2018) [31]presents a behavior-based safety 
program to simulate the impacts of different management measures, i.e., 
rewards and punishments, on workers’ unsafe behaviors. Yu el al. 
(2019) [32] proposed a system dynamic model for intervening workers’ 
unsafe behaviors, in which the impact of individual factors, the physical 
environment, safety leadership, group factors and safety management 
on the development of unsafe worker behaviors are considered. 
The above research considered the inhibition or promotion of 
various management measures and related factors on unsafe behaviors. 
However, little theoretical or methodological support in behavioral 
psychology is taken into account in these researches, and more details of 
mental activities from an individual level is needed. 
2.3. Formation mechanism 
Regarding to people’s psychological process and their consequential 
behaviors, it has a long history in the theories and research, which is 
called behavioral psychology [33]. Some studies have been conducted to 
model people’s behaviors using simulation-based methods, in which the 
conceptual decision-making process is developed [32,34]. 
In the domain of behavioral psychology, behavior can be considered 
as an individual activity adapting to the environment, and its formation 
has reasons and motivations. Kurt Lewin (1890–1947), usually called 
the father of social psychology, established the Lewinian Field Theory, 
which stressed that behavior is a function of the person and the envi -
ronment [35]. External environment factors, including management 
policies, working environment, physiological feelings, etc., are the 
direct drivers of people’s behaviors. But environmental stimuli are not 
the only factors shaping behavior. Even under the same environmental 
stimulus, different people will have their own behavioral choices as a 
result of their personalities. 
On the basis of Lewinian Field Theory, Wilson et al. (2000) [36] 
proposed a dual attitude model to explain the formation of people’s 
social behaviors. The dual attitude theory assumes that people can have 
two completely different attitudes at the time of making a certain 
behavioral decision: one is the automatic and impulsive attitude, and the 
other is the conscious and reflective attitude. 
2.4. Agent-based modeling 
The main components of an ABM include agents, the environment in 
which agents operate, and the interactions between agents and their 
environment as well as with each other [37]. It is also necessary to 
define rules that govern the communication, behavior, and decision- 
making capabilities of agents. The heterogeneous nature of ABM al -
lows for differentiation among individual agents and the generation of 
various scenarios [38]. ABM are an efficient solution for dealing with 
multi-option problems [39]. 
The advantages of ABM have led to their application in construction 
safety management. Some studies focus on a macroscopic level, where 
safety interactions between companies, employees, the public, and the 
government in small enterprises are explored [40]. Agents, such as 
contractors, projects, insurance companies, governments, and the 
environment, are considered to simulate interactions between stake -
holders at the market level with regards to safety [41]. In order to study 
safety performance, interactions between a worksite, individual con -
struction workers, and different safety investments were mimicked [42]. 
The safety behavior of various contractors involved in a project and its 
impact on project performance were modeled [43]. 
ABM have also been used to study interactions among organizational 
factors, supervisors, and workers at the project level. Newaz et al. (2020) 
[44] examined how supervisors’ behavior impacts the safety behavior of 
workers using the concept of psychological contract. The social conta -
gion effect of safety violations within construction crews was explored, 
considering cognitive, social, and organizational aspects [45]. A cogni -
tive analysis model was developed to simulate worker behaviors and on- 
site interactions between construction workers and management teams 
[46]. ABM were employed to investigate how the socio-cognitive pro -
cess and safety management interventions interact and influence 
workers’ safety behaviors under different site risk conditions [47]. 
Raoufi et al. (2018) [48] examined the influence of crew motivational 
factors, crew situational factors, and project situational factors on

Summary: 
Existing research primarily focuses on analyzing the factors that in -
fluence worker unsafe behaviors (causes), understanding the mecha -
nisms underlying the formation of unsafe behaviors (formation process), 
and implementing pre-control methods for unsafe behaviors using 
intelligent tools (such as intelligent identification tools and simulation 
tools based on ABM). However, there are still opportunities for 
improvement in understanding workers ’ behavioral decision-making 
mechanisms and analyzing management strategies. 
On one hand, current research heavily relies on risk perception 
theory to model the formation process of worker unsafe behaviors. This 
theory assumes that workers are rational decision-makers who always 
choose the most reasonable course of action based on risk assessment. 
However, in reality, people ’ s behavior can be influenced by irrational 
factors and impulsive actions due to factors like poor emotions, high 
schedule pressure, and physical conditions [51] . 
On the other hand, the behavioral safety performance of construction 
site workers is the result of the combination of top-level management 
strategy implementation and bottom-level worker behavior decision- 
making and response. Different management strategies can trigger 
different psychological processes and coping response styles in workers. 
For example, some workers may engage in safe behavior voluntarily due 
to a high level of safety attitude driven by a well-established proactive 
control mechanism, while others may engage passively in safe behavior 
due to strict reward and punishment measures [52] . 
The background presented above introduces two main objectives of 
this research. Firstly, to deepen the understanding of the formation 
mechanisms behind worker unsafe behaviors by integrating the theory 
of dual attitudes derived from behavioral psychology. This integration 
will provide interdisciplinary theoretical support rooted in psychology 
for research in related fields. Secondly, to address the sustainability is -
sues of on-site unsafe behavior management effectiveness, specifically 
focusing on the observed rebound in the incidence of unsafe behaviors 
after stopping intervention [53] . 
3. Model development 
3.1. Modeling methods: Hybrid simulation modeling 
The general framework for simulating construction workers ’ unsafe 
behaviors is depicted in Fig. 2 (a). This framework comprises the com -
plex internal activities (internal scope) of individuals and the in -
teractions between individuals and the environment (external scope). To 
accomplish this, the adopted approach in this study utilizes the hybrid 
simulation modeling technique, which relies on the ABM framework 
integrating both system dynamics and discrete event simulation. The 
reasons for selecting these methods are as follows: they are widely uti -
lized modeling and simulation techniques for studying and analyzing the 
dynamic processes of complex systems, and they offer quantitative 
analysis of system behavior and performance. Nevertheless, each 
method possesses its distinctive characteristics and applicability in 
specific scenarios. 
ABM [54] is a modeling technique well-suited for individual repre -
sentation. Every agent is endowed with unique attributes, state, and 
behavior rules, with their state and behavior being influenced by the 
environment [55] . In the present study, by simulating the behavior of a 
considerable number of agents, ABM enables the examination of the 
emergence and evolution of overall system behavior, as well as 
comprehension of the ramifications of individual decisions on system 
performance. System dynamics [56] concentrates on the evolutionary 
behavior of the entire system and captures changes in system state using 
continuous-time dynamic equations. It underscores the interactions and 
feedback mechanisms among different components of the system, 
rendering it suitable for modeling “ causal relationships ” . In this study, 
individual behavior is regarded as the outcome, whereas the in -
dividual ’ s physiological and psychological environment, along with 
safety attributes, are considered as influencing factors. Modeling this 
intricate causal pathway and influence mechanism from influencing 
factors to the result aligns with the applicability and suitability of the 
system dynamics method. Discrete event simulation [57] deals with 
systems where the state changes occur at specific time points triggered 
by “ events ” . In our study, the management measures implemented on the 
construction site adapt automatically based on the behavioral safety 
performance. For instance, if a safety accident occurs, stricter measures 
are enforced. Therefore, modeling the impact effects of these manage -
ment measures aligns with the applicability of discrete event simulation. 
In summary, ABM is useful for developing the framework to simulate 
the interactions between individuals and the overall environment,

3.2. Relevant models for unsafe behaviors management 
3.2.1. Proactive management modeling 
Framework of the discrete choice model. 
The Proactive Safety Management Strategy ensures the psychologi -
cal construction of reflective cognition of workers in terms of behavioral 
safety on a regular basis. Multiple intervention measures can be imple -
mented to awaken and improve the reflective attitude of workers to -
wards behavioral safety, including safety training, safety education, etc. 
Under the proactive strategy of safety management, workers ’ behavioral 
decision is mainly developed based on the dual attitude process. 
As shown in Fig. 3 , according to the dual attitude theory, the unsafe 
behavior of construction workers is actually a choice behaviour [58] , 
consisting of a mental process of judging the merits of multiple options 
and deciding whether to choose one for action. More specifically, the 
choice that workers face is that of complying with the impulse to make 
an unsafe behavior or obeying reflective thinking to make a safe 
behaviour [59] . In the context of choice behavior, workers ’ decision of 
unsafe behavior is simulated using the discrete choice model [60] . 
Discrete choice model is developed based on the random utility theory, 
which assumes that when presented with a discrete set of options, 
people will choose the option with the greatest benefit or utility.

ory, the probability ratio of a worker conforming unsafe behaviors to

the safety performance of on-site workers. No intervention will be

Given the unsafe behaviors of workers, its following consequences 
should be analyzed using the Heinrich ’ s safety triangle theory [67] . The 
safety triangle model assumes a stable ratio of unsafe behaviors, near 
misses, minor injuries, and major injuries in the workplace. In other 
words, depending on the safety conditions on the site, the unsafe 
behavior of the workers has some probability and will still evolve into a 
major safety accident. To elucidate this causation process, the most 
referenced model, Heinrich ’ s Domino Theory [68] , is employed in our 
study. This theory outlines the progression from unsafe worker behavior 
to the occurrence of safety accidents, much like dominoes falling in a 
sequence. It identifies key factors including unsafe acts, hazardous 
working conditions, accidents, and resultant injuries. Integrating both 
Heinrich ’ s Safety Triangle Theory and Domino Theory as our founda -
tional tenets, as depicted in Fig. 5 , this paper posits that safety accidents 
result from a blend of risky worker actions and perilous environmental 
conditions. If a worker is in spatial – temporal proximity to an accident, 
they may get injured, with a higher likelihood of minor injuries and a 
lower probability of major ones. These probability parameters can be 
estimated using expert knowledge.

calculated, which measures the internal consistency between the survey

and the results from the survey are valid. As illustrated in the right 
portion of Fig. 6 , the reliability of our data is further bolstered by the 
diverse range of respondents spanning various occupations, educational 
levels, and project types. 
The test – retest reliability correlation coefficient of 0.0225 suggests low 
stability between two respondents, with significant individual differ -
ences in workers ’ decision-making regarding behavioral safety, as evi -

and an average standard deviation of 2.17 across all question ratings 
imply a high level of variability in the responses of workers. 
From the above two statistical indicators, it can be observed that 
although workers have coherent decision-making patterns and mecha -
nisms when it comes to unsafe behavior, the differences in decision-

5. Simulation experiment 
5.1. Simulation process 
This study used the hybrid simulation model to explore the mecha -
nism by which unsafe worker behavior is formed as a result of envi -
ronmental factors, management measures and psychological processes. 
The simulation experiment in this study attempts to probe into the 
following problems:  
I) In the context of Dual Attitude theory, how does a worker deal with 
the conflicts between his unsafe impulses and professional ethics? 
Simulation Step: 
1.1) Develop a hybrid simulation model for a worker ’ s unsafe 
behavior based on the theory in Section 3.2.1 and 3.2.2 . 
1.2) Test the effects of environmental perturbations on a worker ’ s 
unsafe behavior rate. 
1.3) Test the effects of management frequency on a worker ’ s unsafe 
behavior rate.  
II) In the context of Reinforcement Learning theory, how does a 
worker behave under a high pressure of safety management? 
Simulation Step: 
2.1) Develop a hybrid simulation model for a worker ’ s unsafe 
behavior based on the theory in Section 3.2.3. 
2.2) Test the effects of punishment strictness on a worker ’ s unsafe 
behavior rate. 
2.3) Test the effects of discount factor on a worker ’ s unsafe behavior 
rate.  
III) Comparing proactive management with reactive management, 
what are their features and how are they different?

5.2. Model initialization 
The hybrid simulation model for worker ’ s unsafe behavior consists of 
three parts, i.e., the control panel (from the Project Manager (PM) ’ s 
point of view), the proactive management panel (from a worker ’ s point 
of view) and the reactive panel (from a worker ’ s point of view). 
I) The control panel. As shown in Fig. 8 , the control panel simulates 
the PM ’ s management role on the construction site, including policy 
release, on-site monitoring and the permissibility of penalty and possi -
bility of discharge. 
In the administrator module, various management policies and 
measures can be formulated freely. In the context of proactive man -
agement, how often pre-control and preventive measures are carried out 
for workers can be determined using the slider. The number at the 
bottom left indicates the minimal value of the slider, the number at the 
bottom right indicates the maximum value, and the number at the 
bottom center shows the current value selected on the slider. In the 
context of reactive management, the punishment strictness and the 
discount factor can be adjusted. Also, the frequency of occurrence of 
environmental perturbations, the main causes for unsafe-behavior im -
pulse, can be customized by the software users. The same applies to the 
site environmental parameters, including the environment hazard level 
and the exposure probability, which are important parameters greatly 
affects the number of accidents given a certain number of unsafe 
behaviors. 
In the statistics module, the safety performance of all workers is 
aggregated into several indicators, which represents the overall safety 
management state of the construction site, including the number of 
unsafe behaviors, the number of near miss incidents, the number of 
minor injuries, and the number of major injuries. All these indicators are 
critical in formulating reactive management measures for PM, e.g., 
penalty and possibility of discharge intensity. 
In the punishment module, the intensity of the punishment for a 
worker ’ s detected unsafe behavior or safety accident is determined 
based on the recent safety performance of the construction site. If the 
safety performance of the overall construction site is very poor in recent 
days, severe punitive measures will be taken to create a pressing stance 
against violations, with the aim of preventing workers from behaving in 
an unsafe manner. Conversely, minor punitive measures will be taken if 
the safety performance of the construction site is not so bad. 
The specific modeling steps of the statistics module and the pun -
ishment module have been described in Section 3.2.3.

from a dual attitude perspective. The panel consist of three parts,

Generation.

6. Discussion 
6.1. The rationale behind method selection 
Based on the actual investigation and data statistics in Section 4 , we 
discovered that unsafe behavior among workers results from a combi -
nation of physiological, psychological, and managerial factors. While 
workers exhibit consistent behavioral tendencies in similar situations, 
there is still a high degree of uncertainty and randomness, making it 
difficult to establish a clear, predictable, repeatable, and stable causal 
relationship between input (scenarios) and output (behaviors). 
After comparing the results from Sections 4 and 5 , we found that the 
empirical study findings align with the assumptions and compositions of 
our proposed model, making it an effective tool to explain real-world 
phenomena. For example, Fig. 12 simulations reveal significant varia -
tions in workers ’ safety performance, despite being under the same 
management strategy. While the number of unsafe behaviors decreases, 
the extent, speed, and fluctuation differ significantly. This individual 
variation in safety behavior is consistent with the results presented in 
Fig. 6 , as observed in the survey questionnaire analysis. Furthermore, 
the simulations in Figs. 14 and 15 show that different management 
strategies have varying effects. Proactive management strategies, such 
as safety meetings and training, are progressively improving worker 
safety awareness and skills. This approach is slower, but it usually has a 
more sustainable impact, as workers are more receptive to it. In contrast, 
reactive management through strict rewards and penalties yields im -
mediate results, but has a relatively short-term impact. These findings 
align with the tendencies observed in the answers to questions 16 – 19 
and 24 – 27 in Fig. 6 . Given the consistency between the simulation 
experiment findings and the survey questionnaire statistics, we can 
conclude that our model is not only capable of reflecting certain patterns 
and characteristics of dangerous behaviors in the real world, but also has 
the ability to simulate the evolutionary process of scenarios related to 
worker behavior. 
Based on the results of the above experiments and literature review, 
we further discuss the rationale for using simulation methods to study 
unsafe behavior among workers. Currently, research on unsafe behavior 
among workers can be divided into two categories, i.e., data-driven 
causal analysis methods and ABM methods. Despite the limitations of 
simulation methods in reliability and predictive capability, it has ben -
efits in interpretability, flexibility, and close integration with theories of 
behavioral psychology. The following reasons explain why this paper 
uses multi-agent simulation methods to investigate unsafe behaviors in 
workers: 
)I. ABM is a mainstream research method for human behavior. Un -
safe behavior among workers is also a kind of human behavior, 
and ABM is currently one of the mainstream methods for studying

human behavior, widely used in emergency evacuation behaviors

fields, unsafe behavior among workers is also a type of ‘human 
behavior ’ and should not be an exception.  
)II. The special nature of human behavior. Human behavior is a 
combination of rational and irrational factors, with certain rules 
but also strong randomness and uncertainty (generally not having 
a clear, predictable, repeatable, and stable input – output causal 
relationship). Unlike other physical phenomena with stable 
‘input-output ’ mapping relationships, human behavior as an 
‘output result ’ also depends on the external ‘input stimulus ’ it 
receives (according to Lewin ’ s field dynamics theory), but this 
mapping relationship is often not entirely stable and can easily 
lead to differentiated behavior in the same situation due to 
various irrational or unexpected factors. The ABM method is a 
reliable way to simulate this uncertain and random behavior 
through repeated Monte Carlo experiments.  
)III. Workers ’ opportunistic behavior can hinder the collection of 
authentic psychological data. When workers are aware that they 
are being observed or monitored, they may consciously pay 
attention to the safety of their behavior, even if they do not 
genuinely want to behave safely. Once they are no longer being 
observed or monitored, they may revert to unsafe behavior due to 
a sense of complacency or convenience. Therefore, workers ’ 
opportunistic behavior under observation is not conducive to 
collecting data on their true psychological reactions. This study 
had to resort to anonymous questionnaires to investigate 
workers ’ true psychological activities and used simulation 
methods to replicate this process. 
6.2. Practical use 
The engineering significance of this study is that it emphasizes the 
importance of implementing proactive safety management measures in 
order to improve workers ’ safety awareness and reduce accidents on 
construction sites. The proposed visualization model can be instru -
mental in developing effective safety management strategies by simu -
lating different scenarios and their potential outcomes. Ultimately, this 
approach can result in safer working environments for construction 
workers and a decrease in the number of accidents, injuries, and fatal -
ities on construction sites. Fig. 16 depicts the step-by-step procedure for 
the practical implementation of the proposed model, which are shown 
below. 
Step 1 involves the preliminary formulation of management strate -
gies for ensuring the safety of construction workers, such as choosing 
between proactive or reactive control strategies, identifying specific 
measures for proactive control, and determining the frequency of con -
trol measures. 
Step 2 involves collecting basic information about construction 
workers on-site and obtaining data on their decision-making tendencies 
and behavioral patterns under different environments and management 
strategies through the use of questionnaires. 
Step 3 involves building a simulation model that can simulate 
changes in safety control on construction sites under given management 
strategies. The simulation includes tracking the number of unsafe be -
haviors as well as the frequency of near-miss incidents, minor accidents, 
and major accidents that result from these behaviors. 
Based on the obtained simulation model, it can support the following 
two purposes which are also the research outcomes of this study: 
I) Visualized Safety Management Training . Step 4 focuses on the need 
for proactive management measures to address the lack of workers ’ 
comprehension of safety behavior significance when they are compelled 
to act safely under monitoring pressure. This lack of understanding 
hinders the enhancement of overall safety awareness. Unfortunately, 
many construction projects prioritize safety only during accidents or 
special inspections, neglecting proactive management measures. To 
combat this, a visualization model capable of simulating the dynamics of 
behavior safety performance, and the resulting accidents under different 
control strategies is necessary. The outcomes of the simulation can be 
presented to decision-makers, allowing them to comprehend the 
importance of proactive safety management strategies and develop 
effective safety management approaches. 
II) Enhance Existing Behavior Safety Management Strategies. Step 5 
involves conducting a preliminary assessment of the management 
effectiveness of the pre-determined strategies on the current population 
of workers. In this step, the degree of external interference can be 
controlled, including physiological challenges arising from the work 
environment and unfavorable psychological states of individual 
workers. This allows for the observation and simulation of worker 
behavior safety performance under various possible working conditions. 
Step 6 allows management decision-makers to adjust and enhance 
existing management strategies based on the simulated safety perfor -
mance results obtained under the current management strategy. 
Importantly, the dynamic fluctuation characteristics (which have mostly 
been verified in the analysis of the survey questionnaire results in 
Chapter 4) can illustrate qualitative trends and regularities to some 
extent, providing reference value for the real physical world. 
6.3. Contribution 
This paper presents an agent-based composite simulation model to 
construct and manage the simulation of unsafe behavior among con -
struction workers. The model simulates how unsafe behavior is gener -
ated and managed at both the micro level of individual worker behavior 
decision-making and the macro level of management strategy develop -
ment, as well as how management strategies affect worker behavior. 
The contributions of this study lie in the following aspects. 
I) Theoretical supports: It is supported by several behavioral psy -
chology theories, such as the dual attitude theory, reinforcement 
learning theory, and discrete choice theory. These classic theories make 
the proposed method highly interpretable and non-black-box. Human 
behavior is not always rational, as evidenced by drug addicts who are 
aware of the harm caused by drug abuse but still engage in it, which is no 
exception for workers ’ unsafe behaviors. But existing research has 
largely ignored the relationship between the conflicts of primal impulses 
and rational inhibition, which has been presented in classic theory of 
behavioral psychology [74] . In addition, the use of reward and pun -
ishment measures as an efficient means of modifying human behavior 
has seldom been modeled or explored in previous studies of workers ’ 
unsafe behavior. However, it is clear that there are significant differ -
ences between the Skinner ’ s behavioral reinforcement theory [75] and 
the dual attitude theory [36] in determining human behavior, and they 
should be separately considered and modeled. This study establishes 
simulation models for both proactive and reactive safety management

II) Combination of simulation and real-world data: To ensure authen -

workers, it may be beneficial to use anonymous questionnaires in order

Based on this, simulation methods can serve as an important interface

intelligent agents. On the other hand, behavioral psychology theories

into account the authenticity and reliability of the data, as well as the 
interpretability and exportability of the model. It crosses different levels 
of modeling, including both the inner activities of individual workers 
and the management strategies of worker groups. This allows re -
searchers to explore different hypotheses and scenarios and observe 
their impacts intuitively. Because simulation methods allow researchers 
to experiment with multiple experiments and conditions, they can draw 
more accurate and reliable conclusions. 
III) Valuable findings in line with real-world situations: Valuable and 
realistic findings can be obtained through simulation methods to explain 
the underlying mechanisms of unsafe behaviors exhibited by construc -
tion workers in real-world settings. At the micro-level, our model re -
flects the randomness and uncertainty of individual behavior decisions 
through discrete behavioral choices, which is consistent with the results 
obtained from real-world questionnaires. At the macro-level, workers’ 
unsafe behaviors exhibit characteristics of a complex system that is self- 
adaptive and self-organizing, which is consistent with the dynamic 
adjustment features of actual engineering project management methods. 
This self-adaptive and self-organizing mechanism displays time- 
sensitive differences between proactive and reactive strategies 
regarding their impact on construction workers’ safety performance 
during intervention and withdrawal. The research findings emphasize 
the critical role of proactive safety management in construction sites, 
providing decision-makers with valuable insights into effective safety 
management plans. 
7. Conclusion 
Ensuring workers’ behavioral safety is a persistent challenge for on- 
site managers. Managers utilize a range of strategies, such as proactive 
interventions and reactive management. Proactive interventions, 
grounded in the dual attitude theory, gradually foster workers’ aware -
ness of safety. Once established, this awareness facilitates the mainte -
nance of behavioral safety regardless of external influences. Driven by 
reinforcement theory, reactive management aims to attain immediate 
rewards. In urgent situations, such as accident-prone construction sites, 
implementing stringent measures is necessary to prevent a decline in 
safety performance. However, the removal of these measures may cause 
workers to become lax, resulting in an increase in unsafe behaviors and 
accidents. 
This paper described a hybrid simulation method to explore the 
formation mechanisms for unsafe worker behaviors as well as the effects 
of the management strategy. Managing and controlling unsafe worker 
behaviors is a dynamic, closed-loop and feedback process, which in -
cludes both individual subject activities at the micro level and perfor -
mance emergence at the macro level. Benefitting from the hybrid 
simulation method, the process of behavioral reflection and decision- 
making can be modelled and analyzed in a quantified manner. Results 
from the simulation model provide valuable information for policy 
analysis and decision-making for project managers. The information 
provided by the model help them anticipate and understand how 
workers respond in thought and action as part of their management 
policies. 
Due to various constraints, such as respondent willingness and po -
tential pandemic restrictions, the data collected from the survey ques -
tionnaire phase of our study was limited. However, we implemented 
measures to ensure data reliability and believe our findings are still 
representative. For future research, it is recommended to address the 
limitation by increasing the sample size to strengthen the generaliz -
ability of the findings. Additionally, incorporating advanced equipment 
such as EEG (electroencephalography) and eye-tracking devices can be 
considered to collect psychophysiological information related to 
workers’ unsafe behaviors. This would facilitate a deeper exploration 
and confirmation of the factors influencing unsafe behaviors, the for -
mation mechanisms, and the interactive relationships among workers 
and between workers and management. Moreover, it is important to 
build upon the current focus on individual internal psychological pro -
cesses and the response relationship between individuals and policies by 
further considering the interaction among agents in the future. These 
advancements would provide a more comprehensive understanding of 
the topic and contribute to the field of behavioral safety management.
