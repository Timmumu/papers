Digital Twins for Construction Sites: Concepts,
LoD Definition, and Applications
Jiaying Zhang 1; Jack C. P. Cheng, A.M.ASCE 2; Weiwei Chen 3; and Keyu Chen 4
Abstract: The lack of integration between the digital and physical world results in a lower level of efficiency and collaboration in the
construction industry. Digital twin technology, which creates a visual and digital model of a corresponding physical object for simulating,
monitoring, analyzing, and other actions throughout the whole life cycle, is considered as an effective solution to address these problems. This
research proposes a framework to utilize digital twins and extend the existing level of details (LoDs) of building information modeling (BIM)
for construction site management. This study analyzes and improves the operation principle and mechanism of digital twins, including the
digital representation based on BIM, Internet of Things (IoT), data storage, integration, and analytics, as well as interaction with the physical
environment. Questionnaires and interview results verify that the proposed framework can support construction site monitoring and man-
agement, enhance quality and efficiency, and improve construction safety. It also acknowledges the contribution of LoDs ’ extension to
construction site management. The interviews underline the main challenges that BIM, IoT, and data processes face in practical applications.

Introduction
In the construction industry, the lack of integration between the dig-
ital world and physical world, especially the interaction between
data analysis and the corresponding action, has led to information
fragments as well as data duplication and stagnation between differ-
ent stages in the construction life cycle. It lowers the efficiency
of management and hinders collaboration between staff members,
diverse professions, and distinct construction phases. In particular,
the situations on a construction site are dynamic with constant
changes in facilities, equipment, materials, people, site layout, and
various other components at different phases, which increases the
requirement for coordination and collaboration ( Kumar and Cheng
2015). Consequently, these increase the complexity of monitoring
and management of construction sites.
Construction informatization has become the future direction of
development, whereby data-driven and dynamic modeling methods
are employed to address some concerns in the field of engineering
management in recent years, such as worker-power manage-
ment ( Kim et al. 2020 ), safety monitoring ( Su et al. 2021 ), and
decision-making support ( Abotaleb and El-adaway 2018 )a sw e l l
as workflow administration ( Ma et al. 2018 ). The digital twin has
the potential capability of providing the effective solutions to ad-
dress these problems ( Lu et al. 2020c ).
Digital twin technology is defined as creating a visual and
digital model of a physical object ( Kaur et al. 2020 ). There is a
profound interaction between the physical and digital components
through the continual and dynamic data exchange between the
physical and digital environments. It provides real-time monitoring,
updating, simulating, analyzing, controlling, predicting, and opti-
mizing (Kaur et al. 2020; Schleich et al. 2017), as well as validating
and seamlessly coordinating throughout the life cycle. As a
result, information silos and data duplication can be effectively
avoided. By using this approach, t he construction site situation
can be monitored and managed alongside the whole process. The
digital twin basic architecture contains (1) the digital representa-
tion based on building information modeling (BIM) technology,
(2) Internet of Things (IoT) containing sensor technology, (3) data
storage, integration and analytics, and (4) interaction with the
physical environment.
Digital representation is the foundation of the digital twin. BIM
is the key technology to build the digital representation because
BIM establishes corresponding information models for the target
components and collects information corresponding to compo-
nents. Three-dimensional modeling of BIM for specific construc-
tion projects can provide stakeholders with a visualization function
to show the overall situation of the project and its components
(Davis 2003). Considering the limitations of hardware and software
and the purpose of BIM usage in the different stages, it is not
necessary to model BIM objects with 100% reality ( CIC 2019d ).
As a result, the level of details (LoDs) can be utilized to determine
the minimum detail level of BIM objects. The American Institute
of Architects (AIA) defines LoDs as the measure, estimate, and
schedule progression of a model ( BIMForum 2016). The CityGML
standard clarifies that an object has different representations for
each LoD, which allows for analyzing and visualizing the same
object with several degrees of resolution ( Gröger and Plümer
2012).

The extension of the LoDs definition supports and completes
the BIM utilization, which facilitates the description of BIM used
in BIM plans ( Tolmer et al. 2017 ). In addition, sensor technology
and sensor devices are employed to collect various real-time data
in the whole life cycle ( Lu et al. 2020c ). Data storage, integration,
and analytics are operated to capture, integrate, and analyze these
sensor data. Such processes are conducted to obtain the required
knowledge and realize added-value, supported by algorithmic
analytics ( Babiceanu and Seker 2016 ).
Some of the existing literature has indicated that construction-
related applications of digital twins, and other studies are related to
multiple standards and research on LoDs in the construction field.
However, the literature review revealed that there is currently no
existing research available that jointly considers the digital twin
and LoDs in the construction sites management domain. The cur-
rent research gaps are as follows: (1) there is no existing research
that considers digital twins for the whole construction site process
management and its comprehensive application for construction
sites; (2) no standards or literature are available that define the
LoDs of BIM for construction sites management and its intuitive
model examples; and (3) no studies have yet explored the chal-
lenges faced by the key technologies of digital twins in practical
applications and the critical views of managers in a transinterna-
tional background. As a result, this study proposes a framework
to utilize digital twins for construction site monitoring and manage-
ment and extends the existing LoDs of BIM for construction site
management based on industry requirement analysis, a comprehen-
sive literature review, and practical experience in related fields to
bridge the research gaps.
The “Literature Review ” section covers the previous studies,
and analyzes and summarizes the application cases to determine
the research gaps. The “Proposed Digital Twin Framework” section
analyzes and outlines the operation principle and mechanism of the
digital twin for construction sites, including digital representation;
IoT; data storage, integration, and analytics; and interaction with
the physical environment. For construction site monitoring and
management, the digital twin technology contributes to realizing
the integration of various construction site information, effectively
managing and centralizing these information resources. As a result,
the various requirements of construction sites at different stages can
be monitored in real-time, which further provides decision-making
support based on digital information and predicts the future devel-
opment. The study expands the existing LoDs to construction
sites for achieving BIM use expectation in the “LoDs of Digital
Twins for Construction Sites ” section. Ultimately, the framework
proposed by this research can result in better construction site
processes monitoring and management, including more efficiently
responding to incidents, work efficiency enhancement, emission
and energy performance optimization, safety, and quality promo-
tion as well as equipment, assets, worker power, and supply chain
administration improvement. Interviews and questionnaires are
undertaken to gather the opinions from experts for validating the
framework and supplementing the problems this framework might
encounter in practical application in the “V alidation” section. The
“Future Directions” section points out some technical and nontech-
nical challenges for future study.
Literature Review
Digital twin technology provides a rich virtual representation of the
physical environment ( Kaur et al. 2020 ) for the entire life cycle.
It can support the planning, tracking, monitoring, operating, risks
and problems identifying, improvement, performance optimization,
maintenance, and future resources requirement predicting ( Kaur
et al. 2020 ; Schleich et al. 2017 ). At present, this technology
has been applied in many fields, including construction, industry,
aviation, manufacturing, and health care as well as agriculture and
others. Digital twins have been developed well in fields such as
manufacturing. It guarantees support to manufacturing activities
such as monitoring, management, optimization, and maintenance
(Cimino et al. 2019 ). Furthermore, it can enhance process effi-
ciency to increase the productivity and reduce the energy consump-
tion, as well as predict future resource requirements ( Park et al.
2020). The technology provides the capabilities of tracing product
fault sources and analyzing production efficient bottlenecks ( Lu
et al. 2020c ).
In the health care area, the digital representation of an individ-
ual, including personal data like health data, activity data, and emo-
tional status, can assist in comprehending personal well-being and
working conditions in a factory. This can lead to improving the
psychological and physical health of employees and achieving
upskilling and the best production performance through ultrareal-
istic training programs ( Kendzierskyj et al. 2019 ; Lu et al. 2020c ).
In the aviation sphere, digital twin technology contributes to mov-
ing the aerospace industry from reactive to proactive maintenance
based on predicting the structural behavior and asset failure by sim-
ulating an aircraft ’s behavior and pinpointing critical weak spots
(Glaessgen and Stargel 2012 ; Lu et al. 2020c ). In the agricultural
domain, the digital twin can be utilized to predict climate change,
monitor the growth of plants, and generate advice to farmers for
ensuring harvesting at the most cost-efficient time. It also informs
about changes in the microenvironment such as temperature,
pressure, pH, and soil nutrients to provide improved feedback
(Barnabas and Raj 2020 ).
Digital Twins ’ Application in Construction-Related
Scenarios
The construction-related field has been exploited for smart cities,
built environments, facilities, infrastructure, construction, proper-
ties or estates, workspaces, transportation, and parking systems.
More specifically, in the smart cities scenario, digital twin technol-
ogy can capture the special and temporal implications to optimize
the city. It can accomplish this by detecting urban events, public
traffic, and public environmental monitoring ( Wang et al. 2019 ).
Further, Francisco et al. ( 2020) identified that it can monitor and
solve city environmental and energy problems to help realize low
carbon intensity.
The built-environment domain utilizes digital twins to analyze
climate, carbon, and circular economy outcomes ( Kaewunruen
et al. 2019 ), as well as energy use, air pollution, and water quality
(Ham and Kim 2020 ). Fan et al. ( 2020) developed it to help in
monitoring and analyzing the various situations during built-
environment disruptions in disasters. In extreme weather condi-
tions, based on the visibility of the disaster and the city interaction,
decision makers can monitor and analyze various situations in the
built-environment disaster and the impact on people ( Fan et al.
2020; Ham and Kim 2020 ). Furthermore, it can improve the ability
to deal with the destruction of the built environment in a disaster
and community disaster management for community members,
volunteer responders, residents, and other stakeholders ( Fan et al.
2020; Ford and Wolf 2020 ).
In the property/estate sphere, Barnabas and Raj ( 2020) applied
digital twins to analyze the estate plans to maximize the utilization
of the space. In the workspace field, the digital twin can support
optimal design and usage of office space by leveraging its occu-
pants
’ health and performance data. This can enable proprietors

For buildings, the most significant application aspect is energy
performance assessment and management. One such instance is
building energy analysis during the design phase ( Lydon et al.
2019), and near-real-time energy management during the operation
phase. Based on clarifying the annual energy consumption bench-

quantify the variation between daily energy performance and the
benchmark target for adjusting the building energy plan retrofit
strategy, it can be based on the impact indicators to finally achieve
near-real-time energy management (Francisco et al. 2020). Lu et al.

based on pictures and computer-aided design (CAD) drawings for
existing buildings in the operation and maintenance stages. It has
the ability to effectively access the latest information on existing
buildings in the operation and maintenance phases, which helps
to avoid decision delays caused by incorrect or outdated building
information. Overall, it avoids the negative effects of the response
to residents ’ daily requests and even emergencies. Therefore, this
method can improve organizational efficiency.
There are few relevant literature studies the construction field.
One specific study undertaken by Boje et al. ( 2020) put forward the
transition path from the construction BIM tool to the construction
digital twin based on semantics, after reviewing the multifaceted
applications of BIM during the construction stage. This study in-
dicated some potential methods and technologies to be considered
for digital twins in the construction sphere. The study only touched
on a few points concerning the construction site. It also summarized
the limits and requirements for future construction digital twin
development. Lin et al. ( 2019) proposed analogous digital twin –
based structural safety management in bridge construction. The
construction process, as well as the corresponding structural per-
formance during construction, can be visualized through the inte-
gration of the structural analysis model and the simulation results,
as well as the monitoring data.
In regard to infrastructure, it can be used to monitor infrastruc-
ture conditions (Fan et al. 2020 ) that contribute to decision-making
for infrastructure risk management ( Ham and Kim 2020 ). As for
facilities, the digital twin technology can monitor the working sta-
tus of a facility and further discover any anomalies in time ( Lu et al.
2020b). In the transport systems area, the technology contributes to
self-driving technology by robustly enhancing radar and image rec-
ognition as well as vehicle-to-vehicle communication capabilities.
For instance, it can enable the prediction of bus arrival times, cloud-
based driving direction systems, and active accident-avoidance
applications ( Wang et al. 2019 ). For city parking networks, the
technology can identify underused locations to drive decision mak-
ing on infrastructure reallocation ( Arup 2019 ).
In summary, digital twin technology is currently applied in a
multitude of fields, including construction. Although there are stud-
ies available on how to transition from BIM to digital twins in the
field of construction, the papers only touched on a few points con-
cerning the construction site. Few studies emphasized the specific
aspects of digital twin technology for the construction process,
such as the structural safety management of bridge construction.
Nevertheless, there is a lack of research on the digital twin tech-
nology for completed construction site process management and
its comprehensive application for construction sites.

Level of detail is essentially how much detail is included in the
model element. Level of development is the degree to which the
element’s geometry and attached information has been considered
(BIMForum 2013 ). In addition, Trani et al. ( 2015) explored the
LoDs for construction site design, classifying the levels into pre-
liminary, clear, and execution levels and demonstrated that the de-
tail level clarifies all design issues between different designers.
However, that study focused on the LoDs in construction site de-
sign rather than the construction site management. Cassano and
Trani ( 2017) explored the LoDs for construction site elements.
They divided the levels into conceptual, developed, detailed, and
installed. Their study considered the stages between the draft de-
sign and the detailed design, and the construction stage developed
by contractors for the installation design. However, they did not
consider the whole construction site life cycle.
In general, the major current gaps are as follows: (1) there is no
existing research that considers digital twins for whole construction
site process management and its comprehensive application for
construction sites; and (2) no standards and literature are available
that define the LoDs of BIM for construction sites management and
its intuitive model example.
Proposed Digital Twin Framework
The digital twin presents the bidirectional dynamic mapping
between a physical object and its digital model in cyberspace ( Tao
et al. 2018 ). The establishment of a digital twin framework for
construction site management requires four core components work-
ing together:
1. A physical environment.
2. A digital environment containing the digital model that abstracts
the characteristics of the physical counterpart and a data
processing module that is capable of extracting information
from heterogeneous multisource data to construct real-time rep-
resentation of physical objects ( Lu et al. 2020b ) that facilitate
the flow of information from raw data to high-level comprehen-
sion for supporting humans decision making and further func-
tions at different levels ( Liu et al. 2018 ). Standards play critical
roles in providing information models that describe various
physical objects ( Lu et al. 2020c ).
3. A communication mechanism used for transmitting two-way
data between the digital and physical twins with real-time data
obtained through IoT state synchronization ( Lu et al. 2020c ).
4. Feedback to the physical environment application.

The NBIMS-US ( 2015) states that BIM is a technology that is

from design, construction, operation, and maintenance ( Panushev

building components and equipment components, among others,
and collects information corresponding to components. Three-

can provide stakeholders with a visualization function to show the
overall situation of the project and its components ( Davis 2003 ).
Furthermore, BIM allows centralized storage of information, rapid
updates, and multistakeholder access to reduce file waste by re-
ducing task redundancy, thereby achieving database efficiency.
BIM is a platform that enhances collaboration, efficacy, and quality.

To explain the application scenarios in the construction site
process, this research considers the construction site process from
inception feasibility, design authority, tender, procurement, and
preconstruction to construction stages. Concerning the initial fea-
sibility, historical site usage data are collected to deploy analysis in
the digital model for determining the site condition. For the design
stage, the digital model provides an intuitive and visual design
representation to facilitate cross-professional communication and
collaboration, and jointly optimizes the layout design plan to meet
the actual requirements ( Ning et al. 2011 ) and multiple objectives.
In the tender and procurement stages, time and cost, as well as other
data, are added to reflect the project economy and schedule plan-
ning visually. In the preconstruction stage, physical conditions such
as the weather data around the site, as well as channels, transpor-
tation, and logistics data and so on can be fed back into the model
for deducing the required simulation to predict future problems,
and then optimize the solution. For the construction phase, adding
real-time data contributes to monitoring the construction site in
real-time and optimizing the usage of facilities and equipment,
thereby saving money and adapting to the actual use of the con-
struction site. Furthermore, it can be utilized to synchronize the
plan, check collision conflicts such as the complex integrated
underground pipelines, distance measurement, automatic manufac-
turing, quantity measurement, and the estimation of component
equipment, among others.
Internet of Things
The Internet of Things is an extended and expanded network based
on the Internet ( Miorandi et al. 2012 ). Through sensing devices, all
items are connected to the Internet through the agreed protocol and
open standards to achieve intelligent identification and manage-
ment (Sheng et al. 2013 ). The vital sensing hardware infrastructure
comprises radio-frequency identification (RFID), near-field com-
munication (NFC), and sensor networks ( Whitmore et al. 2015 ).
The sensing devices can be utilized for different construction site
components to achieve certain functions. The main components of
the construction site include site space, boundary, road, facility,
equipment, material, and people, among others.
Regarding the site space, such as the working area and living
area, turning on the lights in a place without people wastes light
energy, except for special situations in which lights are needed
on the construction site. The use of occupancy sensors and light
sensors helps to automatically control and adjust the light system,
as well as manage energy consumption on the construction site
(Garg and Bansal 2000 ). For managing the construction site boun-
daries, sensors can be installed to detect when a door is opened or
closed for identifying any unauthorized access. Utilizing sensors
and actuators to track energy consumption in the construction site,
moreover, allows monitoring and controlling such building infra-
structure as HV AC systems. Construction site safety requirements
can be satisfied by conducting routine surveillance.
Concerning equipment such as construction tower cranes,
encoder sensors are installed to detect the horizontal angle of a
tower crane by quickly calculating the number of spins or the
plumb for preventing collisions (Lee et al. 2012). It can also support
equipment safety management by tracking equipment movement
and predicting operator blind spots ( V asenev et al. 2014). In terms
of personnel management, appropriately distributed wireless sen-
sors on work clothing, combined with applications, can be used
to locate a person and identify whether they are close to or in a
hazardous area ( El Amine and Mohamed 2013 ) of the construction
site, and then verify the required security measures. Furthermore,
personal health and work status can be understood by measuring
the heart rate and body temperature ( Stankovic 2014 ) to prevent
personnel accident occurrence during operation on the construction
site. The IoT can provide detailed, visual, and real-time information
of the supply chain and logistics and further achieve automatic
tracking and management, such as for adjusting the conveyance
route and the number of trucks that transport construction material
in order to improve the efficiency in the process of transportation
(V asenev et al. 2014). It can also be applied to trace construction
materials for managing quality and safety.
Data Storage, Integration, and Analytics
The data processing module is one of the core components of a
digital twin system. It can extract information from heterogeneous
multisource data and realize high-level comprehension from the
raw data through a series of processing stages ( Lu et al. 2020c ).
This section divides data processing into three phases: acquisition,
integration, and usage. It indicates the data processing from data
capture to usage in the digital twin, and points out the potential
problems that need to be solved in each stage, as well as possible
solutions and suggestions. Fig. 2 illustrates the data flow from raw
data to high-level understanding after the three mentioned process-
ing phases.

Data integration mainly covers three stages, namely, data map-
ping and data duplication detection, as well as data fusion. Gener-
ally, the problem that existing data management tools cannot solve
is that information sources do not share common terminology. In
particular, different data formats and architectures, as well as vari-
ous analysis tools and standards, should be considered. The main
goals of data integration are to improve the integrity by adding
more data sources, describing the characteristics of the attributes,
and enhancing the simplicity of the data available to users and
applications by eliminating redundant content ( Bleiholder and
Naumann 2009). Data mapping is the first step in data integration,
which refers to the process of establishing the correspondence be-
tween different data models using a set of definitions.
The second step is duplicate detection. The goal of this step is to
identify multiple semantic representations of the same real-world
entities ( Ramya et al. 2017 ). Data fusion is the key technology
driving digital twins. The process of data fusion fuses several re-
cords corresponding to an identical real-world entity into a single,
consistent, and clean tuple that represents the joint information to a
maximal extent ( Bleiholder and Naumann 2009 ; Bronselaer et al.
2014). It provides specific benefits such as better signal quality,
model performance optimization, prediction improvement, and a
more reliable basis for decision-making ( Liu et al. 2018 ).
The third step in data processing is to achieve a high-level
understanding of the raw data ( Kaur et al. 2020 ), which is driven
by the information fusion technologies. There are some applica-
tions in construction sites. For example, by combining the work-
load data generated by workers in the worker-power management
platform and the construction site sensor data, a dynamic model can
be built. The decision support mechanism consists of prediction
algorithms that support more insights through data fusion ( Wang
et al. 2019 ) for more accurately arranging daily worker power and
worker types.
Interaction with Physical Environment
The digital twin seamlessly merges the practical and virtual worlds
through continuous data exchange. The change in the practical
world directly affects the virtual world and vice versa. Through
integrated computing, the entire process of the digital and physi-
cal worlds is defined as closed-loop ( Guan et al. 2016 ), where
embedded computers and systems usually control and monitor
physical processes. In return, the physical processes can affect
computing. It should be emphasized that integration does not mean
a simple fusion of the physical world and digital world, but that
there is profound interaction between the physical and digital
components. This interaction can be divided into two types: one is
machine-to-machine interaction, and the other is human/object-to-
machine interaction.
For machine-to-machine interaction, the digital twin is able to
achieve dynamic reconfiguration and reorganizing with a high de-
gree of automation in multiple temporal and spatial dimensions due
to the tight coupling and coordination between the digital and
physical worlds (Ding et al. 2018). The collected data are processed
and automatically analyzed, and subsequently send feedback to the
monitoring, controlling, and management loop in the system.
Therefore, on the one hand, problems such as model deviation be-
tween the digital and physical worlds can be automatically adjusted
and corrected, the quality of the construction site model can be im-
proved. On the other hand, it is possible to respond flexibly to dis-
ruptions or modified requirements ( Wang et al. 2015 ).
In terms of human/object-to-machine interaction, the digital
model feeds back the monitoring results, simulation results (Boschert
and Rosen 2016), analysis support, and so on, to the physical con-
struction sites, contributing to a series of construction site process
management actions such as worker-power management, equip-
ment management, asset manageme nt, supply chain management,
safety analysis, and emissions and energy management, among
others. The digital twin can provide support for different stages
of the life cycle in the construction site. The digital twin provides
an intelligent scene of information on the available systems to en-
able participants in the various disciplines access to the models
and results at an early stage, and then optimize the plan for the
following stage. The digital twin model created in the process of
design can be reused in a subsequent process, and the information
it contains can interact with actual data using appropriate interfa-
ces ( Guan et al. 2016 ).
The actual data can be used to evaluate and verify the simulation
model during a later process operation, as well as the input and
output of new information, which enables continuous engineering
improvement. For example, if the project ’s expectations have been
changed and need to be modified, such as in the case of collision
problems found on the site, and time schedule adjustment prob-
lems, among others, the existing model can be utilized and modi-
fied to output the adjusted construction guidance plan. On the other
hand, if the operation data is also collected systematically as part of
the digital twin, it can be used to verify and update the existing
model under actual operating conditions, so that the acquired
knowledge can also be used for similar engineering in the future.
The contribution of the interaction with the physical environment in
construction sites application can be explained as follows:
 Site monitoring: The digital twin can provide users with an in-
terface that displays the information and real-time status of all
the systems for promoting more intuitive data analysis for the
construction site manager. The manager can view the different
aspects that occur on the construction site and at the same time,
extract various data bits and make visual comparisons and as-
sociations, and then make accurate, reasonable, and beneficial
decisions. Notably, in the face of emergencies, the manager can
find problems more quickly and respond more efficiently.
 Equipment management: An interface that visually displays the
equipment status can provide maintenance or replacement early
warnings and visually display the fault location in the digital
model to prevent equipment damage from affecting the con-
struction plan and delaying the construction process. Equipment
usage records, repair records, maintenance arrangements, and
scrap plans can be retrieved from the platform whenever neces-
sary and contribute to the lease and purchase adjustment and
arrangements.
 Asset management: Asset-related data can be directly displayed
in front of the manager through data visualization technology to
support decision making.
 Supply chain management: Supply chain route simulation and
its rationality and limitations analysis can be used for route
adjustment, logistics equipment, and personnel arrangements.
 Worker-power management: Workload data contribute to man-
agers’ accurately arranging daily worker power and worker
types and optimizing adjustment programs based on labor cost
calculations. The corresponding number of scaffold rentals may
also be adjusted.
 Safety analysis: On the one hand, in the construction site design
process, a reasonable structure must be designed so that it can

LoDs of Digital Twins for Construction Sites
In this section, the classification for the components in the con-
struction site is first illustrated for supporting the description of
the LoD division. A hierarchical insight of the construction site
components is given through the tree map (Fig. 3). The second
column of the subbranch is classified according to different com-
ponent categories. The third column is classified according to dif-
ferent functions of the same category. The fourth column signifies
detailed elements. Generally, the elements in the construction site
include the site space, such as the working area, living area and
business area, boundary, road, landscape, facility, equipment,
and prefabrication, as well as material and people. In addition,
the LoD-Graphics for construction sites is proposed from an overall
point of view, pointing out the principles and the required compo-
nents in the different subdivision stages.
The aforementioned are followed by the LoD-Graphics for con-
struction site components that describe the detailed division of sep-
arated components under the same division principle that refer to
approximate, basic, detailed, completed, and verified levels. The
minimum LoD requirement for construction site components di-
rectly explains the LoD relationship between the LoD-Graphics
for construction sites and the construction site components. To
be more specific, it illustrates what components and what LoDs
of the components should be included in the LoD 300. Sub-
sequently, the LoD-Information requirement is presented that con-
tains the project information, time data, and cost data.
LoD-Graphics for Construction Sites
Based on the LoDs, division principles, from approximate to basic,
are detailed, completed, and verified. The five LoD-Graphics for
construction sites are summarized as follows:
 LoD 100: the model elements are graphically represented within
the model by a rough three-dimensional (3D) shape.
 LoD 200: the basic space in the construction site is clearly
divided and the basic framework of the construction site is
graphically indicated.
 LoD 300: the construction site detailed plan and preparation can
be clearly identified.
 LoD 400: the construction site formation should be represented
as a complete and accurate 3D model.
 LoD 500: the model element is a field-verified representation.
Further interpretations and comparisons among these different
standards are given next, and supporting examples and more de-
tailed explanation are shown in Fig. 4. All information obtained
from LoD 100 elements must be considered approximate. In com-
parison with LoD 100, the space division on the site is evidently
reflected in LoD 200, including a number of allocated spaces for
operation and maintenance. In comparison with LoD 200, specific
mounting details are included in LoD 300. In contrast with LoD
300, the model components are more detailed and independent
in LoD 400. Any information is derivable from this level graphic
model, such as time and cost. More specifically, the BIM four-
dimensional (4D) model presents a project time process and the
association among components. Here, 4D time corresponds
to the order and work schedule. Therefore, the details should com-
prise the construction order information.
The BIM five-dimensional (5D) module should include a data-
base system to collect, analyze, and utilize related statistics like
area, volume, unit cost, and total price, among others, as well as
generate reports to provide information regarding project manage-
ment decisions. Accordingly, in 5D, each component in the digital
model must be clear and independent. Each detail should be illus-
trated independently; otherwise, the manager is prone to make er-
rors when calculating the areas through the model, which would
inflict a difference on the final cost of the materials, thereby affect-
ing the accuracy of the assessment. For instance, all layers of the
panels in wall components require calculation to get the accurate
cost. A similar scenario applies to the specific diameter and length
of each pipe in a network of underground pipes and the exact num-
ber of fire sprinklers; all of these relate directly to the cost.
LoD-Graphics for construction site components describes the
detailed division of the separated components. The summary and
examples are demonstrated in Fig. 5. It should be explained that the
stage of appearance of some components may not be from the
beginning, such as equipment and landscape, which appear in rel-
atively late stages.
The LoDs provide a snapshot of the elements from concept to
specified process. The AIA pointed out that only when all the re-
quirements specified in the definition are met can the element de-
velop to a given LoD ( BIMForum 2013); as a result, the concept of
minimum requirements for LoD is proposed. It should be empha-
sized that demand is cumulative. For a given element, each LoD ’s
definition includes all previous LoD requirements. For example, to
make an element qualify for the conditions of LoD 300, it must
meet all the requirements of 200 and 100 and the requirements
specified in the definition of LoD 300 ( BIMForum 2013). Table 2
indicates the LoD relationship between the LoD-Graphics for con-
struction sites and construction site components.

Data/Information Management
For a construction site, data management issues mainly involve
multiparty data management, project information management,
professional technical information, data cooperation and sharing,
and security and privacy issues.
The construction process has a fragmented and complex struc-
ture. It is an information-intensive process because a large volume
of information is exchanged and transferred during the project
life cycle ( Chen and Kamara 2011 ). The traditional paper-based
information management procedure leads to reworking, ineffi-
ciency, data loss, and falsification ( Chen and Kamara 2011 ). The
BIM model provides the possibility of solving these problems. BIM
can cover the entire stage of the project process, from start to
delivery, from which the information flow of the building infor-
mation model in the whole life cycle is unified and open, the in-
teraction process is efficient, and the model is digital and visual
(Ma and Ma 2017 ). Information standardization and integration
are tools for data loss prevention and defragmentation, resulting
in better information sharing, usage and reusage, as well as all par-
ties being involved through communication coordination ( Ozturk
2020).
The transmission and sharing of project information resources
are relatively difficult to achieve in the traditional approach,

Validation
This study adopted semistructured interviews and questionnaire
analysis to test and validate the proposed framework. Question-
naires were conducted to confirm the significance of the proposed
framework’s contribution and to check whether the components in-
cluded in the framework were crucial. Interviews were conducted
to review the framework and its component associations, applica-
tion coverage, and corresponding LoD definitions, and to obtain
in-depth comments and to determine the problems encountered
in the practical application of the framework. After comprehensive
consideration, 31 questionnaires and 10 interviews were conducted
from July 21 to August 6, 2020.
In the questionnaire survey, there were 31 participants, and all
the samples were valid. This number was considered sufficient to
balance the time constraints with the need for a robust sample. The
years for which respondents have been engaged in construction
ranged from 1 year to more than 20 years, and the distribution
of the construction processes in which they were involved included
inception feasibility, planning preliminary, scheme design, detailed
design, authority, submission to approving, tender, procurement,
preconstruction, construction, and research. Overall, the vast cover-
age and general participation make the results more objective and
rigorous. The working area of the participants covered Mainland
China, Hong Kong, China, the UK, the US, Dubai, Australia, and
Singapore. The feedback from such multiregional participants
made the validation of the framework more internationalized. The

Future Directions
At the current stage of the digital twin, there are both technical and
nontechnical challenges. At the technical level, one of the goals of
the digital twin in construction sites is to accumulate knowledge
from design to construction, which helps to continuously improve
the process and support optimization and decision making. The
challenge is to establish a more comprehensive and dynamic digital
model that integrates construction site life cycle monitoring, man-
agement, and operation, so that the entire process model can be
constantly adjusted and updated according to the corresponding
physical environment over time.
At the nontechnical level, existing digital twin applications are
primarily used to monitor and predict purposes and as support
for human decision making. Accordingly, human participation is
essential in the digital twin –driven intelligent manufacturing envi-
ronment. However, it is necessary to establish feedback and control
from the digital twin to the physical world with a certain degree of
autonomy. It is possible to complete many low-level operations
autonomously without the human involvement, whereas various
decision-making activities must be carried out by humans. The
current challenge is that the human element is still not seen as part
of intelligent manufacturing systems. In the future intelligent
manufacturing environment, more research should be done on the
importance and effect of human participation in digital twins.
Another major challenge is how to combine the nontechnical
factors in construction projects to achieve the objectives. This is
a potential point for future research considering the aspects of
construction governance, such as transparency of information, sup-
pressing project corruption effectively, and social benefits improve-
ment, such as labor employment, and market benefits enhancement
such as meeting market demand and promoting the development of
upstream and downstream industries.
Additionally, ethical and moral data issues are challenges for
digital twin databases, and they can be partly handled through
hardware, data, and permissions. The hardware should conduct de-
vice management and security audits for IoT devices to enable the
devices for security use. For data, on the one hand, permissions and
authentication to clarify who the data are open to and shared with
should be set up in order to prevent unauthorized use of data. On
the other hand, identifying and intercepting fake and false sensor
data, and then identifying the correct data and encrypting the data to
protect sensitive data, is required.
Conclusions
Process integration and collaboration are considered as the key en-
abling technologies that drive the construction industry to increase
productivity and efficiency. Based on industry requirement analy-
sis, a comprehensive literature review, and practical experience in
related fields, this research proposed a framework to utilize the
digital twin for construction site management and to extend the
existing LoDs of BIM for construction site management to bridge
the research gaps. Extension of the LoDs ’ definition is the best way
to be compatible with construction site management methods and
meet management requirements.
This study first analyzed and outlined the components of the
digital twin and indicated the operation principle and mechanism
involving digital representation based on BIM, IoT, and data
processing including data storage, integration, analytics, and the
interaction with the physical environment. It emphasized the
way the digital twin can be used in construction sites.
Second, it expanded the existing LoDs in construction sites for
achieving BIM use expectation, dividing construction site compo-
nents at the beginning for better illustrating the level division
for graphics and information details. The LoD-Graphics and LoD-
Information are divided based on the level’s principle from approxi-
mate, basic, detailed, completed, and verified. BIM is able to
support a number of detailed actions during construction site
processing. This research considered the construction site process
from inception feasibility, design authority, tender, procurement,
and preconstruction (planning) to the construction stages, and then
points out the minimum LoD requirement for different BIM uses in
different stages.
Third, this research puts forward a solution to the problem of
data management involving multiparty data management, project
information management, professional technical information, data
cooperation and sharing, and security and privacy issues. Further-
more, interviews and questionnaires with professionals have vali-
dated the value of this framework, complementing the problems
that the framework may encounter in practice. Finally, this research
indicates the future research directions about how humans can con-
tribute to digital twins and the nontechnical factors in a project.
However, there are some limitations in this research. Because of
the lack of a completed framework application case, it is difficult to
clarify the improvement degree of the overall performance in the
validation stage. Moreover, time and resources limited the number
of interviews and questionnaires. More supplementary information
can be extracted with as the number of validation participants
increases.
