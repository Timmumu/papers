Case Study
Driving Factors for the Adoption of Digital Twin
Technology Implementation for Construction
Project Performance in Nigeria
Victor Adetunji Arowoiya 1; Ayodeji Emmanuel Oke 2; Lekan Damilola Ojo 3; and Ayomide Oluwafemi Adelusi 4
Abstract: The adoption of digital twin technology (DTT) for enhancing construction project performance, sustainability, and safety of
construction workers, among others, is common practice in developed countries. Meanwhile, construction projects in developing nations
are suffering from poor outcomes, which could benefit from DTT. Therefore, this research investigates the drivers of the adoption of DTT
implementation in the Nigerian construction industry. Close-ended questionnaires were administered to digitally inclined professionals using
purposive and snowballing techniques to elicit necessary information on the drivers of DTT implementation. These respondents include
architects, engineers, quantity surveyors, and builders in the study area. It was found that technological advancement/trend, reliable data
storage, safety, availability of software, customer satisfaction, and accessibility were the top-ranked drivers for implementing DTT.
A Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to know whether the data are normally distributed or not, which led to the use of the Kruskal-Wallis
H-test. The Kruskal-Wallis test revealed that government policies have diverging views among the professionals, whereas other factors have
converging opinions among the respondents. Factor analysis was conducted to group the key drivers for the implementation of DTT into
innovation, operation, quality, performance, and policy drivers. The findings of this study will provide a reference point for researchers and
construction organizations on the driving force that brings about the implementation of DTT in a business context and the construction
industry. This study realizes that one of the key drivers of the adoption of this technology is a desire for innovation and technological
advancement that can be achieved when the technology performs to the expectation of clients, organizations, and the architecture, engineer-
ing, and construction (AEC) industry. The study also suggests ways of implementing DTT in the construction industry as well. DOI: 10.1061/

Practical Applications: A digital twin (DT) is a virtual replica or representation of a physical object or product. This research proposes
practical implications of DT technology adoption in a developing country with a focus on major drivers for enhancing the uptake of the
technology in the construction industry. This will give more insight to stakeholders on major drivers to improve its implementation in the
construction and other sectors of the country as well as other developing countries. Technological advancement in different countries as
shown in the study will expose professionals to the benefits of digital technologies as well as keep the professionals and other stakeholders
abreast of the potential benefits. This can be realized when the government is at the frontline in the usage of DT for public construction
projects. In addition, there is a need for policy formulation for its implementation in the construction industry to ensure projects are
sustainable and delivered to the satisfaction of concerned stakeholders.

Introduction
In general, the construction industry contributes around 7% –10%
to the gross domestic product (GDP) of different countries,
especially developing nations ( Oladinrin et al. 2012 ; Saleh and
Al-Swidi 2019 ). This sector provides employment, promotes
growth and development, and is intertwined with other industries
(Dixit et al. 2017 ). Meanwhile, the construction industry faces
many issues, which include low productivity, inadequate commu-
nication, poor technological advancement, poor project perfor-
mance, fragmentation, low innovation, and so on ( Farmer 2016 ),
despite several technologies available to improve productivity
and performance in the industry. Besides, the construction industry
has been reported to be slow at embracing innovative technologies
compared with other sectors ( Morledge 2011 ).
Through the fourth industrial revolution (4IR), the construction
industry is experiencing fast-paced growth in different stages of
building, such as design and engineering, construction, operation
and maintenance, and demolition, in developed climes ( Ebekozien
and Aigbavboa 2021). However, its adoption is slow and the use of
4IR technologies is at a nascent stage in the architecture, engineer-
ing, and construction (AEC) industry in developing countries.
The 4IR technologies such as augmented reality/virtual reality
(AR/VR), cyber-physical systems, and DTs have brought increased

in construction due to the benefits derived. These benefits include
the safety of workers, cost efficiency, timely delivery of projects,

waste management, and productivity improvement ( Akanmu et al.

In particular, digital twin is not a new concept; it was introduced
by Micheal Grieves in 2003 by presenting a virtual prototype of a
physical product ( Batty 2018 ; Grieves 2014). It is referred to as a
two-way interaction between the physical entity and a virtual or
digital replica for full integration (Alam and El Saddik 2017). There
are numerous misconceptions about the nomenclatures of DT, such
as digital models, digital shadows, and digital twins ( Sepasgozar
2021). A digital model is just a digital representation of a physical
system with no interaction, whereas a digital shadow is the one-way
interaction of a digital replica and a physical entity. DT works when
there is full integration with several enabling technologies such as
building information modeling (BIM), wireless sensor networks
(WSN), data analytics, and machine learning. Data analytics,
artificial intelligence (AI), and machine learning help to enhance
the capabilities of the DT in anomaly detection, optimization,
prediction, and knowing the health status of equipment or systems
(Boje et al. 2020 ).
Many researchers have examined DTs in various sectors like
manufacturing, health, aeronautic s and aviation, education, auto-
motive, and so on ( Tao et al. 2019a ; Tuegel et al. 2011 ). In the
construction industry, Opoku et al. ( 2022) carried out a systematic
review of the drivers of DT adoption. It was revealed that devel-
oped countries like the US, Australia, the UK, and Italy were
the countries advancing its usage compared with developing
countries. Other researchers also examined DT application in
construction, generally about workforce safety through reviews
(Opoku et al. 2021 ; Hou et al. 2021 ; Ozturk 2021 ; Deng et al.
2021). Little or no attention has been paid to the empirical
study of the drivers of the adoption of DT in the construction
industry, especially in developing countries like Nigeria. This
study will conduct empirical research on the drivers of DT imple-
mentation in developing countries based on the perception of
professionals.
This research will give insight to practitioners, clients, govern-
ment, educators, and other stakeholders concerning the pull factors
to be considered for implementing DT in the Nigerian construction
industry. It will help stakeholders to better understand the DT con-
cept and propel readiness to embrace this technology. The rest of this
study is structured as follows: section “Literature Review” reviews
related and previous work on DT and the drivers. Section “Research
Methods” discusses the research approach or methodology adopted
in this study, and section “Analysis and Review” reveals the discus-
sion of the survey conducted in relation to previous findings and the
implications. The last section provides the conclusion, recommenda-
tions, and areas for further studies.
Literature Review
Introduction
This section contains a review of the driving factors that make DT
to be adopted in any stages of built assets, i.e., design, construction,
operation and maintenance, and demolition stage of built assets.
These drivers of DT that can enhance implementation in the con-
struction industry are synthesized logically.
Drivers of Digital Twin Technology for Construction
Project Performance
A DT has three major components, which are a physical entity,
connected data, and a virtual entity. Most previous research on
DTs was largely based on reviews. This section reviews what has
been stated by other researchers in the extant literature. However,
this research conducted an empirical validation based on the
opinions of professionals. Some of these factors are discussed in
the following sections. The summary of the literature review was

Accessibility and Flexibility
Digital technologies have come to disrupt traditional ways of doing
things in the construction industry. The issue of accessibility and
flexibility should not be left out ( Del Giudice et al. 2021 ).
Fachrunnisa et al. ( 2020) noted that for digital transformation to
be implemented in firms or organizations, there must be flexibility,
agility, and adaptability of its use by workers. Also, there should be
accessibility to data and information for use by organizations at any
time. This information must be restricted from public use, i.e., data
privacy should be guaranteed. Professionals and organizations
should have equal access and opportunities to any digital technol-
ogies made available to improve the productivity and performance
of construction or built assets.
Trust Level
The application of DTs in small-medium enterprises (SMEs) and
large organizations requires trust. DTs will be implemented when
they can demonstrate the separation of data and secure data.
Herein, this includes the separation of nonvolatile and therefore
non-real-time-dependent data such as ground plan, layout, pur-
pose of the production, machine, parts list, qualification of em-
ployees, shifts, and so on. V olatile time-dependent data such as
production flow, movements of e mployees, machine assignment,
transport routes, and others come to bear ( Uhlemann et al. 2017 ).
When the trust level about digital security and privacy is minimal,
it hampers the propensity of users of DTs in organizations.
Government and large establishments would not want to adopt
DTs if there is a high level of risk and public display of private
or personal data. Also, there should be a high level of trust in the
enumerated capabilities of DTs for employers to adopt ( OECD
2016).
Government Policies
To ensure that the benefits of new technology reach every layer
of society with enforced ethical consideration, the formulation
of policies and regulations is required. There should be a major
effort toward the democratization of technology. The different
governmental bodies should initiate feasibility studies for utilizing
DTs within their sectors. In particular, investigating different sce-
narios on how they can profit from projecting insights obtained
from relevant predictive twins into their governmental responsibil-
ities is important ( Leng et al. 2019 ).
Policies implemented by the government do drive organizations
to change the use of digital technologies. The components for cre-
ating DTs are very expensive, so there should be an arrangement for
the government and organizations to implement these technologies
for more productivity. The government can subsidies these technol-
ogies and give a chance for installment payments to the companies.
This should be cut across every sector in the nation ( Oke and
Arowoiya 2021a ).

Friendliness
Being user-friendly is a valuable concept and an overworked phrase
that has lost much of its meaning. User-friendly is not merely the
addition of high-tech hardware such as a mouse, icons, or three-
dimensional graphics. This refer to ease at which end users can ac-
cess, navigate different softwares used in creating digital twin. For
example, digital twin cloud services can aid in simulation and model
which could learn by themselves. This give increase insights, easy-
to-understand data and user friendly dashboards that can be utilized
by teams in business or organization without the need of data experts
(Oladinrin et al. 2023). We infer that user-friendly suggests an entity
that is warm and comforting to the one who uses it (Jara et al. 2014).
Organizational Acceptance
In the last century, there was intense competition to enhance infor-
mation and communication technology (ICT), which caused rapid
changes and development of technologies. As a result, many organ-
izations had to adjust and adapt to the changes in ICT. At present,
ICT is being used as a tool to advance development in numerous
fields (Zou et al. 2017 ). A DT is referred to as an innovatively new
technology for executives and employees. Therefore, it is necessary
to understand the benefits of the technology for the organizations to
which they belong.
Drive for Innovation
Firms are trying to maximize their innovation efforts by efficiently
serving their new or existing customers with new innovative or modi-
fied products and services, adding value in the customer’se y e s(Brem
and V oigt 2019). There was a belief that the main source of innovation
was large enterprises. The increase in firm size leads to higher inno-
vation capabilities; thus, large firms can make greater innovation de-
velopments than small ones. The ICT industry is characterized by
continuous technological change, high research and development
(R&D) expenditures, and innovation. In service sectors, innovation
heavily depends on the skills and knowledge of employees, which
are oriented to fit specific customers’ wants by developing innovation
in close interaction with clients ( De Jong and Marsili 2016 ).
Quality and Safety
In any business organization or construction industry, profit and
delivery of quality products are the ultimate goals. Profit may
be maximized by cutting costs for the same selling price per unit.
If it is a monopolistic business, without giving much importance to
cost-reduction programs, the price may be fixed suitably to earn
sufficient profit. To survive in a competitive business environment,
goods and services produced by a firm should have the minimum
required quality. Extra quality means extra cost, so the level of qual-
ity should be decided by other factors such that the product is well
absorbed in the market. Quality is a measure of how closely a good
or service conforms to a specified standard that can be achieved by
a digital replica of the product ( Raz et al. 2017 ).
Quality standards may be any combination of attributes and
variables of the product being manufactured. The attributes will
include performance, reliability, appearance, commitment to deliv-
ery time, and so on, and the variables may be some measurement
variables like length, width, height, diameter, surface finish, and so
on (Tah and Carr 2020). Quality begins with the design of a product
to the customer ’s specifications. It further involves the established
measurement standards, the use of proper material, the selection
of a suitable manufacturing process, and so on. Quality is a rela-
tive term generally used concerning the end use of the product.

Technological Advancement/Trends
Technology advancement is generally regarded as a catalyst for na-
tional development because it offers, among other things, the nec-
essary support for change in all major sectors of the economy, most
especially in the industrial sectors. Therefore, it is unarguably the
prime source of change of innovations and adaptations required for
improving production methods needed to propel growth and devel-
opment. Technological advancement, which is required to acquire,
select, or utilize source technology for company competitiveness,
becomes the competitive edge and key capability to gain distinc-
tiveness (Wang et al. 2014). Technological results and commerciali-
zation are the outcomes of technological development capability.
There is a need to identify the capability required for successful
technological development performance and technology commer-
cialization for companies.
Better Management and Performance
The concept of performance is a reference both in theoretical ap-
proaches and practice. The field of economic performance includes
various terms, the most important of which include competitiveness,
productivity, profitability, and business growth. DT implementation
can better the project management process and enhance effective
stakeholder collaboration during the construction process. Activities
in construction are properly managed with the use of DT applications
in the construction industry ( Opoku et al. 2021 ). DTs have shown
considerable potential in different fields. However, the development
of DTs is still in its infancy. No universal definition, implementation
framework, and protocol are available. Therefore, it can be found
that there lack of comprehensive and in-depth analysis of DTs from
the perspective of concepts, technologies, and industrial applications
(Jo et al. 2018 ).
Reliable Data Storage and Reliability
With the rapid development of embedded technology, embedded
systems have widely infiltrated science, engineering, military tech-
nology, and other areas of daily life. Every field of study wants the
reliability of the data stored. This has been a widely concerning
issue, which affects product performance, stability, and security.
Therefore, the reliability of embedded systems technology for
data storage has become a new hotspot and may affect the future
direction of the development of embedded systems ( Knapp et al.
2017). Reliability refers to when a data storage system ensures data
integrity and effectiveness. This is when data kept are not aborted,
damaged, or lost. The reliability of data storage is affected by many
factors such as power failure, memory failure, system instability,
human error, and so on. They are a direct result of data storage
reliability problems. Therefore, the reliability of data storage is a
matter of hardware, storage technology, operating systems, and
other areas of the comprehensive study ( Wang et al. 2018 ).
Client and Industry Demand
The demand for DT technology is being felt across all sectors.
From manufacturers who are already using DT to simulate their
processes to aging hospitals that need to understand their assets
so they can make informed decisions, and infrastructure projects,
including critical assets such as pavements and rail, all are starting
to see the benefits of combining building information modeling
(BIM) with sensor technology (Y u et al. 2020). For business owners
who want to take their business to the next level, the adoption of DT
technology is essential ( Jara et al. 2014 ).
Availability of Software
The development phase includes building a toolkit that can be used
to create a unique DT for various assets. Toolkit development is the
hardest part of the DT ’s life cycle. It is typically done in R&D by
highly specialized data scientists, machine learning specialists, and
more. The software development work involves gathering informa-
tion about the physical characteristics of the asset, the operating
environment, and any conditions under which the asset is operated.
In addition, the automatically collected data from the asset are re-
quired to develop the required machine learning methodologies.
The result is a mathematical model that can be used to model the
behavior and performance of an asset ( Yahya and Shafie 2018 ).
Basic Operation Skills
Technical skills are necessary for workers to use these technologies
in construction or building assets. Workers and professionals must
know how to effectively use these technologies in the organization
(Andrews et al. 2018 ). Firms should be ready to train their workers
with innovative technologies to improve the performance of proj-
ects. This training can also be incorporated into tertiary institutions
to enhance students ability to meet work demands in the industry
(Oke and Arowoiya 2021b ).
Customer Satisfaction
Users and organizations must be satisfied and get value from any
emerging technologies such as the DT they are using to improve
their business. If there is a low level of satisfaction, it will surely
affect the level of demand by managers of firms. Customer needs
keep changing over time but emerging technologies should be able
to meet the multidimensional needs of clients in construction. With
the capabilities of DTs to perform diagnostics, prognostics, both
predictive and prescriptive of any system accurately, it will propel
more of its usage in the design, construction, operation, and main-
tenance of built assets ( Williams and Naumann 2011 ).
Research Methods
A quantitative research method was adopted to investigate the driv-
ers of DTs using a survey technique. Questionnaires were used to
elicit information from construction professionals in Lagos state,
Nigeria. Lagos state was chosen based on the level of growth of
digital technologies among construction professionals in the indus-
try compared with other states in Nigeria, the ever-increasing level
of urbanization in the state, and the volume of construction projects
being executed ( Fagbenle et al. 2011 ).
Population
The study population was composed of architects, engineers,
builders, and quantity surveyors. The process of selecting these
professionals was through purposive and snowballing techniques,
which means that not all professionals in Lagos state were included
(Kothari 2014). Purposive sampling was based on the judgment of
the researcher. Professionals with industrial technology skills were

Data Collection
The research adopted questionnaires as the instrument for primary
data collection targeted at addressing the research objective. The
questions were asked on a five-point scale with 5 = V ery high,
4 = High, 3 = Average, 2 = Low, and 1 = V ery low. The Likert
scale was chosen for ease and uniformity of responses. The Likert
scale provides a more versatile and user-friendly means to address
the drivers of DTs. The professionals were asked to rank how im-
portant the factors or variables can influence DT adoption.
The questionnaire designed for this research was such that the
first section dwelt on the background information of the respond-
ents, and the other section focused on the drivers of DT implemen-
tation on construction projects. The drivers used in the second
section of the questionnaire were obtained from extant literature
on drivers of DTs identified in previous studies. The questionnaire
was administered to 369 construction professionals in the target
area of study. The construction professionals work in contracting
firms, consultancy firms, and government agencies that play impor-
tant roles in building construction in Lagos state. Of the 369 copies
of the questionnaire administered, 178 were found suitable for use,
which represents a 48.2% response rate ( Fosnacht et al. 2017 ).
Data Analysis
The following statistical techniques were used in analyzing the
data: frequency, percentile, mean item score (MIS); standard
deviation (SD), factor analysis, Shapiro-Wilk test, and Kruskal-
Wallis H-test.
Frequency and percentiles were adopted to analyze the demo-
graphic information of the respondents. MIS was used for analyz-
ing and ranking the opinions of the respondents on the drivers.
SD was employed where two or more variables had the same MIS.
An item with a lower SD was ranked higher than another variable
with a mean score but a high SD. The Kruskal-Wallis H-test was
used to test the significant difference in the opinion of four catego-
ries of respondents.
The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of the dis-
tribution. This is used to know whether the factors are normally
distributed or not ( Corder and Foreman 2014 ). The result showed
that the factors are not normally distributed, which led to the use of
the Kruskal-Wallis test. Factor analysis was used to group variables
into clusters for ease of discussion. The sample-to-item ratio in
the study satisfied the minimum requirement of 5∶1 for conducting
factor analysis ( Pallant 2020 ). Cronbach ’s alpha test was used in
this research to test the reliability and validity of items that consti-
tute each factor formed in the factor analysis ( Taber 2018 ). In
addition, Horn parallel analysis was conducted to determine the
most suitable number of components for the factor analysis (Pallant
2007).

Horn Parallel Analysis . The minimal level of factor loading
acceptance is 0.3 or 0.4, which serves as a rule of thumb to deter-
mine significance ( Hair et al. 2010 ). The factor loading value for
this study was set at 0.3 in the Statistical Package for the Social
Sciences (SPSS) software version 28, and all the loadings were
above the cut-off value, which was retained. To determine the num-
ber of factors to be extracted, horn parallel analysis was conducted
after the actual eigenvalue was conducted for principal component
analysis. Horn ’s parallel analysis is said to be the most accurate
method for determining the number of factors to retain compared
with Kaiser’s criterion and scree test, which made us consider it in
the study (Pallant 2007). Both Kaiser ’s criterion (eigenvalue ex-
ceeding 1) and the screen test revealed the presence of five com-
ponents that need to be verified in parallel analysis. With the aid of
Horn’s parallel analysis as the most accurate method to determine
the number of factors to be extracted (Pallant 2007), all five com-
ponents were supported and accepted. The rule governing the par-
allel analysis is that the actual eigenvalue from principal component
analysis (PCA) (SPSS output) must be greater than the criterion
value from parallel analysis as indicated in Table 4.
Factor Analysis. The adequacy of the sampling approach was con-
sidered in terms of size of the set data and the extent of the pop-
ulation. According to Tabachnick and Fidell ( 2007), Bartlett ’s test
of sphericity should be significant ( p < 0.05) for factor analysis to
be suitable. They also propounded that a Kaiser —Meyer—Olkin
(KMO) index should range from 0 to 1, with 0.50 considered suit-
able for factor analysis. In this case, the KMO index is 0.786, and
the test of sphericity is significant with a value of 0.000 (Table 5);
therefore, factor analysis is suitable in both cases ( Hadi et al. 2016).

Discussion of Findings
As presented in Table 6, a model of five items was deemed adequate
to represent the drivers of DT for construction project performance.
Therefore, only five reduced components were obtained from this
factor analysis. It becomes highly imperative to name the five ex-
tracted components before a proper and concise interpretation can
be made. From the literature, there exists little or no known prin-
ciple for naming extracted components of factor analysis ( Henson
and Roberts 2006 ). The implied meaning of the preceding state-
ment is that naming these factors could be subjective and based on
the perception, background, and training/education of the viewer/
researcher/analyst. After careful consideration of the variables
grouped into each component, the following names were deemed
appropriate: (1) innovation drivers, (2) operation drivers, (3) quality
drivers, (4) performance drivers, and (5) policy drivers.
Innovation Drivers
Innovation drivers, which was the first component extracted, con-
tains five items, and it depicts a driver that has to do with processes
involved in organizing a construction project. Also, it refers to
concept-driven factors that would enhance improvements in design,
project planning, delivery, safety, and decision making. The five
items under this category are as follows: drive for innovation, with
a factor loading of 0.785; industry demand, with a factor loading of
0.734; accessibility, with a factor loading of 0.712; and availability
of software, with a factor loading of 0.680, and safety, with a
significance of 0.384. In reality, the drive for innovation is essential
for adopting technology by clients, organizations, governments,
and professionals ( Adafin et al. 2022 ). Construction stakeholders
without enthusiasm for technology may prefer the use of traditional
approaches as long as they can achieve the project goals. The inertia
to change construction stakeholders forms an impediment to the

Quality Drivers
Quality driver is the name allotted to the third extracted component.
As the name implies, the quality-related variables loaded strongly
under the same factor and so have been grouped. It contains three
items: user-friendliness, with a factor loading of 0.826; organiza-
tional acceptance, with a factor loading of 0.731, and need for
quality, with a significance of 0.495. Technology and its devices
minimize the difficulty of executing any given task and thus does
DTs to the construction projects, the industry, and the entire eco-
system through replicating physical systems for ease of decision
making (Ford and Wolf 2020). The user-friendliness and flexibility
of digital technologies are motivators for their adoption in various
industries ( Gangwar et al. 2015 ).
In fact, organizational acceptance of technology is associated
with the confidentiality of data from other competitors. In the last
century, there was intense competition to enhance ICT, which
caused rapid changes and development of the relevant technologies
(Zou et al. 2017 ). As a result, many organizations had to adjust,
adapt, and accept the changes brought by ICT because it is an
indispensable tool for advancing development in the construction
industry and other fields. Although the construction industry is
often reported to lag in the adoption of technology among other
sectors (Morledge 2011 ), top management officers in construction
organizations must understand DTs ’ benefits for wide acceptance
in the industry.
Performance Driver
Performance drivers are the fourth extracted component. It consists
of three items, namely, better management and performance, with a
factor loading of 0.839; reliable data storage, with a factor loading
of 0.751 and technical advancement/trend, with a factor loading of
0.649. The application of DTs in the design, construction, or oper-
ation phases should enhance performance in terms of cost and time
reduction (Opoku et al. 2021 ). This is an important aspect because
digital technologies such as DTs could improve the time, cost, and
safety performance of construction projects. Large construction
projects consist of many interconnected parts, which may lead to
cost and time overruns if not well-managed (Leung and Olomolaiye
2010). However, through DTs, areas where performance-related
deficiencies that could lead to increased budgets and excessive time
can be easily identified, and addressed accordingly ( Kim and Kim
2020; Tao et al. 2019b ).

Policy Drivers
The fifth extracted component is policy drivers. It consists of three
items, namely, government policies, with a factor loading of 0.820;
client demand, with a factor loading of 0.779, and customer satis-
faction, with a significance of 0.741, respectively. When there is
legislation and policy on the usage of innovative technology, it will
improve client demand, and its usage brings satisfaction ( Agrawala
2019). The policy will enhance the implementation of DT usage
even though clients are not willing to adopt these technologies.
Interestingly, the government is the most influential client in the
construction industry (Fox and Skitmore 2007). According to Leng
et al. (2019), the benefits of the new technology should reach every
layer of society and actors in construction projects, which can be
spearheaded by the government. This implies that different govern-
mental bodies should initiate feasibility studies for utilizing DTs
within their sectors. In particular, they should investigate different
scenarios on how they can profit from projecting insights obtained
from relevant predictive twins into their governmental responsibil-
ities. Prior studies have proved that government policies play a
crucial role in adopting innovation in the construction industry
(Hazarika and Zhang 2019 ).
On the other hand, enforcement of the policies with fines for
defaulters has been an impetus through which industries in devel-
oping countries embraced any policies ( Adeniyi et al. 2020 ).
Although large enterprises working in developing nations are more
proactive in welcoming government policies, micro, small, and
medium-sized establishments are a bit laid back. Perhaps, large or-
ganizations know the implications of breaching government law
and policies on their reputation. The use of DTs on construction
projects could be linked with client demand because the adoption
of the technology would translate into substantial financial outlay
on the part of the organizations, which would be recouped from the
project owner at the end ( Kshetri 2021). Thus, a client with limited
funds to execute a project may be reluctant to request the use of any
digital technology on the project. However, a financially buoyant
client may not demand the technology if they are deficient in the
knowledge of the benefits of the project.
Conclusion, Recommendations, and Further Studies
Conclusion
A DT is the digital representation of a physical object with con-
nected data. This real-time data helps in managing buildings, im-
proving productivity, and decision making. A DT has various
capabilities depending on the enabling technologies used in devel-
oping DT technology. Proper orientation and awareness of these
levels or hierarchy of DTs and how it resolves issues and enhances
satisfaction in the industry are critical in its adoption. This study
examined the pull factors for using DT technology in the construc-
tion industry using descriptive and inferential statistics. The de-
scriptive statistics revealed that technological advancement/trend,
government policies, industry demand, availability of software,
reliability, customer satisfaction, and client demand were ranked
the topmost drivers of DT implementation. The results of the
Kruskal Wallis H-test indicated converging views of the groups
of professionals on 17 of the drivers, except for government pol-
icies. The divergent opinions of the respondents on government
policies are linked to the size of the respondent ’s establishment.
Factor analysis was conducted to group the drivers of DTs into
manageable sizes. The factor analysis was conducted to affirm the
critical drivers, components, and horn parallel analysis to ascertain
the number of factors to be extracted. The five components that the
factors were grouped into are innovation, operation, quality, perfor-
mance, and policy drivers. The findings of this study will provide a
reference point for researchers and construction organizations on
the driving forces that bring about the implementation of DTs in
a business context and the construction industry. The study gave
insight into the pull factors of DTs in the construction industry.
Recommendations
Some recommendations from the study to improve the adoption of
DTs in the construction industry are as follows:
 Emphasis should be made by the appropriate bodies such as
private clients, construction developers, professionals, and dif-
ferent stakeholders in the construction industry on the function-
alities and benefits derived from using DTs in construction, built
assets, and service firms, such as safety and reliability in dis-
seminating stored information, which have been examined in
this study, so that it will spur the acceptance of stakeholders.
 Firms and organizations must be ready to train their staff and
workers on digital technology skills such as DTs because such
basic operation skills have been stated as one of the drivers of
DTs to be able to solve challenges faced in the construction
industry.
 Government should enact a policy on the use of digital tech-
nologies such as BIM, which was enforced in the UK for
public projects. This will facilitate the use of these technologies.
Also, the purchase of these technologies should be subsidized
for firms/organizations so clients and other stakeholders can
demand these technologies.
 The use of technologies must be based on business value and
technological drive. Organizations that adopt these technologies
must be satisfied, which enhances the implementation. The
choice of the capabilities of the DT should be based on the
challenges faced by the organizations and their size. This will
further enhance the productivity and performance of companies
in executing their project.
Limitations and Areas of Further Study
The study was limited to Lagos state, Nigeria, but the findings can
be applied to other states of the federation, and to developing coun-
tries where development is similar. The limitation of the study is
that a qualitative study was not carried out to broaden the percep-
tion of professionals. A survey of construction professionals was
mainly used to obtain opinions on drivers of DTs in this study.
Although this approach can be considered suitable to achieve the
aim of the study, there may be other drivers that were not captured
in the research instrument. Therefore, future studies can explore
other drivers of DTs via focus groups or Delphi surveys from
