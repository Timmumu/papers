 A B S T R A C T
Decentralized federated learning (DFL) enables peer-to-peer training, but hyperparameter optimization (HPO) 
in serverless environments remains underexplored. This paper presents a DFL framework featuring a one-
shot cooperative HPO mechanism based on Sequential Model-based Algorithm Configuration (SMAC). The 
architecture employs a Digital Twin (DT) orchestration layer to manage edge nodes and network topologies 
without centralizing the learning process. Nodes perform independent local SMAC tuning followed by 
performance-weighted aggregation to derive a global hyperparameter configuration. Evaluated on worker-
safety and MNIST datasets using a Raspberry Pi testbed, our approach significantly outperforms fixed settings 
and Random Search across IID and non-IID conditions (p<0.01p < 0.01 p<0.01). Results demonstrate faster 
convergence and higher accuracy; specifically, convergence rounds dropped from 42 to 18 for worker safety 
and from 8 to 3 for MNIST. In representative IID mesh experiments, accuracy rose from 92.4% to 95.3%, and 
MAE improved from 0.058 to 0.042 compared to fixed hyperparameter settings. These findings validate the 
efficiency of SMAC-based HPO for decentralized, edge-oriented environments.
1. Introduction
Ensuring worker safety is a critical concern in modern industrial en-
vironments such as construction sites, manufacturing facilities, and lo-
gistics hubs. Predictive modeling can help proactively detect hazardous 
conditions and prevent accidents, but achieving this in real-time and in 
the field remains technically challenging. Traditional centralized ma-
chine learning approaches are ill-suited for such deployments because 
of latency, bandwidth restrictions, and privacy issues while sending 
sensitive data from edge devices to cloud servers. FL offers a promis-
ing alternative by enabling multiple clients to collaboratively train 
a shared model without exchanging raw data [1,2]. However, most 
FL frameworks follow a centralized architecture, relying on a server 
to coordinate communication and aggregation. This CFL paradigm 
introduces bottlenecks related to communication costs, vulnerability 
to single points of failure, and limited scalability [3–5]. To address 
these issues, DFL has emerged as a more resilient alternative, where 
peer nodes train and exchange model updates directly, eliminating the 
requirement for a central server [6,7]. DFL is especially attractive in 
use cases involving data heterogeneity, such as healthcare [8], UAV 
networks [9], and edge-based industrial systems [10–12].

While DFL addresses key infrastructure-level challenges, it presents 
new ones at the optimization level, particularly in hyperparameter 
optimization (HPO). In centralized settings, HPO benefits from global 
visibility; in DFL, each node holds only a small, possibly non-IID subset 
of data and has limited computational power. Traditional HPO meth-
ods, such as grid search or black-box optimization, become impractical 
due to their resource demands and reliance on repeated global evalu-
ation cycles [13]. Existing federated HPO methods, such as FedEx and 
FLoRa, remain tailored to CFL settings and do not generalize well to de-
centralized topologies [14]. Recent research, such as BrainTorrent [15], 
BlockDFL [16], DFedSAM-MGS [17], and Fedstellar [18], has explored 
DFL infrastructures. However, HPO in fully decentralized FL remains 
an underexplored area with only a few recent contributions [19,20]. 
Notably, while consensus-driven HPO works like DFed-HPO [20] have 
utilized Bayesian methods, they often rely on iterative peer-voting 
cycles that increase communication complexity on edge hardware.
To manage the complexity of decentralized training across con-
strained nodes, we integrate DT technology. Initially proposed by 
Grieves in 2002 [21], DTs offer synchronized digital replicas of physical 
systems for real-time control [22]. In our framework, the DT serves as

2. Related work and background
Hyperparameters are settings used to control the training process of 
a machine learning model. Unlike model parameters, which are learned 
during training, hyperparameters are set before the training process 
begins and can significantly impact the performance of the model.
2.1. HPO in machine learning and federated learning
HPO is foundational for improving the performance of machine 
learning models, particularly in scenarios involving complex architec-
tures or sensitive data distributions. Classical techniques such as grid 
search and random search [25,26] are easy to implement but inefficient 
in high-dimensional or continuous hyperparameter spaces. Bayesian 
optimization has gained prominence as a more intelligent approach 
that models the objective function and guides the search process based 
on prior evaluations [27]. In federated learning (FL), HPO introduces 
new challenges. Early studies, such as those by Hsu et al. [28] and 
Li et al. [2], explored tuning learning rates and local epochs us-
ing synthetic or proxy datasets on centralized servers. However, this

introduces discrepancies between validation and real-world data, par-
ticularly under non-IID conditions. More advanced HPO methods have 
been proposed to optimize FL-specific challenges. FedEx [29] utilizes 
Bayesian optimization and Thompson sampling to coordinate hyperpa-
rameter selection across multiple clients, whereas FedTune [30] intro-
duces an adaptive controller for adjusting local epoch and batch sizes. 
FLoRA [31] offers a single-shot optimization method that pre-selects 
hyperparameters before model training, reducing communication over-
head. Additionally, reinforcement learning–based approaches, such as 
Auto-FedRL [32] and PSO-based schemes like HPO-PSO [33], show 
promise; however, all still rely on centralized orchestration for global 
coordination. These approaches are either impractical or insufficient 
for fully decentralized environments, where no central server exists to 
direct HPO decisions.
2.2. Decentralized federated learning architectures
By allowing P2P communication between clients, DFL removes 
the requirement for a central server. This improves system resilience 
and privacy while reducing communication bottlenecks. Applications 
in healthcare [7], mobile edge computing [34], UAV networks [35], 
and Internet-of-Vehicles [36] have demonstrated the feasibility of DFL 
under constrained and heterogeneous conditions. To address data and 
device diversity, Kang et al. [37] introduced blockchain-assisted archi-
tectures that ensure privacy-preserving updates, while Li et al. extended 
this to scalable, incentive-driven systems (e.g., BLADE-FL [9]). Chen 
et al. [38] proposed mobility-aware learning using multi-agent rein-
forcement strategies for DFL in vehicular networks, and Li et al. [39] 
introduced DFedADMM to reduce model inconsistency across asyn-
chronous clients. Recent works, such as Fedstellar [40], present plat-
forms that support both decentralized and semi-centralized modes, 
introducing features like secure aggregation and asynchronous update 
scheduling. Yet, these DFL frameworks primarily focus on communi-
cation efficiency, model convergence, or data heterogeneity, leaving 
hyperparameter tuning either unsolved or rigidly pre-defined. Our 
proposed approach complements these efforts by embedding an HPO 
mechanism that adapts to local data dynamics without requiring cen-
tralized oversight.
2.3. HPO in decentralized federated learning
HPO within DFL remains underexplored due to the challenges posed 
by serverless architectures and non-IID data. Recent studies have begun 
addressing these limitations. Wang et al. [41] introduced decentralized 
bilevel optimization methods focusing on first-order approaches; their 
work primarily addresses optimization theory rather than direct hyper-
parameter tuning in federated settings, limiting its direct applicability 
to DFL-specific HPO. Khan et al. [42] introduced a bilevel HPO strategy 
for DFL, though it primarily targeted neural architecture search in static 
ring topologies. Most recently, Khan et al. [43] proposed DFed-HPO, 
which utilizes HyperOpt with the Adaptive Tree-structured Parzen 
Estimator (ATPE) for HPO on a Raspberry Pi testbed. Their approach 
relies on a consensus-driven mechanism where nodes iteratively vote 
on hyperparameter sets to reach a peer agreement. While DFed-HPO 
proves that Bayesian-based HPO is feasible in decentralized settings, it 
relies on iterative consensus, which can introduce significant communi-
cation overhead as the number of nodes or the complexity of the search 
space increases. Furthermore, TPE-based methods like the ATPE model 
the density of good vs bad configurations, which can be less efficient 
than the Random Forest surrogate models used in SMAC when handling 
highly non-linear, noisy objective functions on constrained hardware. 
Our work distinguishes itself by integrating an SMAC-based tuner 
within a one-shot cooperative aggregation framework. Unlike iterative 
consensus, our method performs a single, performance-weighted propa-
gation of local incumbents, drastically reducing communication cycles. 
Additionally, we integrate a DT orchestration layer to provide real-time 
observability into the decentralized tuning process, a capability absent 
in current P2P HPO frameworks.
Among Bayesian HPO strategies, TPE/ATPE-based methods model 
the density of promising versus non-promising configurations and have 
already shown feasibility in decentralized edge settings, as reflected 
by recent consensus-based DFL-HPO studies. In contrast, our method 
adopts SMAC, whose Random Forest surrogate is better suited to the 
mixed continuous/discrete/categorical search spaces and low-budget, 
noisy evaluations considered in this work. We therefore position our 
contribution not as the first Bayesian HPO method in DFL, but as a 
communication-efficient one-shot SMAC-based alternative tailored to 
resource-constrained peer-to-peer learning. Beyond DFL-HPO-specific 
studies, recent edge-AI works have also addressed demanding applica-
tion domains such as remote-sensing segmentation [44], structural ob-
ject detection [45], industrial robot and machinery fault diagnosis [46,
47], and pavement crack detection [48], highlighting the growing need 
for efficient on-device optimization under heterogeneous sensing and 
deployment conditions.
2.4. Digital twin–enabled occupational risk assessment
DT systems have progressed from static replicas to real-time orches-
trators that fuse IoT sensing, human behavior modeling, and environ-
mental monitoring. In construction and industrial settings, early work 
by Zhao et al. [49], Akanmu et al. [50], and Somanath et al. [51] 
couples DTs with wearable sensors and augmented-reality feedback to 
track posture and site conditions. Fang et al. [52] and Sacks et al. [53] 
extend this line by introducing DT-based simulations for behavioral-risk 
assessment and closed-loop safety control. Recent research has begun to 
marry DTs with distributed learning paradigms. Earlier, Sun et al. [54] 
integrated adaptive federated learning with DTs for industrial IoT, 
showing that DT-guided local updates accelerate convergence under 
device heterogeneity. Kamalakannan et al. [55] propose DTFL-DF, a DT 
architecture powered by federated learning and decision forests to pre-
dict and mitigate underground-mine fire accidents without sharing raw 
sensor data. Tang et al. [56] develop a DT-enabled, communication-
efficient federated learning scheme for vehicular collision warning 
systems, achieving faster convergence and reduced bandwidth con-
sumption. Although these studies highlight the synergy between DTs 
and federated analytics, most still rely on a coordinating server or 
assume homogeneous resources. In contrast, our work tightly couples 
DT technology with a fully decentralized cooperative HPO pipeline 
and collaborative learning loop. The DT layer virtualizes each edge 
node, visualizes the SMAC-based HPO lifecycle, and provides real-time 
inspection of model configuration and inference results, an essen-
tial capability for safety systems, while preserving the privacy-centric, 
serverless nature of DFL.
2.5. Research gaps
Despite these advancements, a clear gap exists for HPO frameworks 
that are both computationally efficient and operationally transpar-
ent. Current DFL-HPO methods either use iterative consensus logic 
(e.g., DFed-HPO), which scales poorly in high-latency edge networks, 
or assume static configurations. Moreover, while Bayesian optimiza-
tion using TPE has been explored, the use of SMAC, which is better 
suited for the mixed (categorical and continuous) search spaces found 
in worker-safety models, has not been applied to fully decentralized 
federated loops. Finally, most studies focus purely on the optimization 
logic and neglect the runtime management and monitoring required for 
industrial AIoT deployments. Our proposed framework fills these gaps 
by combining SMAC-based local tuning with a lightweight one-shot 
aggregation rule, all while leveraging DT technology for lifecycle ob-
servability. Table  1 summarizes the notable existing works and research 
gaps addressed in comparison to our work.

3.2. Proposed DFL-HPO mechanism
To address the challenges of statistical and system heterogeneity 
in worker-safety environments, we propose a fully decentralized HPO 
framework. Our approach prioritizes a node-oriented tuning phase fol-
lowed by a performance-centric weighting mechanism. Fig.  1 illustrates 
the overall architecture.
While Bayesian Optimization (BO) is a standard choice for HPO, we 
specifically utilize SMAC (Sequential Model-based Algorithm Configu-
ration) for three critical reasons:
1. Handling Mixed Search Spaces: Unlike Gaussian Process (GP)-
based BO, which struggles with non-continuous variables, SMAC 
uses a Random Forest surrogate that natively handles the mixed-
type search spaces (categorical, discrete, and continuous) typical 
of deep learning architectures.
2. Scalability: GPs scale cubically with the number of trials (𝑂(𝑛3)), 
making them computationally expensive for edge nodes. SMAC’s 
surrogate scales much more efficiently, allowing for higher trial 
budgets on resource-constrained Raspberry Pi devices.
3. Robustness to Noisy Landscapes: Worker-safety sensor data is in-
herently noisy. SMAC is more robust to high-variance validation 
losses than Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) or GP-based 
methods.
Unlike consensus-based DFL-HPO approaches (e.g., [43]) that re-
quire iterative cycles of evaluation and voting to reach an agreement, 
our method employs a one-shot cooperative aggregation. This mini-
mizes the communication overhead on AIoT devices while ensuring that 
the final global configuration is informed by the most successful local 
training landscapes.
3.2.1. Hyperparameter optimization phase
We chose SMAC specifically for DFL due to its unique ability to han-
dle high-dimensional, mixed-type search spaces (categorical, discrete, 
and continuous), which are common in deep learning architectures. 
Unlike Gaussian Process-based Bayesian Optimization, SMAC uses a 
Random Forest surrogate that scales efficiently with the number of 
trials and is robust to noisy objective functions often encountered on 
resource-constrained edge devices such as Raspberry Pis.
The HPO phase is designed to be communication-efficient, utilizing 
a one-shot cooperative approach. This process is divided into local 
search, global propagation, and decentralized aggregation.

3.3. Digital twin orchestration and observability plane
Our framework utilizes a DT layer as a control and observability 
plane, while the DFL nodes constitute the data plane. Unlike a cen-
tralized federated learning server, the DT does not collect raw training 
data, execute local model training, or perform decentralized weight 
aggregation. Instead, it virtualizes the lifecycle of the edge nodes and 
provides system-level services required to deploy, monitor, and manage 
decentralized HPO and DFL in a practical AIoT environment.
Fig.  2 presents the functional mapping of the DT orchestration 
plane. The DT first supports device registration, where edge devices are 
enrolled using network descriptors such as IP address and port. It then 
provides topology configuration, enabling the peer-to-peer communi-
cation structure to be organized as mesh or ring before decentralized 
execution begins. Through device virtualization, the DT maintains a 
digital representation of each node, including status and device infor-
mation, allowing the system to monitor node availability and runtime 
conditions in real time.

The DT also supports model management and task generation. In 
this stage, the model structure and execution settings for both DFL 
and HPO are configured before runtime, including the selected dataset, 
number of communication rounds, local epochs, and task type. In 
addition, the DT handles data management only at the metadata level, 
such as device descriptors, performance logs, and exported monitoring 
records. This means that the DT manages lightweight control-plane 
information rather than raw sensor data or model parameters.
During runtime, the DT provides device monitoring and device 
control. The monitoring function tracks node connectivity and run-
time performance indicators, while the control function enables the 
operator to activate or deactivate devices and trigger HPO or DFL-
related processes. Finally, the result visualization module displays the 
outcomes of decentralized execution, including local/global hyperpa-
rameter configurations, device-level training performance, and worker-
safety inference results. This mapping clarifies that the DT is not a 
conceptual overlay, but a concrete orchestration layer that improves co-
ordination, traceability, and operational transparency in decentralized 
edge learning.
From the system-overhead perspective, the DT introduces a signifi-
cantly lighter burden than a centralized training server because it does 
not aggregate model updates or process the full learning workload. Its 
role is restricted to control-plane signaling, metadata management, and 
visualization. Therefore, the additional overhead of the DT is primarily 
associated with registration, topology setup, telemetry handling, and 
interface rendering, while the computationally intensive training and 
inference tasks remain on the edge devices. The quantitative hard-
ware and communication implications of this design are discussed in 
Section 5.3.

4.2. Implementation results
This subsection describes the implementation results of the HPO 
and occupational risk assessment on DT, which serves all operations 
to ensure a smooth process through its virtualization and visualization 
capabilities.
4.2.1. Hyperparameter optimization and observability via DT
In fully decentralized environments, the absence of a central server 
can lead to black-box training where node-level failures or suboptimal 
configurations go unnoticed. We incorporate a DT layer to serve as a 
secure Orchestration and Observability Plane. It is crucial to distinguish 
this from a central server: the DT does not process raw data or perform 
model aggregation; rather, it virtualizes the lifecycle management of 
edge nodes to provide high-level administrative control.
As shown in Fig.  4, the DT platform provides a web-based interface 
for node registration and HPO monitoring. Once the user defines the 
network topology (Mesh or Ring), the DT acts as a control-plane trigger 
to initiate the local SMAC tuning.
To preserve the decentralized integrity of the system, the DT-led 
HPO process is a one-shot task. The DT monitors the logs (as shown 
in Fig.  5) as the nodes communicate peer-to-peer to aggregate their 
hyperparameter incumbents. This architecture ensures that the com-
putational and privacy-centric benefits of DFL are maintained, while 
the DT provides the transparency required for safety-critical industrial 
deployments.
4.3. Occupational risk assessment
After completing the DFL, the trained model, which has been en-
hanced by aggregating knowledge from neighboring sources, is used 
to predict outcomes using unseen data from IoT devices equipped 
with sensors. To facilitate the prediction process for worker safety, DT 
creates a virtual replica of each worker along with a corresponding 
monitoring panel to display the safety index. The safety index ranges 
from 1 to 10, where a score of 1 to 4 indicates the safest conditions, 
5 to 7 signifies caution, and 8 to 10 represents dangerous conditions. 
On the DT interface, the worker’s safety index is visually represented 
with colors ranging from green (safe) to red (danger). Fig.  6 illustrates 
the user interface for occupational risk assessment on the DT. The 
performance of the model on each node is visualized on DT for tracking 
and evaluation. On the right side of the UI, when the ‘‘Start Inference’’ 
button is clicked, the monitoring results for the safety of each worker 
become visible.
Fig.  7 depicts the step-by-step inference process used on the node 
to enable real-time occupational risk assessment. In this setup, sensor 
data from outdoor construction sites is gathered every 15 s. This data 
includes structural metrics (strain, load, inclinometer), environmental 
factors (wind, temperature, precipitation), and worker-specific informa-
tion (heart rate, GPS location). It is transmitted via EdgeX microservices 
and stored locally. After preprocessing, the test data is processed by 
the inference model on each edge device. The model generates a 
Safety Index that indicates each worker’s predicted safety level in 
near real-time. This approach allows continuous, low-latency safety 
monitoring and prediction directly at the site, without relying on cen-
tralized processing, making it ideal for dynamic outdoor construction 
environments.

the overall global statistics of the dataset. As a result, each node 
observed a distinct proportion of low-, medium-, and high-risk samples, 
which directly altered the local validation landscape explored during 
SMAC tuning and influenced the subsequent convergence behavior of 
decentralized training.

reaches or exceeds a specified threshold (e.g., 90%). If the threshold is 
never reached, the metric is recorded as undefined (NaN).

only a compact metadata tuple comprising the incumbent hyperpa-

exchange remains substantially lighter than repeated model transmis-

traffic.

proposed framework.

Convergence and topology trade-offs. The performance differences ob-
served between the Mesh and Ring topologies can be further contextu-
alized through the lens of graph theory and consensus optimization. In 
decentralized learning, the convergence rate is significantly influenced 
by the spectral gap (𝜆2) of the communication graph, where a larger 
gap indicates better connectivity and faster information propagation. As 
shown in our results, the Mesh topology, possessing a superior spectral 
gap compared to the Ring, reached target accuracy in significantly 
fewer rounds. While more sophisticated weight-balancing schemes like 
Metropolis–Hastings or push-sum gossip could potentially accelerate 
convergence further, we opted for uniform neighbor averaging. This 
choice was a deliberate design decision to minimize the CPU and mem-
ory overhead on the Raspberry Pi nodes, which were already tasked 
with running the Orchestrator and the SMAC-based HPO phase. These 
results provide an empirical baseline for the DFL-HPO framework, and 
exploring advanced variance-reduction optimizers remains a promising 
avenue for future high-performance edge deployments.
Trade-off between HPO overhead and convergence gain. A practical ques-
tion is whether the additional cost of running local SMAC instances 
on resource-constrained edge devices is justified by the resulting per-
formance gain. Our results indicate that the answer depends on the 
task complexity and deployment objective. On the Raspberry Pi 4 
testbed, the one-shot HPO phase required approximately 3–9 min for 
the Worker Safety dataset and 8–20 min for MNIST, while the sub-
sequent 50-round decentralized training phase required approximately 
20–35 min and 35–60 min, respectively. Peak memory usage remained 
within the 4 GB device limit, ranging from 1.6–1.9 GB for the Safety 
task and 2.5–2.9 GB for MNIST. In return for this one-time tuning cost, 
the proposed method achieved consistently better predictive perfor-
mance and faster convergence than fixed hyperparameter settings and 
standard Random Search, while exchanging only lightweight metadata 
during HPO. For example, in the representative mesh results reported in 
Table  6, the proposed method reduced the rounds to target convergence 
from 42 to 18 for the Worker Safety task and from 8 to 3 for MNIST, 
while also improving MAE and final accuracy. These results show 
that the front-loaded HPO cost is particularly favorable when rapid 
convergence, stable training, or repeated deployment under similar 
operating conditions is required.
Statistical validation. To determine if the performance improvements 
provided by the SMAC-based HPO are statistically significant, a one-
tailed paired sample 𝑡-test was conducted on the final MAE results of 
the nodes (𝑁 = 6 ). The null hypothesis 𝐻0 stated that there is no 
difference in mean error between the fixed-HP and HPO-enabled DFL. 
For the Worker Safety dataset, the results yielded 𝑡(5) = 4 .12 and a 
𝑝-value of 0.0046. Since 𝑝 < 0.01, we reject 𝐻0 and conclude with 99% 
confidence that the proposed cooperative HPO significantly reduces 
prediction error compared to standard decentralized learning.
Robustness and variance quantification. To ensure the reproducibility 
and stability of the proposed framework, all experiments, including 
the SMAC-based HPO phase and the subsequent decentralized training, 
were repeated over five independent runs with different random seeds. 
The results presented in Figs.  9–16 depict the mean performance, while 
the shaded regions (or error bars) represent the standard deviation (𝜎) 
across these runs and the participating nodes. This approach accounts 
for the inherent stochasticity in the SMAC surrogate modeling, local 
SGD updates, and the random sampling associated with non-IID data 
distributions.

5.4. Ablation study
To evaluate the contribution of HPO to DFL performance, we 
conducted an ablation study comparing training outcomes with and 
without HPO across both image classification (MNIST) and regression 
(Worker Safety) tasks, under various network topologies (mesh and 
ring) and data distributions (IID and non-IID). The results consistently 
demonstrate that integrating local HPO substantially enhances model 
convergence speed, prediction accuracy, and training stability.
In the Worker Safety regression task, models trained with HPO 
achieved lower mean absolute error (MAE) across all nodes and com-
munication rounds. Under IID mesh settings, HPO led to smoother 
and faster convergence, Figs.  9(a,b), lower final per-node MAEs, Fig. 
9(c), and significantly improved learning stability, as evidenced by 
lower AUC metrics, Fig.  9(d). In the more challenging non-IID mesh 
scenario, the benefits were even more pronounced: HPO not only 
maintained its performance lead across nodes Figs.  10(a,c), but also 
accelerated early-stage learning Fig.  10(b) and produced more stable 
convergence patterns Fig.  10(d). These gains also persisted under a ring 
topology. In both IID and non-IID cases (Figs.  11, 12), HPO consis-
tently reduced MAEs, improved inter-node consistency, and minimized 
training variance, despite the sequential communication limitations 
of ring structures. This performance gap between topologies aligns 
with theoretical expectations regarding the spectral gap of the network 
graph; the higher connectivity of the mesh topology facilitates faster 
information propagation, which the SMAC-based HPO leverages to 
achieve quicker consensus.
The MNIST classification task further supports the effectiveness of 
HPO in DFL. Under IID mesh conditions, models using HPO achieved 
higher average and per-node accuracy Figs.  13(a,b), reached 90% 
accuracy more quickly Fig.  13(c), and showed higher AUC values, 
indicating stable learning Fig.  13(d). When trained on non-IID data, 
HPO led to striking improvements: accuracy gains were substantial 
across nodes Fig.  14(b), convergence was accelerated Fig.  14(c), and 
learning remained stable despite data heterogeneity Fig.  14(d). Similar 
advantages were observed in the ring topology, where HPO improved 
final accuracy and convergence times under both IID and non-IID 
distributions (Figs.  15, 16).
Overall, this ablation confirms that including a local SMAC-based 
HPO module results in significant improvements across various learning 
tasks, topologies, and data distributions. Importantly, the computa-
tional overhead of the one-shot HPO phase (averaging 6-16 min as 
shown in Table  7) is offset by faster convergence, reducing the total 
time-to-target compared to fixed hyperparameters. The consistent re-
duction in error, faster convergence, and enhanced learning stability 
across all scenarios underscore the critical role of hyperparameter 
optimization in fully decentralized federated learning systems.
5.5. Practical implications of results
The experimental results presented in this work have direct and 
significant implications for deploying DFL systems in real-world edge 
environments, particularly in industrial safety-critical domains. The 
consistent performance gains observed across diverse datasets, network 
topologies, and data distributions demonstrate that integrating local 
HPO with decentralized learning pipelines is not merely a theoretical 
improvement; it is essential for ensuring model robustness, scalability, 
and responsiveness under real-world constraints.
First, the demonstrated reduction in prediction error and faster 
convergence using local HPO (via SMAC) means that on-device AI 
models can reach higher levels of accuracy in fewer communication

rounds. This directly translates to lower training latency, reduced en-
ergy consumption, and improved responsiveness, which are critical in 
industrial contexts where edge devices (e.g., wearables, sensors, embed-
ded systems) operate under strict power and processing constraints. Our 
hardware benchmarking confirms that this is achievable on Raspberry 
Pi 4 nodes, with peak memory usage remaining below 2.9 GB, leaving 
ample headroom for the underlying OS and sensor drivers.
Second, the system’s ability to maintain accuracy and stability under 
non-IID conditions and heterogeneous nodes reflects its robustness 
to real-world deployment scenarios, where uniform data distribution 
and synchronized resources are often unrealistic. In the context of 
occupational risk assessment, for example, different edge devices might 
observe varied working conditions, user behavior, and sensing quality. 
Our framework effectively adapts to such heterogeneity, making it suit-
able for decentralized safety analytics across large, diverse workforces 
without requiring centralized retraining or manual tuning.
Third, the performance benefits across both mesh and ring topolo-
gies indicate that the proposed approach is topology-agnostic, offering 
deployment flexibility for a wide range of network infrastructures 
from ad hoc mobile environments to structured sensor grids. This is 
particularly important in domains such as construction, logistics, or 
disaster response, where network connectivity and structure can change 
dynamically.
The integration of Orchestrator-driven virtualization significantly 
improves the observability and control of model training, hyperpa-
rameter tuning, and inference pipelines. The Orchestrator effectively 
separates the control plane from the data plane, ensuring that the 
management of the DT does not disrupt the critical inference cy-
cles necessary for safety in high-stakes environments. This feature not 
only provides real-time feedback to operators and safety managers but 
also establishes a foundation for automated orchestration, debugging, 
and compliance monitoring—elements that are increasingly vital in 
regulated industrial and healthcare settings.
In summary, our findings highlight that deploying SMAC-driven 
HPO within DFL systems offers a practical, scalable, and privacy-
preserving solution for real-time edge AI applications, particularly in 
environments where safety, accuracy, and reliability are paramount.
Implications for larger-scale edge-AI tasks. Although the current eval-
uation focuses on MNIST classification and worker-safety regression, 
the proposed framework is also relevant to larger-scale edge-AI work-
loads such as cross-domain remote-sensing segmentation, structural 
object detection, and crack detection/segmentation. Compared with the 
tasks studied in this paper, such applications typically involve denser 
outputs, larger model backbones, and higher communication and mem-
ory demands. Therefore, extending the present DFL-HPO framework 
to those settings would likely require budget-aware search spaces, 
model compression, hierarchical or sparse communication, and task-
specific optimization objectives. We view these application domains 
as important next-step testbeds for assessing the scalability of decen-
tralized hyperparameter optimization beyond lightweight classification 
and tabular regression.
Practical limitation of overhead analysis. Although the current study 
quantifies wall-clock time, memory footprint, and communication pay-
load, we did not directly instrument energy consumption or fine-
grained CPU utilization on the edge devices. Therefore, our feasibility 
discussion is based on measured execution time, peak memory usage, 
and relative communication burden rather than full power profiling. A 
more detailed energy-aware analysis, including per-process CPU usage 
and battery impact under continuous operation, remains an important 
direction for future work.
Adaptivity and concept drift mitigation. A potential limitation of the 
current one-shot HPO approach is its sensitivity to concept drift, partic-
ularly in dynamic industrial environments where sensor distributions 
may shift due to seasonal changes or new operational protocols. To 
ensure long-term model resilience, the Orchestrator can be configured 
to perform drift-triggered re-tuning. By leveraging the DT’s real-time 
observability, the system can monitor the variance in local gradients 
and MAE. If performance degrades below a set reliability threshold, the 
Orchestrator initiates a localized HPO cycle using a sliding window of 
recent data. Our hardware analysis (Section 5.3) demonstrates that such 
retuning cycles would require only a brief 6-minute computational win-
dow, making dynamic adaptation a practical, low-overhead addition to 
the DFL pipeline.
6. Conclusions
Enhancing model performance in fully DFL environments remains 
a significant challenge due to the absence of a central server, non-IID 
data distributions, and heterogeneous device capabilities. This paper 
presents a robust HPO mechanism tailored for DFL, leveraging local 
SMAC-based tuners and a performance-weighted hyperparameter ag-
gregation strategy. Our decentralized and cooperative HPO pipeline 
enables each node to autonomously adapt to its unique data and 
resource profile while still participating in a collaborative learning 
process without having a central server. By integrating this mechanism 
into a topology-flexible DFL framework and validating it on both public 
MNIST and private Worker Safety datasets using a Raspberry Pi testbed, 
we demonstrate substantial improvements in predictive accuracy, con-
vergence speed, and learning stability. These benefits persist across 
both mesh and ring topologies, as well as under both IID and non-
IID data conditions, underscoring the generalizability and robustness 
of our approach. Moreover, coupling this HPO pipeline with a DT layer 
provides real-time observability, configurability, and control, which 
are key features for deploying dependable AI at the edge in safety-
critical scenarios. Our findings strongly suggest that decentralized HPO 
is not just beneficial but essential for achieving scalable and resilient 
performance in practical on-device AI networks. As such, the presented 
method lays the foundational work for DFL-HPO systems that are both 
resource-aware and performance-optimized.
Future research will focus on several key areas motivated by our 
experimental findings. First, we plan to implement dynamic hyper-
parameter scheduling to address potential concept drift. Our bench-
marking results, which show that periodic re-tuning completes in less 
than 7 min, confirm that adaptive HPO is feasible for long-term de-
ployments. Second, we aim to investigate advanced consensus mech-
anisms, such as Metropolis–Hastings weights, to improve convergence 
in ring topologies, which currently take longer than in mesh configu-
rations (as detailed in Table  7). Furthermore, we intend to extend the 
SMAC tuner toward multi-objective optimization, balancing accuracy 
against latency and energy consumption to better align with real-world 
AIoT constraints. Finally, we will explore Byzantine-robust aggrega-
tion strategies, such as loss-clipping, to enhance system reliability 
in open-membership peer-to-peer environments beyond our current 
semi-trusted industrial setting.
