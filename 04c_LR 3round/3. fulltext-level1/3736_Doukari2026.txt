 a b s t r a c t
Achieving the EU’s energy and decarbonisation goals requires more eﬃcient  management of building renovation 
projects. Whilst digital twin (DT) technology is increasingly adopted across the built environment, most existing 
research and practice focus on its application during the operational phase of built assets such as energy monitor-
ing, space and occupancy management, and predictive maintenance. However, there remains a gap in leveraging 
DT potential in renovation projects, supporting multi-domain performance tracking. This study presents a pilot 
implementation and preliminary evaluation of a Renovation Digital Twin (RDT) platform tailored to the spe-
ciﬁc challenges of renovation projects. The RDT integrates real-time data and dashboards to monitor 13 Key 
Performance Indicators (KPIs) across ﬁve domains: cost, schedule, quality, safety, and environment. Designed 
with GDPR compliance and usability in mind, the platform aims to support Architecture, Engineering, Construc-
tion, and Operations (AECO) stakeholders by improving transparency, early issue detection, and decision-making 
throughout project delivery. The RDT was evaluated using a quantitative questionnaire based on the Technol-
ogy Acceptance Model (TAM) involving 102 industry professionals to assess perceived usefulness, ease of use, 
and overall user acceptance. Results demonstrated high user conﬁdence, with Attitude Toward Use (RII = 0.894) 
ranking highest amongst TAM constructs. Deployment on the French RINNO demonstration site, achieving 73% 
emissions reduction but facing economic viability challenges, demonstrates the platform’s capacity to surface 
complex multi-domain trade-oﬀs. Whilst minor usability improvements were identiﬁed, initial ﬁndings provide 
evidence of the platform’s viability and user acceptance, though broader validation across diverse renovation 
contexts is required. This research contributes an initial, stakeholder-informed DT framework for renovation, 
advancing the role of digital technologies in improving coordination, monitoring, and performance in occupied 
retroﬁt environments.
1.  Introduction
The construction industry plays an essential role in global economic 
growth and the development of urban environments. However, the in-
dustry is considered the world’s largest consumer of natural resources 
and a major contributor to environmental challenges such as resource 
depletion, carbon emissions, and energy use [1]. According to the 2019 
Global Status Report for Buildings and Construction, the sector is respon-
sible for 36% of ﬁnal energy use and approximately 40% of energy- and 
process-related CO2 emissions [2]. By 2045, the global urban popula-
tion is projected to exceed six billion, potentially resulting in increased 
construction activities to meet housing and infrastructure demands [3]. 
In response to these environmental pressures, governments worldwide 
have implemented ambitious decarbonisation policies. The EU has es-
tablished the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD), set-
ting energy savings targets for 2030 and 2033 [4], whilst the UK has

integrated socio-technical approaches that simultaneously address tech-

diverse stakeholders with often conﬂicting motivations.
Digital twin (DT) technology oﬀers a promising approach to over-
coming these multi-dimensional barriers, provided it can eﬀectively 
bridge the gap between technological capability and practical adoption. 
A DT represents an up-to-date virtual representation of a physical en-
tity and its functional properties, leveraging dynamic data streaming 
and analytics capabilities to support informed decision-making [9,10]. 
DT applications in construction include optimised resource manage-
ment through real-time monitoring [11]; enhanced stakeholder collab-
oration via shared digital platforms [12]; accelerated project delivery 
through virtual simulations and predictive analytics [13]; comprehen-
sive progress monitoring using 4D BIM integration [14]; improved qual-
ity control and safety management [15,16]; and enhanced sustainability 
monitoring of carbon emissions and supply chains [17,18]. These capa-
bilities position DT as particularly valuable for managing the complex, 
multi-domain aspects essential for successful renovation projects.
Despite this potential, applying DT technology in renovation con-
texts presents distinct challenges compared to new-build construction. 
New-build projects oﬀer greater ﬂexibility for technology integration 
without occupancy disruptions, whilst renovation projects must accom-
modate existing building constraints and occupied environments, signif-
icantly complicating DT implementation [19]. Current research demon-
strates limited eﬀective application of DT in renovation settings, with 
existing tools often inadequately adapted from new-build contexts [20]. 
Furthermore, most DT implementations focus on single-domain moni-
toring rather than the comprehensive, multi-domain approach required 
for managing renovation complexity, particularly during the onsite 
retroﬁtting phase where coordination challenges are most acute. This 
creates a critical research gap: while DT technology holds promise for 
addressing renovation barriers, its application for multi-domain perfor-
mance monitoring during onsite renovation, including both technical 
implementation and user acceptance, remains underexplored.
This paper addresses this gap through a pilot study investigating the 
development, deployment, and evaluation of a Renovation Digital Twin 
(RDT) that enables multi-domain performance monitoring and deliv-
ers real-time feedback to project stakeholders. The research examines 
whether integrated DT platforms can eﬀectively bridge technological 
capability with practical adoption in renovation contexts, evaluating 
both technical viability and user acceptance factors that inﬂuence tech-
nology diﬀusion. Using Design Science Research (DSR) methodology, 
the study grounds design requirements in stakeholder needs and eval-
uates platform performance through technical validation on a French 
demonstration site and quantitative user acceptance assessment with 
construction professionals. The paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 
reviews related research; Section 3 presents the research methodol-
ogy; Section 4 details RDT design, development, and deployment; Sec-
tion 5 covers validation and evaluation; and Section 6 presents conclu-
sions.
2.  Literature review
2.1.  DT Applications in building retroﬁt
Building retroﬁt currently relies on static tools with broad as-
sumptions and generic inputs, often lacking digital modelling capabil-
ities or methods for identifying optimal retroﬁt measures for speciﬁc 
projects [21,22]. DT technology oﬀers potential to address these limita-
tions through dynamically updated digital replicas for retroﬁt planning 
and decision-making. However, existing research reveals critical lim-
itations in current DT implementations for renovation contexts. Most 
studies fail to address the multi-stakeholder, multi-domain complexity 
inherent in renovation projects, where coordination between occupants, 
contractors, and project managers becomes essential for success.
2.1.1.  Project phases and DT coverage
Renovation projects involve distinct phases requiring diﬀerent mon-
itoring approaches and stakeholder coordination mechanisms. Design 
and planning phases (D&P) focus on scenario evaluation and retroﬁt 
strategy development. The onsite retroﬁtting phase (OR) involves ac-
tive construction work where real-time monitoring becomes critical for 
managing quality, safety, cost, and schedule performance. Operation 
and maintenance phases (O&M) address post-renovation building per-
formance optimisation. Current DT research shows uneven distribution 
across these phases. Most studies address design/planning activities or 
post-renovation operations, whilst the onsite retroﬁtting phase, where 
coordination challenges are most acute, receives limited attention. This 
gap is particularly signiﬁcant given that renovation projects typically 
experience greater time and cost overruns, increased safety risks, and 
quality issues compared to new-build construction [23].
2.1.2.  Use cases and applications
Building on Pan et al.’s [24] theoretical framework for DT in renova-
tion, current applications have emerged across four primary domains.
Indoor condition monitoring maintains occupant health and safety 
during renovation through environmental parameter tracking. Arsiwala 
et al. [25] developed a DT solution integrating IoT, BIM, and AI to au-
tomate CO2 emission monitoring, demonstrating spatial data visuali-
sation for air quality management with machine learning-based emis-
sion prediction. Studies by Mainini et al. [26], Daniotti et al. [27], and 
Banﬁ et al. [28] developed systems for monitoring temperature, humid-
ity, and CO2 emissions. Tan et al. [29] proposed a DT lighting system 
for dynamic occupant comfort management. Energy modelling and 
monitoring represents the most developed application area, reﬂecting 
retroﬁtting’s energy eﬃciency  goals. Jradi et al. [22] proposed Dan-
RETwin for decision-making and energy optimisation in existing build-
ings through IoT sensors and AI-driven performance analysis. Bocullo 
et al. [30] developed a city block renovation approach using renew-
able energy sources with scenario evaluation capabilities. Trombadore 
et al. [31] and Kaewunruen et al. [32] focused on energy modelling for 
retroﬁt scenario evaluation. Cost analysis applications address eco-
nomic evaluation during planning phases. Trombadore et al. [31] devel-
oped a methodology for heritage building renovation, providing tools to 
evaluate cost-eﬀectiveness whilst monitoring air quality, building per-
formance, and user behaviour. Safety monitoring remains the least de-
veloped domain. Khajavi et al. [33] developed a DT architecture em-
phasising real-time ﬁre detection, incorporating data fusion, localisa-
tion, and propagation simulation. Their approach demonstrated safety 
beneﬁts during renovation when building systems are oﬄine,  though it 
addressed only ﬁre hazards.
2.1.3.  Technical components
DT implementations integrate four core technological components: 
data sensing devices for reality capture and performance tracking; BIM 
and 3D visualisation for digital representation; visualisation dashboards 
for stakeholder communication; and AI for automated data processing. 
Most studies rely on IoT sensors for data exchange between physical 
and digital systems, collecting parameters including temperature, hu-
midity, occupancy, luminance, CO2, and energy consumption [22,27]. 
However, BIM capabilities remain underutilised, with most studies fo-
cusing solely on 3D visualisation rather than leveraging design coordi-
nation, clash detection, or 4D simulation functions [34]. Visualisation 
dashboards are underdeveloped, with limited implementations provid-
ing comprehensive monitoring across multiple performance domains. 
AI adoption remains restricted to speciﬁc applications such as emission 
prediction and computer vision for hazard detection [25,33].

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), developed by Davis [35],

2.3.  Research gaps and contributions
Table 1 reveals three critical gaps in existing DT research for ren-
ovation contexts. First, project phase analysis demonstrates uneven re-
search distribution with only 27% of studies targeting onsite retroﬁtting 
activities where coordination challenges are most acute. Among onsite-
focused studies, only the current RDT platform provides comprehensive 
multi-domain performance monitoring. Second, most studies focus on 
single-domain monitoring rather than the multi-domain approach re-
quired for managing renovation complexity. Only one previous study 
attempted triple-domain monitoring [31], whilst none addresses all ﬁve 
performance domains simultaneously. Third, systematic assessment of 
DT acceptance using established theoretical frameworks remains lim-
ited. This gap is signiﬁcant given that technology adoption in renova-
tion projects depends on alignment with diverse organisational needs 
and stakeholder motivations beyond technical functionality. The contri-
bution of this research is threefold: (i) present a pilot study of a compre-
hensive RDT solution enabling multi-domain monitoring during onsite 
retroﬁtting activities; (ii) provide detailed methodology for DT imple-
mentation in renovation contexts; and (iii) evaluate the platform using 
TAM methodology, addressing the gap in technology acceptance evalu-
ation for multi-domain platforms in renovation environments.
3.  Research methodology
Given the identiﬁed gap in onsite retroﬁtting research and the 
need for multi-domain performance monitoring (Section 2.3), this study 
adopts a DSR methodology to develop and evaluate a comprehensive 
RDT platform. The research addresses the theoretical challenge of bridg-
ing DT technological potential with practical adoption in renovation 
contexts, speciﬁcally targeting the under-researched onsite retroﬁtting 
phase where coordination challenges are most acute. The RDT frame-
work is part of the Horizon Europe-funded RINNO research initiative, 
which aims to optimise deep renovation processes across Europe [38]. 
The RINNO project involves 19 partners from 10 EU countries with 4 
demonstration sites, providing a robust collaborative environment for 
developing and testing renovation technologies. The French demon-
stration site serves as the primary case study for the RDT implemen-
tation [39].
3.1.  Research questions
The research builds upon the comprehensive barrier framework es-
tablished in the foundational RINNO study, which identiﬁes four critical 
categories constraining deep renovation eﬀorts: human, technological, 
organisational, and external environment barriers [8]. This framework 
reveals that eﬀective technologies alone cannot solve low renovation 
rates, necessitating integrated solutions that address both technical func-
tionality and human acceptance factors. Building on this framework and 
the identiﬁed research gaps (Section 2.3), this study addresses two pri-
mary research questions:
RQ1 - Platform development and deployment: can a multi-
domain DT platform be developed that integrates real-time performance 
data across cost, schedule, quality, safety, and environmental domains 
whilst maintaining GDPR compliance and functional operation in oc-
cupied renovation environments? This question addresses the techni-
cal feasibility gap identiﬁed in the literature review, where existing DT 
implementations rarely target onsite retroﬁtting with comprehensive 
multi-domain monitoring (Table 1).
RQ2 - User acceptance evaluation: what are construction profes-
sionals’ perceptions of the RDT platform’s usefulness, ease of use, atti-
tude toward using the system, and intention to adopt it in future reno-
vation projects? This question addresses the human acceptance factors 
critical for DT adoption in multi-stakeholder renovation environments, 
evaluated through the TAM framework. Given the RINNO barrier frame-
work’s emphasis on human factors as equal to technological barriers [8], 
understanding user acceptance is essential for assessing the platform’s 
practical viability beyond technical functionality.
3.2.  DSR Implementation
The methodological approach follows the ﬁve-stage DSR process pro-
posed by Peﬀers et al. [40], with each stage addressing speciﬁc aspects 
of the research challenge (Fig. 1):
Stage 1 – Problem identiﬁcation and motivation: Literature re-
view identiﬁed research gaps in onsite retroﬁtting monitoring and multi-
domain performance tracking. Collaborative workshops with fourteen 
industry stakeholders from RINNO consortium partners, including on-
site workers, site managers, project managers, and engineers, identi-
ﬁed information gaps, reporting needs, and workﬂow bottlenecks dur-
ing retroﬁtting activities.
Stage 2 – Deﬁnition of objectives: Stakeholder input was cate-
gorised into ﬁve key use cases: quality, cost, scheduling, safety, and en-
vironment. Through structured workshops, 13 critical KPIs were iden-
tiﬁed and deﬁned according to four criteria: relevance to renovation 
project success, feasibility of data collection in occupied environments, 
alignment with industry practices, and integration potential with digital 
monitoring systems. Each KPI includes speciﬁc data sources, calculation 
formulas, and visualisation formats (Table 2). Building on stakeholder 
input from Stage 1, weekly measurement frequencies were established 
to balance data granularity with practical site reporting capabilities.
Stage 3 – Design and development: The RDT platform was devel-
oped through iterative prototyping. An initial version was deployed lo-
cally via NGROK [41] for stakeholder testing and feedback. The ﬁnal 
platform, incorporating BIM and comprehensive dashboards, was de-
ployed on HEROKU cloud platform [42] for scalable access.
Stage 4 – Demonstration and evaluation: The RDT was vali-
dated through technical testing using the French demonstration site BIM 
model and functionality veriﬁcation, followed by TAM-based user ac-
ceptance evaluation with 102 industry professionals.
Stage 5 – Communication: Research outcomes, including design 
artefacts, empirical ﬁndings, and methodological insights, are reported 
in this paper with contributions to theory and practice.
4.  The RDT platform
The RDT platform is designed to support the monitoring of 
retroﬁtting progress and the timely provision of feedback to project 
stakeholders. Its main objectives, informed by ﬁndings from the litera-
ture review and stakeholder input, are to: (i) collect onsite information 
related to weekly project performance, including safety, quality, cost, 
task completion, delays, and waste management; and (ii) provide stake-
holders with timely insights to support informed decision-making and 
corrective actions when necessary.

4.2.  System architecture
The development of the RDT platform is guided by the seven-
component architecture presented in Fig. 2. This architecture addresses 
key challenges in DT implementation for renovation contexts: multi-
domain data integration, stakeholder-speciﬁc access control, real-time 
performance feedback, and GDPR compliance. The dual-server de-
sign separates raw data from processed analytics, enabling both real-
time monitoring and historical analysis whilst maintaining data in-
tegrity. Each component fulﬁls speciﬁc design requirements identiﬁed in
Table 3.
The Acquisition Service collects raw data from onsite renovation activ-
ities (quality incidents, complaints, safety issues) and transmits them to 
the Data Storage Server. Data collection employs two primary channels: 
(i) manual input via mobile application for quality, safety, and envi-
ronmental metrics, and (ii) API integration with existing project man-
agement systems for cost and schedule data. To ensure GDPR compli-
ance (DR7), data collection is implemented through these non-intrusive 
methods rather than ﬁxed sensing technologies (e.g. IoT devices or 
sensors), which typically require occupant and operator consent [43]. 
Project managers use the mobile application to input data related to 
project execution and progress, which is also shared with traditional 
project management software such as Teamoty, SAP, and Dalux to 
enable integration with existing project workﬂows. API connections 
utilise RESTful endpoints with JSON data exchange formats, whilst data 
transmission employs secure HTTPS protocols. Data synchronisation oc-
curs on a weekly basis for systematic metrics (cost, schedule, qual-
ity controls) with event-driven reporting for critical incidents (safety 
events, quality issues, complaints), enabling convergence between phys-
ical and digital onsite states at a rate appropriate to renovation project 
needs [44]. The Data Storage component consists of a Structured Query 
Language (SQL) server that stores the collected raw data [45], serving 
as the primary data source for the Processing Service (DR1). The Pro-
cessing Service retrieves raw data from Server 1 and queries traditional 
project management software via API to collect supplementary sched-
ule, cost, and safety data. The service computes relevant KPIs using 
SQL queries and stores the output in KPI Storage (Server 2), generat-
ing reliable KPI data for downstream services and ensuring comprehen-
sive multi-domain monitoring (DR1). The Notiﬁcation Service functions 
as a continuous background process (the ‘KPI watcher’) that monitors 
KPI values and triggers alerts when predeﬁned thresholds are exceeded. 
For each KPI (Table 2), minimum values ensure data quality and con-
sistency, whilst maximum values trigger warnings visualised through 
colour-coded indicators in the Visualisation Service (DR5). The Simulation 
Service enables time-based simulation of historical KPI values stored in 
Server 2, visualised using a ‘Timeliner’ module integrated into the Visu-
alisation Service. KPI trends are mapped onto BIM model elements using 
Forge APIs [46], supporting 4D simulation [43] (DR3). The KPI Storage
component, hosted on a second SQL server, maintains calculated KPIs 
and serves as the central data source for the Simulation, Notiﬁcation, and 
Visualisation services (DR2). The Visualisation Service provides the cen-
tral interface through which users access all visual content, including 
current and historical KPIs. It integrates the BIM model via Forge APIs 
and presents data through multiple formats: shaded BIM elements, 4D 
timelines, and domain-speciﬁc charts and graphs (Figs. 3 and 4). This 
component oﬀers an intuitive and information-rich dashboard (DR4).

face. The MUI library [48] was employed to ensure simplicity, clarity, 
and UI responsiveness across various device types (smartphones, tablets, 
laptops) in both onsite and oﬀsite contexts.

5.3.3.  Discussion
The TAM evaluation reveals comparable importance across all con-
structs, with attitude toward use highest (RII = 0.894), followed by 
perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and behavioural intention 
(all RII = 0.87). The narrow range (0.87–0.894) suggests that suc-
cessful DT adoption requires addressing multiple dimensions simultane-
ously rather than prioritising any single factor. This ﬁnding challenges
simpliﬁed technology acceptance models that emphasise usefulness or 
ease of use alone, and aligns with the RINNO framework’s emphasis on 
comprehensive stakeholder engagement [8].
Analysis of individual items reveals a notable performance gap: Pur-
pose Conﬁdence and Needs Alignment (both RII = 0.92, rank 1) exceed 
Independent Operation (RII = 0.82, rank 24). This 11% diﬀerence indi-
cates that whilst users exhibit strong conceptual acceptance of the plat-
form’s value and relevance, they demonstrate lower conﬁdence in au-
tonomous usage. This pattern suggests that organisational factors, such 
as training provision, technical support availability, and institutional 
implementation strategies, may critically inﬂuence adoption outcomes. 
The predominance of early-career professionals (72.5% with less than 
5 years experience) in the sample may contribute to lower independent 
operation conﬁdence scores, as professional experience typically corre-
lates with technology self-eﬃcacy  in construction contexts [54]. This 
demographic pattern requires further investigation through experience-
stratiﬁed analysis.
System Integration (RII = 0.91, rank 3) emerged as the highest-
ranked ease of use factor, demonstrating that perceived interoperabil-
ity with established BIM tools and workﬂows facilitates acceptance. 
This ﬁnding supports design principles emphasising seamless integra-
tion over standalone systems [55]. The diﬀerence between attitude to-
ward use (RII = 0.894) and behavioural intention (RII = 0.87) may 
indicate that external contextual factors, such as organisational poli-
cies, budget constraints, or project-speciﬁc requirements, inﬂuence im-
plementation decisions beyond individual user acceptance. Future re-
search should examine these organisational and contextual moderators 
through longitudinal adoption studies.
The ﬁnding that attitude toward use achieved the highest acceptance 
score, albeit marginally, suggests that in multi-stakeholder renovation 
environments, trust and organisational alignment may be as important 
as functional performance or ease of use. This aligns with the RINNO 
barrier framework’s identiﬁcation of human factors (trust, alignment) 
as equally critical as technological factors (functionality, usability) in 
determining adoption outcomes [8]. The stakeholder-informed design 
process, which positioned users as active co-creators throughout devel-
opment, likely contributed to the strong conceptual acceptance reﬂected 
in high Purpose Conﬁdence and Needs Alignment scores.
5.3.4.  RDT Application context
The French demonstration site’s lifecycle assessment (Section 4.3, 
Table 5) illustrates the complex sustainability landscape the RDT is 
designed to navigate. The 73% emissions reduction achieved substan-
tial environmental goals, yet the 39% cost increase (NPV: -e326,000, 
no payback) reveals ﬁnancial viability challenges that require transpar-
ent multi-stakeholder communication, precisely the coordination need 
that motivated high ATU scores (RII = 0.894). This tension between 
environmental imperatives and economic constraints exempliﬁes why 
multi-domain KPI tracking becomes critical in occupied retroﬁt envi-
ronments. The RDT’s simultaneous monitoring of cost, schedule, quality, 
safety, and environmental performance enables stakeholders to identify 
trade-oﬀs in real-time, supporting the informed decision-making nec-
essary when capital costs are high and regulatory requirements strict. 
Technology-speciﬁc insights from the assessment (e.g., biobased K-FLEX 
insulation achieving comparable thermal performance whilst reducing 
embodied emissions) demonstrate the granular visibility the platform 
provides. The 50-year lifecycle perspective captured in Table 5 reveals 
how operational savings oﬀset upfront environmental impacts, insights 
that would support evaluating policy interventions (subsidies, incen-
tives, carbon pricing) required to bridge economic viability gaps. This 
contextualizes the platform’s value proposition: in projects where envi-
ronmental performance is technically achievable but economically con-
tingent on supportive frameworks, the RDT’s multi-domain data be-
comes essential for stakeholder alignment and evidence-based policy 
advocacy.
5.4.  Limitations and future work
Several considerations should be acknowledged when interpreting 
these ﬁndings and planning future implementations. The RDT platform 
was tested on a single renovation project (Project Sarrazins, Lille), lim-
iting generalisability to other retroﬁt contexts involving diﬀerent build-
ing types, scales, and renovation scopes. The proof-of-concept demon-
stration focused on functional veriﬁcation rather than comprehensive

empirical validation. Whilst the TAM-based evaluation provided in-
sights into user acceptance, the study lacks quantiﬁed performance im-
provements (cost savings, schedule adherence, safety incident reduc-
tion) that could validate the platform’s core beneﬁts. The short eval-
uation period may not capture long-term usability issues or eﬀective-
ness throughout complete renovation lifecycles. The TAM sample ex-
hibited bias toward early-career professionals (72.5% with less than 5 
years experience), potentially limiting applicability to experienced prac-
titioners with diﬀerent adoption patterns. Technical limitations include 
absence of AI capabilities, with the simulation service supporting only 
historical KPI visualisation without predictive analytics for proactive is-
sue identiﬁcation. Reliance on manual data entry through mobile appli-
cations may introduce inconsistencies compared to automated sensor-
based collection systems. The evaluation relied on self-reported percep-
tions rather than observed usage behaviours, which may not accurately 
reﬂect actual adoption patterns in real-world scenarios [56]. The sus-
tainability outcomes from the French demonstration site (Section 4.3, 
Table 5) represent a single case study with speciﬁc economic and regu-
latory characteristics that may limit generalisability of the cost-beneﬁt 
ﬁndings.
Future research should address these considerations through multi-
site validation across diverse renovation projects (residential versus 
commercial, varying scales and types) in diﬀerent climate zones and 
regulatory environments to establish broader applicability. Longitudi-
nal performance studies tracking actual project metrics before, dur-
ing, and after RDT implementation would provide empirical evidence 
of platform eﬀectiveness. Research involving experienced profession-
als and comparative analysis between RDT-supported projects and tra-
ditional approaches would quantify relative beneﬁts and identify opti-
mal implementation strategies. Technical enhancements should priori-
tise automated data integration through IoT sensors alongside predictive 
analytics using deep learning for proactive project management sup-
port [57]. Integration of predictive monitoring methods into the RDT 
platform to assess the impact of renovation works on indoor conditions 
could inform occupants and stakeholders before retroﬁtting processes 
begin [58]. Likewise, incorporation of material circularity metrics into 
the RDT platform to monitor reuse rates, recycled content percentages, 
and end-of-life recoverability would align renovation tracking with EU 
Circular Economy Action Plan targets [59]. The current RDT framework 
provides foundation for developing cognitive digital twin systems capa-
ble of learning from project patterns, providing intelligent recommen-
dations, and supporting autonomous decision-making processes [60].
6.  Conclusions
This paper presents the development, deployment, and evaluation 
of an RDT platform designed to support multi-domain performance 
monitoring in building retroﬁt projects. The research addresses iden-
tiﬁed gaps in DT applications for renovation contexts, where exist-
ing frameworks predominantly focus on operational phase monitoring 
rather than onsite construction performance across quality, cost, sched-
ule, safety, and environmental domains. The RDT was developed using 
DSR methodology, incorporating stakeholder input from RINNO consor-
tium partners to deﬁne 13 KPIs across ﬁve performance domains. Tech-
nical validation on the French demonstration site conﬁrmed functional 
operation across all seven system components, successful BIM integra-
tion, multi-stakeholder access control, and GDPR compliance through 
mobile-based data collection.
User acceptance evaluation with 102 construction professionals re-
vealed strong positive perceptions across all TAM constructs. Attitude 
toward use emerged as the strongest adoption driver (RII = 0.894), with 
Purpose Conﬁdence and Needs Alignment as highest-rated items (both 
RII = 0.92), demonstrating strong conceptual acceptance of the plat-
form’s value and relevance. System Integration (RII = 0.91) emerged 
as the most valued ease of use factor, whilst Independent Operation (RII 
= 0.82) identiﬁed training and support as priority enhancement areas. 
These ﬁndings conﬁrm that interoperability with established BIM work-
ﬂows critically facilitates adoption [55].
Theoretically, the research extends TAM to multi-stakeholder con-
struction environments, revealing that attitude-driven acceptance may 
be as important as perceived usefulness in complex renovation con-
texts. This ﬁnding provides empirical support for the RINNO barrier 
framework [8], demonstrating that human barriers (trust, alignment) 
can equal technological barriers (functionality, usability) in determining 
adoption outcomes. The study advances socio-technical systems theory 
by demonstrating successful coordination of multiple performance do-
mains through GDPR-compliant architecture with stakeholder-speciﬁc 
access control.
Practically, the stakeholder-informed design process positioned 
users as active co-creators, embodying the ‘human-in-the-loop’ prin-
ciple essential for eﬀective DT implementation [61]. Strong perfor-
mance of integration-related factors provides empirical guidance em-
phasising that seamless workﬂow integration represents a critical suc-
cess factor. The platform demonstrates potential for establishing new 
project management routines supporting sustainability’s three pillars: 
enhanced collaboration (social), waste reduction (environmental), and 
improved cost and schedule management (economic). Deployment on 
the French demonstration site illustrates this multi-domain capability: 
whilst achieving substantial environmental performance (73% emis-
sions reduction), the project faced signiﬁcant economic challenges (39% 
cost increase, NPV: -e326,000), exemplifying the complex trade-oﬀs the 
RDT’s KPI tracking framework is designed to make transparent to stake-
holders. Successful DT implementation requires not only technical capa-
bility but also systematic attention to human factors, training provision, 
and organisational change management processes that enable the socio-
technical transformation necessary for deep renovation acceleration.
