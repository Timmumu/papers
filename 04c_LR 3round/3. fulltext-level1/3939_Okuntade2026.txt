ABSTRACT
Little attention has been given to the quality of construction as a potential influence on the energy and carbon 
performance of buildings in many developing economies. In addition to causing post-construction defects that 
compromise building envelopes, poor construction quality reduces the long-term operational efficiency of 
building materials. In this paper, we reconceptualize construction defects as indirect drivers of energy and carbon 
use by integrating defect severity, repair frequency, and lifecycle considerations into a Quality-Sustainability 
Integration Index (QSII). We employed a mixed-methods approach comprising a survey of 83 construction 
professionals in Nigeria and a longitudinal review of maintenance records for 14 residential, commercial, and 
institutional buildings in Lagos, Nigeria. BIM-based Lifecycle Assessment was used to quantify the embodied 
carbon associated with defect-induced repair cycles, and a conceptual Digital Twin prototype was developed to 
visualize defect propagation pathways and intervention strategies. Results indicate that envelope- and 
waterproofing-related defects generate disproportionately high lifecycle carbon emissions and indirect opera -
tional energy penalties due to thermal degradation in hot-humid climates. Findings are calibrated for Lagos ’ s hot- 
humid climate and institutional context; application to other climatic or regulatory settings requires local 
empirical validation and parameter recalibration. By positioning construction quality as a latent lever for energy 
efficiency rather than solely a management issue, this research advances building performance discourse beyond 
the design stage and emphasizes defect prevention for long-term sustainability.
1. Introduction
The United Nations Environment Programme [1] states that 37% of 
all global carbon emissions come from buildings ’ construction and 
ongoing maintenance. The significant amount of research on building 
energy has mainly focused on design phase optimization, and there is 
relatively little empirical evidence about how buildings perform after 
completion. Buildings rarely operate under ideal conditions because the 
envelope of a building, the management of moisture, continuity of 
insulation, and air-tightness of the building are often negatively 
impacted due to poor workmanship, lack of maintenance, and recurring 
construction defects. All of these problems will reduce the thermal 
performance and drastically alter the total carbon footprint throughout 
the life cycle of the building.
In the case of Lagos Nigeria, a rapidly developing megacity situated 
in a hot-humid tropical environment, building envelope failures occur 
because of inadequate waterproofing, insufficient concrete cover, poor 
roof detailing, and discontinuities in the thermal barrier, leading to 
increased moisture intrusion and conductive heat transfer, which in -
creases demand for cooling, accelerating premature material replace -
ment. The result is substantial added embodied carbon from repair 
processes as well as indirect operational energy costs. However, the 
overwhelming majority of current building energy codes measure 
operational performance only at their initial occupancy and do not 
factor in ongoing deterioration of the building envelope or lifecycle 
carbon emissions. This creates a large gap between projected theoretical 
energy use and actual in-use performance, particularly for new econo -
mies, which do not have sufficient monitoring capabilities.
Quality management systems in construction have typically classi -
fied defects as either technical failures or maintenance costs [2,3] and 
assessed their severities based on structural integrity and the costs of 
rectification without considering the energy or carbon costs involved. 
Okuntade [4] has developed a systematic taxonomy of 29 types of de -
fects, ranking them with respect to the Nigerian construction industry.

The purpose of this research is to provide an integrative framework 
for evaluating both the quality aspect of a construction defect and its 
sustainability consequences via employment of a new composite metric 
called Quality-Sustainability Integration Index (QSII). QSII expands 
upon the work of Okuntade [4] , who used the severity of defects to 
evaluate defects according to their lifecycle carbon footprint. QSII pro -
vides a composite metric of sustainability by taking into account addi -
tional variables such as repair frequency and carbon emissions from the 
complete building lifecycle. The QSII was developed through a combi -
nation of primary data collected from 83 construction professionals 
during a survey and an analysis of building maintenance records for 14 
buildings over five years in Lagos, Nigeria. The buildings also underwent 
carbon modelling using building information modelling (BIM). In 
addition to developing the QSII, a conceptual digital twin prototype was 
developed to visually represent ways that defects propagate (i.e., how 
they develop over time following original detection) and evaluate in -
terventions for programmatic processes. This study's primary focus on 
Lagos provides much-needed context for developing context-specific 
metrics of defect-related carbon emissions for construction systems 
located in a hot-humid climate and consists of unique features such as 
institutions, material-specific supply chains, and construction methods. 
There are three specific goals of this study. First, to expand the theory of 
defect severity to include lifecycle carbon and envelope performance; 
second, to determine the lifecycle carbon multiplier for common defect 
types in Lagos; and third, to demonstrate use of a quality-sustainability 
assessment method that works well in emerging economies with limited 
access to complete data. The remainder of this article will be organized 
according to sections/parts. Sections 2 provides a review of applicable 
literature and theoretical models; Section 3 explains the methods used to 
collect data regarding defect severity and lifecycle carbon emissions; 
Section 4 discusses the findings and modelling; Section 5 analyzes the 
contributions and implications of this research towards theory and 
practitioner; and finally, Section 6 provides recommendations for 
further research.
2. Literature review & theoretical framework
2.1. Construction defects: from technical failures to performance 
degradation
Traditionally construction defects have been defined in very narrow 
technical terms. For example, Ramson [5] states that any deviation from 
intended design or specified quality or workmanship standards can 
constitute a defect. The primary way in which to evaluate this defect 
type is according to the cost of remediating the defect. Seeley [6] also 
provides an extension of Ramson ’ s definition of construction quality by 
correlating construction quality to both future maintenance re -
quirements and service life performance. In his landmark paper on 
construction defects in the Nigerian context, Okuntade [4] provided a 
systematic listing of 29 different types of construction defects based on a 
consensus of professional opinion and established a hierarchy of severity 
of each type with the type having insufficient cover over reinforcing of 
concrete rated as the most severe type of defect due to its effects on the 
durability of a structure. However, like most construction defect man -
agement research, the research conducted by Okuntade only examines 
and defines defects from technical and economic perspectives, without 
considering the environmental externalities associated with these 
defects.
In more recent research, a systemic perspective began to develop. 
Atkinson [7] found that nearly 82% of construction defects are caused 
by management or organisational factors (as opposed to simply tech -
nical issues), indicating that governance-level intervention is key. In the 
Nigerian institutional setting, Fagbenle and Oluwunmi [8] have iden -
tified inadequacies in quality assurance systems as a contributing factor 
in building collapse rates nearly 24 times higher than those of OECD 
countries. Although they have studied the governance aspects of the 
problem, none of the studies identified a quantitative relationship be -
tween defect recurrence and CO
2 
emission and/or envelope energy 
performance degradation over the operational life of a building. The 
lack of a connection between defect severity and long-term carbon and 
energy consequences is the most significant gap present in hot-humid 
climates, where envelope failures result in moisture intrusion, reduced 
thermal resistance, and hastened material deterioration, which both 
create embodied carbon (due to repair cycles) and generate operational 
energy penalties (due to poor envelope performance).

Data integration for all lifecycle phases has been furthered through

There are great similarities between the recent developments made 
in building performance analysis and the methodology described here. 
As an example, a study by Yang et al. [16] assesses the performance of 
different types of retrofit of courtyard dwellings in China, with specific 
reference to their location in hot-humid climates. The results indicate 
that retrofits of courtyard dwellings   that have similar features to the 
ones assessed in this study   may be subject to substantial energy 
penalties in hot-humid locations due to increased cooling capabilities; 
these results provide important context for the focus of this project on 
determining retrofitted courtyard dwellings in hot-humid areas and how 
that will affect their thermal performance. In another study by Yang 
et al. [17] , a framework for multi-objective optimization of the green 
renovation of Tibetan courtyard dwellings has been developed using 
NSGA-II; by employing Sobol' sensitivity analysis and optimization with 
back propagation neural networks (BPNN) to reduce energy use, thermal 
discomfort hours and initial cost simultaneously, the researchers 
developed a composite indicator methodology that provides useful 
comparison for the QSII's multidimensional assessment framework. 
Further, both studies emphasize the necessity of calibrating building 
performance assessments to the climate where the building is located, 
thereby supporting the justification for creating context-specific indices, 
like the QSII, rather than applying globally generic frameworks.
2.3. Theoretical positioning: governance, circularity and carbon multiplier 
mechanisms
This research combines the Transaction Cost Economics (TCE) and 
the Circular Economy theory to form the theoretical basis of the quality- 
sustainability relationship. Williamson [18] has shown within TCE that 
defects in initial quality cause additional transaction frequency due to 
maintenance actions being repeated and information asymmetries 
existing between the parties involved in the construction process. In 
construction, repeated deficiencies produce hidden governance costs 
which materialise as repeated repair contracts and the use of supervisory 
intervention representing greater than just financial costs through both 
material waste and total embodied carbon created during the defect 
lifecycle. Circular Economy principles [19,20] provide additional 
context to support characterized repair cycles induced by defects as 
interruptions to circularity of materials as each repair cycle creates 
embodied carbon and reduces the effective service life of the compo -
nent. Therefore, investments in quality can be reconceptualized to be 
interventions that support the achievement of circularity by extending 
service life, decreasing material turnover and preventing total embodied 
carbon accumulation.
This research introduces the carbon multiplier concept as a formal 
way to express the non-linear link between a defect and its overall 
carbon footprint. Instead of being linear models where all repair carbon 
comes only from the number of materials required to complete the repair 
(e.g., waterproofing), carbon multiplier models have a cascading impact 
due to other aspects of the failure mechanism. For example, if a water -
proofing defect fails, it can cause secondary damages to the ceiling 
system, structural elements and electrical systems, which in turn will all 
require additional materials (and, therefore, carbon) to remediate. As a 
result, the carbon multiplier model provides a more comprehensive 
basis for prioritising quality investment than merely looking at struc -
tural severity criteria alone, as it provides a more complete view of the 
defect's downstream carbon liability.
3. Methodology
3.1. Research design and philosophical approach
The study adopts a pragmatic epistemological approach, using a 
mixed methods research design. It collects quantitative survey data, 
conduct a case study analysis, and create a conceptual model. The design 
philosophy acknowledges that construction knowledge exists within 
both social and cultural contexts, and therefore seeks to use established 
local methodologies that are compatible with Nigerian practice rather 
than applying an externally developed framework. This is termed as 
‘contextual theoretical extension ’ and builds incrementally on Okun -
tade ’ s [4] validated framework of defect severity, adding dimensions of 
sustainability but retaining empirical validity for the Nigerian context.
A three-step process is used in this research. Phase One uses a 
structured survey of professionals to obtain data on the types of defects 
and their severity (this is a repeat of Okuntade's [4] methodology). 
Phase Two uses a longitudinal study to determine how defects propagate 
from one item to another and what materials were used to repair them. 
Phase Three integrates the findings from both previous phases into a 
composite Quality Assurance for Information Technology (QAIT) metric 
and a conceptual Digital Twin prototype. The results of each phase are 
used to impact the other phases, i.e., the calibration of the survey in -
forms the parameters for the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) model; how 
defects propagate informs the architecture of the Digital Twin's defect 
scenario.
3.2. Data collection procedures
3.2.1. Quantitative survey
A structured questionnaire survey was conducted with construction 
professionals working in Lagos to replicate Okuntade's [4] defect 
severity framework and extend it to include dimensions of sustainabil -
ity. The survey serves as a confirmation tool for the parameters of pro -
fessional judgement in terms of determining defect severity, carbon 
footprint, and reoccurrence of repairs for the analytical framework, 
rather than a representative sample of the population. Participants were 
recruited from professional networks containing builders, architects and 
civil engineers with direct experience in the delivery of construction 
projects, supervising construction projects or maintaining construction 
projects. A minimum of three years ’ experience as a professional was 
required to participate in the study.
Data were collected in the form of 83 valid responses received over a 
period of January to April 2023, giving rise to a total response rate of 
72%. The total sample consisted of 36 builders (43.4%); 22 architects 
(26.5%); and 25 civil engineers (30.1%). The survey included all 29 
categories of defects identified by Okuntade in 2014 for methodological 
consistency; therefore, each respondent evaluated traditional defect 
severity on a four-point scale, carbon emission impact judgment on a 
five-point ordinal scale, and annual repair frequency estimates. Survey 
results provided calibration for the QSII ’ s Severity Index (SI) compo -
nent, Carbon Emission Index (CEI) component, and not distributions

3.2.2. Case study analysis
Collection of maintenance records that could count towards the 
overall performance of the given buildings in Lagos was done at 14 
buildings represented by a total of eight residential, four commercial 
and two institutional buildings, with formal consent obtained from each 
building ’ s facility manager. All locations have been constructed within 
the last five years (between 2015 and 2018) and have a minimum of 
three years of records. In instances where there is a larger set of records 
available (5 years of records   2018 – 2023 was preferred), they were 
used. A representative sample of buildings were selected for variance in 
type of building (residential, commercial or institutional), quality of 
construction in order to achieve a broad range of analysis at all levels of 
typology and performance level, as described previously. Due to the 
unavailability of Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs) specific to 
Nigeria, the amount of embodied carbon was determined using the 
University of Nottingham's (ICE v3.0) emission factors as baseline 
relative to the carbon emissions for each of the buildings examined. The 
results of the sensitivity analysis that altered the emission coefficients 
( ± 20%) confirmed that the aggregate rankings of all the defect cate -
gories were stable throughout. To quantify embodied carbon, standard 
LCA reference databases were applied. Given the absence of Nigeria- 
specific EPDs, ICE v3.0 emission factors were used as relative multi -
pliers; detailed limitations and uncertainty mitigation strategies for 
these proxy factors are provided in Section 3.2.4 .
3.2.3. Data completeness and estimation procedures
Data completeness summary: A total of 847 repair entries were 
reviewed. Approximately 18% of repair entries (153) required partial 
material quantity estimation. Approximately 7% of repair entries (59) 
required complete reconstruction of both quantity and cost data. Flag -
ged entries (reconstructed) are provided. As required for full trans -
parency of methodology, these results are provided for ease of 
identifying the repairs needing these methods.
The maintenance documentation quality was inconsistent 
throughout the 14 buildings studied. Frequency of repairs for each 
building was consistently reported; however, there were some entries 
without documented quantities of materials used. For all entries missing 
quantities, an estimated quantity was calculated using the median 
documented quantity of materials used for repairs that are representa -
tive of the project type (i.e., residential, commercial, or institutional). 
Any entries with missing quantities or costs were reconstructed using 
descriptions of defects, records of work performed, and material speci -
fications obtained from similar repair projects in the same data set.
Estimations of parameters that were reconstructed were used to 
generate regular distributions rather than single points to avoid inap -
propriate statement of the precision of an estimation. All estimations of 
uncertainty are carried forward in a way that makes it clear to all users 
of the analytical model that uncertainty related to documentation is 
carried transparently through the analytical model rather than being 
absorbed covertly into single point estimates.
3.2.4. Expert consensus validation workshops
In Lagos, 17 seasoned professionals (architects, engineers and con -
tractors) participated in three structured validation workshops to reach 
consensus on established backgrounds related to building performance 
and assessing defects to determine their weight in the QSII. The single 
round of consensus validation was conducted with the use of a struc -
tured process different than the Delphi survey method consisting of one 
round. The workshops were used as an opportunity to validate survey 
findings and calibrate the QSII weighting coefficients. The participants 
each assigned relative weights to each of the three QSII criteria based on 
a maximum total weight of 1.0; the final calibrated weights were based 
on the median of all individual allocations (weights assigned by all 
participants). The weights validated through consensus were: W

3.2.5. Limitations of proxy emission factors.
The Inventory of Carbon and Energy (ICE) v3.0 database has been 
used for embodied carbon calculations because there are no Nigeria- 
based Environmental Product Declarations (EPDs). ICE v3.0 does have 
peer-reviewed and internationally referenced emission factors; howev -
er, this particular database also has some important limitations that 
should to be stated explicitly: 
(1) Regional Differences in Materials: Nigerian cement blends are 
more often produced with higher levels of local supplementary 
materials (e.g., calcined clay or limestone fill) than European 
formulations that form the foundation for the ICE factors, which 
could lead to a variance of + /- 15 to 25 percent in the carbon 
footprint of the produced cement [21] .
(2) Supply Chain and Transportation Differences: Local energy sup -
plies, alternative sources of materials to find, and methods of 
moving materials around Lagos will affect lifecycle emissions 
when compared to standard industrialized production 
assumptions.
(3) Imported Coefficient Uncertainty: Applying coefficients derived 
from Global North contexts to emerging economies risks sys -
tematic bias when production systems, grid intensities, and ma -
terial standards differ substantially [20] .
The limitations of this study were alleviated using: (a) ICE factors 
used only as relative multipliers for comparative defect rankings [22]
instead of absolute carbon accounting; (b) partial to full sustainability 
variation of ± 20% for emission coefficients to demonstrate stability of 
relation between defect categories; and (c) all carbon values indicated 
but not definitive. We hope that the creation of Nigerian EPDs within 
specific contexts will be developed as tools to support timely lifecycle 
assessment that may occur through research.

3.3.2. Conceptual digital twin development
A conceptual Digital Twin prototype was developed as a descriptive 
visualization and scenario exploration tool to parameterize defect 
propagation pathways and compare lifecycle carbon trajectories under 
different construction quality scenarios. The prototype integrates BIM 
geometry with lifecycle carbon estimation and Monte Carlo uncertainty 
analysis (5,000 iterations) to generate probabilistic carbon projections 
reported as 95% confidence intervals. Three high-impact defect cate -
gories identified in the survey and case study phases (insufficient con -
crete cover, poor waterproofing, and inadequate thermal envelope 
detailing) were parameterized to simulate degradation processes under 
Lagos climate conditions. Consistent with Grieves and Vickers [14] , this 
conceptual Digital Twin prototype serves strictly as a conceptual support 
tool for stakeholder communication and intervention scenario compar -
ison; it is not validated for real-time operational control or predictive 
energy simulation due to current data infrastructure limitations in the 
Nigerian context. All uncertainty arising from reconstructed mainte -
nance records is explicitly propagated through the Monte Carlo frame -
work to maintain methodological transparency.. Fig. 1 illustrates the 
integrated methodological framework, showing the flow from data 
collection phases through the QSII analytical structure to Digital Twin 
scenario outputs.
4. Findings
4.1. QSII rankings and carbon multiplier analysis
Okuntade [4] classifies defect severity traditionally; however, these 
rankings do not align with the QSII results when sustainability criteria 
are introduced. For instance, while inadequate concrete cover (SI =
83.7) was classified as the most severe defect based on structural 
durability, the highest composite QSII score was assigned to inadequate 
waterproofing and drainage (QSII = 86.2). This shift reflects the sub -
stantial carbon multiplier (3.4 × ) attributed to waterproofing failures 
and their elevated repair frequency. Specifically, initial moisture intru -
sion initiates a cascade of secondary failures across ceiling, structural, 
and mechanical systems, each requiring additional repairs that generate 
substantial embodied carbon. The full QSII rankings for the top 10 defect 
categories are presented in Table 2 .
It is important to clarify that the operational energy penalties 
referenced throughout this study were conceptually inferred from 
established building physics principles and Lagos climate data, rather 
than directly simulated via dynamic energy modelling software. Using 
published U-value degradation coefficients and cooling degree-day 
metrics, we estimate that envelope-related defects likely increase cool -
ing demand by 12 – 18% in hot-humid conditions, consistent with 
climate-adaptive proxy methods [16] . These estimates are presented as 
indicative directional impacts to contextualize the total sustainability 
liability of carbon-amplifying defects.
Statistical validation confirmed strong inter-professional consensus 
in severity assessments: Kruskal-Wallis analysis revealed no statistically 
significant differences in defect severity ratings across professional 
groups (builders, architects, engineers): H(2) = 1.24, p = 0.54, consis -
tent with Okuntade [4] . Spearman's rank-order correlation indicated a 
moderate, statistically significant positive relationship between tradi -
tional SI scores and composite QSII rankings ( ρ = 0.68, p < 0.01), 
confirming substantial overlap while also highlighting meaningful 
divergence where sustainability criteria reorder priorities. For example, 
poor thermal envelope detailing ranks 4th in the QSII with a carbon 
multiplier of 3.1 × , representing a high-recurrence, carbon-amplifying 
defect that remains obscured in traditional structural severity rankings. 
All statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.2.1; effect sizes and 95% 
confidence intervals are reported alongside exact p-values to support 
interpretability and reproducibility (Cumming, 2014).
Taken together, these results empirically demonstrate that 
sustainability-informed defect prioritization cannot be reliably inferred 
from structural severity rankings alone, particularly for moisture- 
management and thermal envelope defects in hot-humid climates such 
as Lagos.

5. Discussion
5.1. Theoretical contributions
The research contributes to advancing construction sustainability 
through three different theoretical contributions that are each addressed 
within the framework of gaps found in the literature that pertain to the 
three contributions in Section 2 .
The first contribution is an advancement of the theory of construc -
tion defect severity via the introduction of a new conceptual type of 
defect known as a ‘carbon-amplifying defect ’ ; by definition, these types 
of defects do not fit perfectly within the defined structural severity 
criteria. As referenced in Section 2.1 , the gap within previous literature 
was that there was no framework linking defects, severity, and the long- 
term carbon and energy implications of these types of defects in devel -
oping economy countries that are classified as hot-humid climates. The 
gap in current literature can now be closed by the empirical research 
provided by this study, demonstrating that the carbon multiplier effects 
associated with the carbon-amplifying defect (ranging from 1.6 × to 
3.4 × ) systematically reorder the defect priority hierarchy based on the 
inclusion of sustainability-type criteria, meaning the existing conven -
tional defect severity ranking developed by Okuntade [4] materially 
under-represents the sustainability liabilities associated with carbon- 
amplifying defect types. This is not merely an improvement to an 
existing theory; it is a complete reconceptualization of what it means for 
severity to be evaluated against long-term performance of a building 
rather than immediate structural risk.
Secondly, this research contributes to the advancement of the 
quality-sustainability integration (QSII) instrument by introducing it as 
an empirically calibrated, operationally viable data-limited framework 
for use in developing countries as called for by Zhang et al. [10] , where 
they identify a gap in the current research literature surrounding inte -
grated frameworks applicable to those types of economies. Current 
sustainability indices, including the aggregated-composite methods 
discussed by Yang et al. [17] , are mostly developed with a consideration 
of having access to high-quality simulation inputs and/or longitudinally 
monitored data. In contrast, the QSII was designed and built with the 
realities of operating in an environment with extremely limited data 
availability due to both its use of expert-concurrent calibration (rather 
than instrumented monitoring) and its use of documented uncertainty as 
an uncertainty input to the model (rather than as a limitation that needs 
to be concealed). This methodological reframing creates a new theo -
retical method for assessing the sustainable development amounts of 
emerging economies on a regional basis using the QSII as a common, 
standards-based facility for assessing sustainable development in 
developing countries.
Theoretical relationships between lifecycle performance of buildings 
and construction management quality control and construction man -
agement are strengthened because building repair is the major factor 
causing carbon differences between scenarios (42% to 38%) rather than 
material selection. This clearly fills the gap identified in Section 2.2 of 
the LCA literature regarding failure-driven turnover of construction 
materials not being properly accounted for in lifecycle carbon models. 
This research creates a framework for conducting future studies of the 
relationship between local knowledge and developing theories to better 
understand collaborative sustainable development practices based on 
Transaction Cost Economics and Circular Economic principles.
5.2. Comparison with related research
The carbon multiplier ranges (1.6x to 3.4x) identified in this study 
support the common principle that the total amount of embodied carbon 
from repair cycles is significantly greater than just the direct material 
carbon emitted in defect-prone building systems [20] . However, very 
few studies have used carbon multiplier methodologies in warm/humid 
countries, which limits the number of direct comparisons to be made to 
other countries/regions (this is a critical contribution of this study 
because it identifies a knowledge gap in the methodology used).
The excess lifecycle carbon associated with waterproofing defects is 
much higher (28%) than what would typically be experienced due to 
performance penalties associated with similar types of envelope failures 
in temperate climates because moisture-driven deterioration mecha -
nisms tend to occur at a significantly faster rate in humid climates like 
Lagos (consistent with [16] ). This also emphasizes the importance of 
developing defect management systems that are specific to climate 
zones when developing building codes and considering how to weigh 
the construction quality vs. materials used within a building code. The 
amount of carbon attributed to the frequency of repairs (42%) creates 
substantial opportunity for reformulating how the LCA process is 
applied, and creates opportunities for the building industry to imple -
ment innovative defect management practices.
5.3. Practical implications
5.3.1. For construction professionals
The QSII provides a usable spreadsheet tool to evaluate the sus -
tainability effects of building defects through data present to a con -
struction industry professional. The defect propagation pathways 
( Table 3 and Fig. 2 ) provide an abstract model of lifecycle concepts, 
showing, in a graphical format, how latent defects will show up in the 
form of sustainability liabilities. The case study analysis showed that 
remediation of a waterproofing deficiency after the fact took 3 – 5 times 
as many resources as could have been spent on preventing the deficiency 
at the time of construction. The quality-first building example produced 
18 – 29% less carbon emissions over its lifecycle and 22 – 31% less cost to 
maintain over a 15-year period, with carbon reduction paybacks of 
12 – 18 months.
5.3.2. For policy and regulatory development
Incorporating quality-adjusted carbon factors into current life cycle 
assessment databases would allow government agencies to generate 
more accurate environmental product declarations based on actual 
performance during use, rather than idealised laboratory performance. 
Integrating quality service indicator (QSI) metrics into assessment sys -
tems for buildings and public procurement may facilitate the institu -
tionalisation of defect prevention as part of a sustainable strategy and 
treat quality assurance and sustainability as one regulatory system, 
rather than two discrete, separate systems.
5.3.3. For digital implementation strategy
The Digital Twin model illustrates the correct order for adopting 
technological innovations in environments where there are limited re -
sources. It starts with providing stakeholders with ways to visualize and 
communicate scenarios; by first improving their awareness of the sys -
tem, they will be ready for operational deployment of predictive sys -
tems. Additionally, the use of productized, standardized (industry- 
standard) software such as BIM-LCA (Building Information Modeling 
Life-Cycle Assessment) tools like Revit and Tally rather than bespoke 
software, helps enhance the methodological robustness of the

5.4. Limitations and boundary conditions
Geographic scope represents a key boundary condition: data collec -
tion occurred exclusively in Lagos, Nigeria. While this provides strong 
internal validity for the local context, transferability to arid, temperate, 
or other tropical sub-climates cannot be assumed without empirical 
verification. The hot-humid climate accelerates moisture-driven degra -
dation mechanisms; carbon multiplier values for envelope defects, for 
instance, may differ substantially in regions where precipitation pat -
terns and cooling demands vary. Therefore, one cannot assume that a 
direct transfer can be made to an arid or temperate or other tropical sub- 
climates, without specifically calibrating for their respective climates. 
The maintenance dataset consists of only five years of observations so 
the information will not fully capture the long-term propagation path -
ways of slowly developing defects and therefore cannot be used as a 
model for the carbon contribution from the lifecycle of a defect at the 
end of its lifecycle. Since ICE v3.0 emission factors were used as proxies 
for carbon intensities of the supply chain in Nigeria, there is an 
assumption that the absolute value of emissions may vary from the ac -
curate representation; however, the sensitivity analysis to rank defects 
was shown to be robust for relative defector rankings. The QSII 
weighting was confirmed through structured consensus workshops, and 
while it does contain subjectivity, there is no empirical validation for the 
Conceptual digital Twin prototype or operationally validating as it is 
presently a conceptual tool only.
6. Conclusion
In this study, we provide empirical support that construction quality 
management is a latent but quantifiable lever for improving energy and 
carbon performance in buildings. Using data collected from Lagos, 
Nigeria, we illustrate how traditional ways of estimating the severity of 
defects have not taken into account the sustainability liabilities associ -
ated with defects that increase carbon emissions. By developing the 
Quality/Sustainability Impact Index (QSII), we demonstrate a signifi -
cantly different order of prioritizing defects than through structural 
severity alone; envelope and waterproofing defects are now ranked as 
the top sustainability risks because of their high carbon multiplier (up to 
3.4 × ) and high frequency of repair cycle. Equally, longitudinal case 
studies show that the triggering of waterproofing failures causes sec -
ondary damage to the building and accumulates 82.3 tCO
2
e of emissions 
per 1,000 m
2 
over a five-year period, while envelope defect cases 
generate 28% more lifecycle carbon than comparable cases with quality 
control measures in place. The scenario analysis shows that the lifecycle 
carbon difference between high-quality and low-quality construction 
scenarios is 44%, well in excess of the 3 – 4% increase in initial con -
struction costs associated with quality-first practices. Most importantly, 
42% of the lifecycle carbon difference can be attributed to frequency of 
repair rather than simply selection of materials, disputing common as -
sumptions about lifecycle carbon.
The geographical and climatic conditions define the parameters of 
this research. Therefore, all findings, carbon multipliers and defect 
propagation pathways have been developed with respect to Lagos ’ hot 
humid climate, local material supply chains and construction practices 
used in institutional settings. The moisture-induced accelerated degra -
dation mechanisms described in Lagos may not apply in the same way to 
arid climates or to temperate or other tropical sub-climates. Thus, while 
the QSII methodology can be used in different locations; the quantitative 
outputs of the QSI method must first be validated using local data, and 
recalibrated with local parameters prior to using the QSI method in 
other locations.
Future research should place priority on the development of Envi -
ronmental Product Declarations in Nigeria (to replace the Proxy Emis -
sion Factor), long-term monitoring of 15 – 20 years to reflect the entirety 
of defect propagation lifecycles; geographical verification studies to 
adapt the QSII parameters to the range of climate zone; and the pro -
gressive operationalization of the Digital Twin conceptual prototype as 
the building performance monitoring infrastructure matures within 
emerging economies. By reconceptualising defects in construction as 
liabilities to systemic sustainability, this study offers a practical and 
context-appropriate framework for integrating carbon reduction and 
quality assurance within resource-constrained environments.

• Material quantity documentation for repairs

For “ Inadequate waterproofing and drainage ” :
