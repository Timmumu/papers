 A B S T R A C T
High stacking precision is essential to prevent structural collisions, reduce safety accidents, and enhance 
project efficiency in modular integrated construction. However, current stacking precision is constrained 
by delayed hoisting control, limited perception, and fragmented information sharing. Therefore, this paper 
proposes a digital twin-driven pose estimation and trajectory prediction system via spatio-temporal feature 
fusion modeling, leveraging real-time information from high-fidelity digital twins. First, physical entities 
are transformed into smart stacking objects that combine static inherent properties with real-time dynamic 
activities captured by Ultra-Wideband sensors and Inertial Measurement Units. Then, a digital twin of stacking 
operations is used to interact with their physical counterparts in real time, enabling seamless mapping 
and visualization of the stacking process. Third, the PosTraFormer network is proposed for 6-dimensional 
module pose estimation and trajectory prediction based on spatio-temporal fusion modeling of trajectory point 
relations, to provide stacking guidance and improve installation precision. Both visualization and quantitative 
analysis from comparative experiments and case studies demonstrate the significant advantages of the proposed 
approach.
1. Introduction
As a disruptive innovation, Modular Integrated Construction (MiC) 
is increasingly replacing traditional methods, offering benefits such 
as improved quality, simplified management, and shorter project du-
rations (Pan and Hon, 2020; Zheng et al., 2020). During the MiC 
construction process, efficient module stacking is critical for timely 
assembly, high construction quality, and safety (Shan et al., 2019; Zhai 
et al., 2019). In practice, module stacking still depends on manual ob-
servation and the expertise of crane operators (Zhu et al., 2022), which 
gives rise to several critical challenges. First, crane operators often 
exercise delayed control due to insufficient real-time information for 
accurate perception of module status, thereby increasing the likelihood 
of safety incidents, stacking errors, and module collisions (Kim and Chi, 
2021). Second, the lack of timely and integrated information sharing 
among stakeholders causes delays and fragmentation, which hinder 
coordination and perception across the project team (Zhao et al., 2022).

coming events. Therefore, global perceptual monitoring supported by

simulation techniques often fail to dynamically adapt to changes in 
the physical environment and lack the required level of interconnec-
tivity and coordination for complex tasks such as module stacking. In 
contrast, Digital Twin (DT) technology provides a robust solution by 
replicating physical processes in a virtual environment and integrating 
sensors for real-time monitoring and prediction (Zhou et al., 2019; 
Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Within the DT framework, module 
stacking is redefined to include real-time visibility, cyber–physical 
connectivity, and traceability. Furthermore, a DT is incorporated into 
collaborative platforms that facilitate access to and sharing of module 
information among stakeholders, enhancing information synchroniza-
tion. Consequently, this paper aims to leverage the advantages of DT 
to achieve comprehensive visibility and traceability at module stacking 
sites through real-time data collection and positioning.
Nevertheless, addressing delayed control and achieving predictive 
guidance requires further development of precise methods for module 
pose estimation and trajectory prediction within the DT framework. 
Computer vision-based methods have been proposed by researchers 
and practitioners (Zhang et al., 2019). Yang et al. (2023) suggested 
surveillance cameras for environmental perception and worker trajec-
tory prediction to boost construction safety. Similarly, Cheng et al. 
(2022) introduced a stereo-vision technique for aligning precast con-
crete bridge components. Considering that vision-based methods often 
fail to capture accurate 3D spatial relationships, Xie et al. (2025) 
proposed a framework that leverages point cloud data to detect human–
object interactions on construction sites. However, on-site practices re-
veal that these methods struggle in dynamic and variable construction 
environments, poorly addressing challenges like cluttered backgrounds, 
wind, vibrations, fluctuating lighting, and limited camera angles (Kop-
sida and Brilakis, 2020; Omar and Nehdi, 2016; Liu et al., 2023). In 
contrast, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors can capture high precision, 
multisource stacking data in real-time, offering superior environmental 
adaptability and reliability. Combined with IoT devices, high fidelity 
virtual models in the DT framework can reflect the dynamic state, be-
havior, and changes of the physical stacking system in a comprehensive 
perspective (Zhou et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021).
Current methods for predicting trajectories with IoT sensors pri-
marily use temporal models to analyze historical motion data se-
quences (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang and Ge, 2022). 
These sequential relationships expose the temporal continuity and 
directional trends of motions, enabling more reliable future trajectory 
predictions. However, in complex spatial movements like module ro-
tation or flipping, reliance solely on temporal relationships for the 
module trajectory prediction proves challenging. This is mainly because 
such movements entail multi-dimensional displacement and spatial 
pose adjustments. This difficulty often requires deep learning networks 
with advanced capabilities for handling complex nonlinear problems. 
Recently, deep learning models have achieved outstanding perfor-
mance in multiple domains (Shang et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Gao 
et al., 2025). Specifically, in tasks that involve extracting complex 
spatiotemporal features, Zhang et al. (2024) designed a multi-attention 
network based on spatio-temporal feature fusion to predict pedestrian 
crossing intention.  Ali et al. (2025) proposed a spatio-temporal graph 
convolutional recurrent network with multi-scale feature extraction 
and dual attention mechanisms, achieving superior long-term urban 
traffic prediction on real-world datasets. Wang et al. (2025) recently 
proposed a large language model (LLM)-driven probabilistic trajectory 
prediction framework, which integrates spatio-temporal encoding with 
normalizing flows to model uncertainty and complex motion dynamics. 
Although these methods struggle to represent the complex spatial 
posture changes during the module installation process, they show 
that accurate pose prediction of modules depends crucially on effective 
modeling of spatial relationships and temporal dependencies among 
trajectory points, particularly in complex movements. Spatial structures 
elucidate the distribution and interrelation of points in space, vital for 
deciphering movement patterns. Consequently, this paper introduces 
a DT-enabled module stacking system (DTPTS) with a novel network, 
PosTraFormer, which enhances controllability by modeling the com-
plex spatial posture changes during the module installation process 
through multi-layer graph convolutional networks (GCNs), and by 
estimating posture and predicting trajectories through the integration 
of spatiotemporal modeling.
The lifecycle of MiC is illustrated in Fig.  1. Initially, dynamic spatio-
temporal data from assets involved in the stacking process (such as 
cranes, workers, and modules) are captured using Ultra Wide Bands 
(UWBs) and Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) for enhanced position-
ing accuracy in MiC environments. All real-time data and intrinsic 
characteristics, including geometry and semantics, are updated to the 
Smart Industry Foundation Classes (SIFCs) via the Mobile Gateway 
Operating System (MGOS) for efficient data management. DTPTS en-
ables real-time automatic mapping of stacking activities and unified 
information sharing among stakeholders, while PosTraFormer analyzes 
key stacking factors for enhanced trajectory prediction. Our primary 
contributions are as follows:
(1) A DT platform based on IoT is proposed for real-time perception, 
pose estimation, and bidirectional interoperability between the 
cyber and physical modules in MiC stacking scenarios.
(2) A novel network called PosTraFormer is proposed to model the 
spatial–temporal structure of trajectory points to predict future 
motion trajectories, which is embedded into the DT platform to 
prevent potential structural collision and misaligned stacking.
(3) Comparative studies with the current state-of-the-art trajectory 
prediction models are conducted to validate the feasibility of the 
system and the effectiveness of the proposed method.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 
presents the research related to MiC. Section 3 describes the DTPTS 
framework. Section 4 gives the pose estimation and trajectory predic-
tion algorithm. Comparative experiments are conducted in Section 5. 
Section 6 applies the DTPTS in a real-life MiC construction site and 
validates the effectiveness of our proposed method. Section 7 concludes 
the whole paper and gives the future research directions.
2. Literature review
This section reviews research related to MiC on DT, pose estimation, 
and trajectory prediction. At the end of this section, research gaps are 
summarized.
2.1. DT in MiC
DT has become a revolutionary force for MiC lifecycle management 
to facilitate real-time bidirectional interoperability between physical 
and virtual environments (Nguyen et al., 2025; Zhou, 2025). DT frame-
work with four layers has been widely recognized by researchers as 
the conceptualized workflow which includes the physical layer (data 
acquisition), data layer (data transmission, integration, and preprocess-
ing), virtual layer (digital modeling and data analysis), and service 
layer (visualization) (Tao and Zhang, 2017; Tuhaise et al., 2023; Preg-
nolato et al., 2022). In recent years, studies based on DT have made 
significant progress with the development of information and commu-
nication technology, such as high precision IoT devices, state-of-the-art 
artificial intelligence, robust cloud computing, secure blockchain, and 
scalable big data analytics (Rangasamy and Yang, 2024; Q. Liu et al., 
2024). Representative papers can be roughly categorized into four 
aspects according to different MiC processes: (1) Design and planning; 
(2) Transportation scheduling; (3) Construction and management; (4) 
Operation and Maintenance.

(1) Design and planning. During the design phase, DT technology 
can optimize MiC design schemes through three-dimensional 
modeling and simulation analysis under the unified digital de-
sign platform for multiple design stakeholders. Building Informa-
tion Modeling (BIM) related 3D software has been extensively 
utilized to support the design generation and optimization for 
the MiC industry due to its advantages in the virtual represen-
tation of building geometries and semantics. For example, Li 
et al. (2024) integrated BIM and graphical convolutional neural 
networks to automatically generate prefabricated space layouts 
by extracting paired spatial geometric features. J. Liu et al. 
(2024) proposed a modular high-rise residential building de-
sign method based on graph constrained generative adversar-
ial networks considering domain specific knowledge and con-
straints. Kosse et al. (2025) developed a modular semantic DT 
architecture to support knowledge integration and planning in 
construction projects. Li et al. (2025) proposed LSDTs, which 
integrate LLMs with DT to extract knowledge from unstruc-
tured documents and enable regulation-aware, high-fidelity, and 
adaptable infrastructure planning.
(2) Transportation scheduling. DT technology can eliminate trans-
portation scheduling deviations by simulating logistics processes 
to keep synchronized with construction progress. Radio Fre-
quency Identification (RFID) and Global Positioning System 
(GPS) are primarily used in most research for locating and 
recording the information of transport vehicles and modules 
and coordinating the supply chain in conjunction with on-site 
construction progress. Lee and Lee (Lee and Lee, 2021) devel-
oped a hybrid geographic information system framework with 
DT and IoT for real-time module logistics simulation and supply 
chain coordination, which predicts potential logistics risks and 
accurate module arrival times to reduce the module accumu-
lation at the construction site. Tan et al. (2025) applied DT 
to prefabricated component factories, optimizing manufacturing 
and logistics processes.
(3) Construction and management. Hoisting safety is a critical 
research domain in prefabricated construction. Liu et al. (2021) 
proposed a safety risk coupling model using multiple data col-
lection terminals and the apriori algorithm to mine associated 
risks, while Zhao et al. (2022) applied the Dijkstra algorithm for 
hoisting path planning with IMUs, GPS, and RFIDs, supported 
by LoRa-based data transmission. Beyond hoisting-specific stud-
ies, Jiang et al. (2020) developed an IFC-based BIM model for 
comprehensive safety management in mega projects, and Yuan 
et al. (2016) introduced a cyber–physical system to monitor 
temporary structures against failures. Other efforts have ex-
plored advanced human–machine interaction, such as Hasan 
et al. (2022), who linked augmented reality with DT to en-
hance equipment interaction. Building on this, DT technology 
has also been applied to MiC on-site assembly, where Jiang et al. 
(2022a,b) developed systems for real-time monitoring, planning, 
and execution using UWBs and RFIDs. More recently, Akha-
vian et al. (2025) proposed BIM2RDT, an AI-driven framework 
that converts BIM into a robot-ready DT to support automated 
operations and safety management.
(4) Operation and maintenance. In the maintenance process, DT 
technology enables intelligent management of the entire build-
ing lifecycle through real-time monitoring and predictive main-
tenance. A large number of studies about indoor comfort and 
energy conservation have been extensively reported. For in-
stance, Tan et al. (2022) proposed a visualized DT lighting O&M 
system to support timely adaptive adjustment of ambient bright-
ness considering pedestrians detected by YOLOv4. Hosamo et al. 
(2022) conducted a DT predictive maintenance framework for 
stable air handling unit functioning in heating, ventilation, and 
air conditioning (HVAC) systems in which analysis of variance 
and support vector machine are integrated for condition predic-
tion. Tagliabue et al. (2021) focused on the indoor air quality 
problem and utilized IoT devices for real-time indoor air condi-
tion monitoring to control the HVAC systems and define open-
ing/closing patterns for windows. Furthermore, Lu et al. (2020) 
proposed an anomaly detection system based on DT for asset 
monitoring which is based on IFC data integration. DT also sup-
ports emergency management by providing real-time monitoring 
and simulation drills for emergency plans, enhancing building 
safety and emergency response capabilities. For instance, Cheng 
et al. (2017) constructed a BIM based intelligent fire prevention 
system in which Bluetooth-based sensors and mobile apps are 
used for personal localization and relief guidance. Cheng et al. 
(2025) constructed high-fidelity multi-physics building models 
for multi-energy system analysis, enabling precise simulation 
and optimization of building operations. Additionally, DT tech-
nology has broad and effective applications in advanced project 
management (Pan and Zhang, 2021), structural health monitor-
ing (Tuhaise et al., 2023), and information sharing (Lee et al., 
2021).

2.2. Pose estimation
Pose estimation aims to accurately describe the position and rota-
tion of an object in three-dimensional space. It has been widely used in 
many scenarios such as autonomous driving (Raffin et al., 2021), aug-
mented reality (Song et al., 2018), and industrial robotics (Peng et al., 
2022). To effectively improve efficiency in a complex, dynamic site 
environment with diverse equipment, many researchers have paid a lot 
of attention to the application in construction scenarios. These studies 
can be broadly categorized into two main types: non-visual sensor-
based techniques (Azar et al., 2015) and vision-based techniques (Han 
and Lee, 2013; Han et al., 2013; Żywanowski et al., 2025).
Vision-based techniques use cameras to capture visual information, 
which can be further divided into marker-based and marker-less meth-
ods. For the marker-based method, tags are susceptible to damage 
due to harsh construction environments or equipment intense move-
ment (Lundeen et al., 2016). In contrast, the marker-less methods 
are more suitable for the on-site environment of MiC. Liang et al. 
(2019) proposed a stacked hourglass network for marker-less excavator 
pose estimation to perform proximity detection and object tracking 
on construction sites. Tian et al. (2023) combined internet data and 
cinema4D generated data to construct a framework for real-time 3D 
position estimation of excavators. Recently, Fu et al. (2025) proposed 
a synthetic vision data-enhanced method for automated 3D pose recog-
nition of construction workers, improving pose estimation accuracy 
and robustness in complex construction environments. Additionally, 
pose estimation can be found in other aspects of construction sites, 
including excavated objects, trucks, workers (Yu et al., 2021), and 
other equipment. However, these vision-based methods still cannot 
adapt to the complex dynamic environment of construction sites, as 
the accuracy of pose estimation is easily affected by variable and 
insufficient lighting conditions, object occlusion, and adverse weather 
conditions (Tang et al., 2023). Secondly, the coverage of construction 
scenes can only be achieved through the strategic placement of multiple 
cameras (Partsinevelos et al., 2024). Thirdly, rigorous system calibra-
tion at regular intervals is necessary to ensure precise camera alignment 
and the consistency of depth information. In such cases, the system 
needs to process a large amount of image data, consuming significant 
computational resources.
To address the shortcomings of vision-based pose estimation in 
construction, many researchers have increasingly focused on non-visual 
sensor-based technologies, which can be further categorized into single-
sensor methods and sensor fusion methods. For example, Tang et al. 
(2022) proposed an IMU-based approach for unconstrained whole-
body machines pose estimation in construction sites and provided 
the optimal IMU installation strategy. Liu et al. (2025) proposed the 
UMotion framework, which fuses six IMUs and UWB sensors using an 
Unscented Kalman Filter to achieve robust and accurate 3D human 
pose estimation. However, IMU cumulative errors may lead to the 
problem of pose drift over time. Methods based on GPS are prone to 
problems such as signal blocking and occlusion in the on-site assembly 
of MiC due to the lack of penetration into concrete (Groves, 2011). 
Meanwhile, the multipath effect can lead to large positioning errors. Ra-
dio frequency methods, such as Wireless Local Area Network (WLAN), 
UWBs, and RFIDs, lack orientation information for a single application. 
Compared with the limitations of single-sensor methods, the sensor 
fusion methods may provide a new solution for pose estimation in MiC 
on-site assembly, which allows for the integration of multiple sensors 
and the fusion of multimodal data. Although sensor fusion methods 
have achieved excellent results, the effects of lost data and signal 
interference should be considered, and further search for robust fusion 
algorithms to address calibration and synchronization between sensors 
should be made.
In conclusion, preliminary exploration has been conducted on the 
pose estimation for physical assets in construction sites. However, 
there is little research on module pose estimation for the on-site in-
stallation process. Through the literature review mentioned above, an 
innovative method based on sensor fusion can be found for module 
pose estimation to facilitate the correct alignment, connection, and 
positioning of modules. In addition, deep learning methods can help 
solve computational difficulties when fusing multimodal data due to 
their ability to efficiently process various types of data and extract 
valuable features (Brachmann et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2021).
2.3. Trajectory prediction
The trajectory prediction aims to take a sequence of historical 
trajectories as input and outputs a predicted trajectory for a future 
period. Many researchers have integrated trajectory prediction into 
applications in architectural scenes, thereby reducing the occurrence 
of risk incidents. Trajectory prediction methods can be categorized into 
four types: physics-based methods, classical machine learning methods, 
deep learning methods, and reinforcement learning methods. Recently, 
data-driven methods are increasingly being utilized as they do not 
require explicit modeling of motion dynamics and can be generalized to 
various scenarios (Hu et al., 2020). Indeed, Long Short-Term Memory 
(LSTM) is one of the most widely used deep learning methods in 
trajectory prediction (Xue et al., 2018). Yang et al. (2023) proposed a 
framework for predicting worker trajectories based on LSTM networks, 
which comprehensively considers the interactions between workers and 
the interactions between workers and the environment. Hu et al. (2020) 
proposed an A* algorithm and dynamic window approach for finding 
the optimal path for construction robots and integrated the worker 
trajectory prediction method based on LSTM into the existing robot 
platform to enhance its performance potential. Zhang and Ge (2022) 
applied a trajectory prediction model based on the Transformer to pre-
dict the trajectories of loads and workers for the next ten seconds and 
established an early warning model. Further advancing deep learning 
for construction trajectory prediction, a novel framework employing a 
Transformer encoder-decoder with graph attention networks was devel-
oped to model worker temporal interactions and spatial relationships, 
achieving low error in short-term predictions (Alduais et al., 2025). 
However, they only considered the trajectories on the plane position 
and ignored the impact of the height during the object movement 
process. Nevertheless, several research gaps are identified from the 
literature review:
(1) DT technology for the module stacking process to enhance pre-
cision, coordination, and visualization is scarcely reported.
(2) Limited study explores multimodal data-based module pose es-
timation algorithms during MiC on-site stacking.
(3) Trajectory prediction in complex environments needs to be sys-
tematically analyzed.
Taking into account a certain spatial and temporal relationship 
of the trajectories of the lifting weights, this paper presents a DT-
enabled pose estimation and trajectory prediction network based on 
spatio-temporal feature fusion modeling to fill these research gaps.
3. DT-enabled module stacking management system (DTPTS)
To overcome the challenges in MiC on-site stacking, this section 
proposes a DT framework to realize the interoperability of MiC stacking 
guidance, as shown in Fig.  2. The framework is divided into three 
layers: the physical layer, the virtual layer, and the service layer. The 
assets on site are embedded with smart IoT sensors for intelligent 
perception. Multidimensional and multiscale data of the static intrinsic 
properties and dynamic activities are captured by the IoT sensors. Then 
these data are uploaded to the IFC-based virtual layer via MGOS for the 
high-fidelity DT modeling. The service layer provides comprehensive 
stacking task management for project managers, stacking guidance for 
crane drivers, and collision warning for workers.

3.1. Physical layer
The MiC stacking process is primarily composed of four main physi-
cal entities: assembly site environment, modules, equipment, and work-
ers. On-site entities are embedded with intelligent IoT sensors to fa-
cilitate real-time data acquisition, dynamic perception, information 
sharing, and command acceptance for improving installation precision.
(1) Assembly site environment. Given the complexity and vari-
ability of actual assembly site, achieving a fully one-to-one 
replication within a digital environment for module stacking 
remains an unattainable objective. Therefore, critical environ-
mental components that significantly impact module assembly 
are considered to ensure the DT model effectively balances the 
modeling complexity and actual assembly scenario mapping. The 
integration of two critical components, including static terrain 
and dynamic environmental changes, is essential. The terrain 
contours and existing road networks determine the layout of the 
buffer, crane, and modular building, as well as the transportation 
routes for module trailers. Surrounding permanent buildings in-
fluence the operational range of cranes. These static terrains are 
non-invasively captured by unmanned aerial vehicles equipped 
with cameras and laser scanners, supplemented by terrain data 
acquired from Google Earth Engine. Dynamic environmental 
changes, such as temperature, wind speed, and ground vibration, 
are captured in real-time by IoT sensors deployed on-site to 
mitigate the precision effect of pose estimation and trajectory 
prediction.
(2) Modules. Intelligent sensors integrating RFIDs, UWBs, and IMUs 
are embedded in modules before assembly to capture both static 
properties and dynamic poses. Globally unique identifiers, ge-
ometry, material types, weight, and connection methods are 
recorded in RFIDs. This information is crucial for module align-
ment, balancing the stacking center of gravity, selecting appro-
priate lifting equipment, and adjusting control parameters. To 
model spatio-temporal characteristics for pose estimation and 
trajectory prediction, accurate and real-time data acquisition of 
the module position and attitude is essential. UWB measures 
position with high precision and resistance to interference which 
is suitable for complex environments. However, UWB has lim-
ited capability in sensing rotational dimensions. IMU employs 
accelerometers and gyroscopes to provide high frequency and 
real-time measurements of acceleration and angular velocity, 
enabling precise tracking of rapidly changing dynamic postures. 
Nonetheless, IMU is prone to drift, which can lead to cumula-
tive measurement errors and reduced localization accuracy. To 
address the inherent deficiencies of UWBs and IMUs, this study 
employs a Kalman filter to complementarily correct IMU drift 
and UWB’s limited directional sensing capability. Additionally,

(3) Equipment. The tower crane is the most crucial piece of machin-

radius, maximum load capacity, and maximum hook height, can 
be accurately obtained from technical handbooks and equipment

tionally, the tower crane integrates a human-machine interface 
that displays the module status in real-time and synchronized 
cyber–physical visibility. This interface also visualizes the pose 
estimation and trajectory prediction of the modules to guide 
crane operators in the stacking operations. Other construction 
equipment, such as module unloading forklifts, trailers, exca-

prevent potential collisions with on-site workers.
(4) Workers. Industrial wearables, including smart wristbands,
smart helmets, and visualization interfaces, are equipped for 
workers. Static properties such as the worker’s ID, department, 
age, weight, skill level, professional qualifications, and safety 
training records are stored in the smart wristband. Multi-functi-
onal IoT sensors in industrial wearables continuously update and 
monitor real-time location, working duration, heart rate, fatigue 
level, and relative distance to other entities.
(5) MGOS. LoRaWAN network communication protocol is used to 
define how IoT devices are connected and how data is uploaded. 
Star network architecture is adopted for simplified network man-
agement, maintenance, and expansion. MGOS updates the static 
properties and dynamic properties of the other four entities to 
the virtual layer in real time and transports control commands 
to the physical level for real-time interoperability.
3.2. Virtual layer
The virtual layer mainly realizes the unified management of the data 
uploaded by the physical layer and establishes the high fidelity DT of 
the stacking process.
3.2.1. Unified data management based on IFC
The multi-dimensional static and dynamic data in the stacking pro-
cess are often uploaded and stored in different formats with different 
sensors. Manually extracting and processing diversified and fragmented 
stacking information is not only extremely time consuming but also 
hinders information sharing. Therefore, a unified standard building 
data architecture is needed to integrate and process fragmented data. 
IFC, as a widely adopted industrial data format, is considered for the 
unified DT construction and intelligent data management, as shown in 
Fig.  3. Firstly, the data of various aspects of the stacking process is 
organized with the ‘‘IfcRoot’’ entity as the root node to better describe 
the relationships and hierarchical structure. For objects involved in 
the MiC hoisting process, such as environment, workers, modules, 
and equipment, ‘‘IfcObjectDefinition’’ is used for definition, and the 
inherited description property is further explained for objects. This 
mainly includes static predefined properties and dynamic expandable 
properties. These static predefined properties come from the initial 
design model of the project and are visually constructed in 3D through 
Computer Aided Design (CAD) software (such as AutoCAD, Revit, 
and Solidworks). Dynamic properties refer to all dynamic activity 
information from the buffer zone to installation completion, which 
is collected in real-time by on-site IoT devices and pointed to the 
corresponding data position for updating and storage. The relationships 
between various objects are defined in detail using ‘‘IfcRelationship’’ 
to describe the bidirectional association. For instance, workers are 
usually responsible for installing, moving, or handling modules, and 
the ‘‘IfcRelAssignsToActor’’ can be used to associate modules with 
workers to describe which workers are responsible for specific modules. 
Cranes are typically used to move modules from the buffer zone to the 
installation position. The ‘‘IfcRelAssignsToCrane’’ relationship can be 
used to aggregate modules with cranes. Furthermore, IFC-compatible 
interfaces and application programming interfaces are developed to 
facilitate access and real-time modification of data by various types 
of software and visualization pages in the service layer. Real-time 
synchronization and consistency of data are ensured to support the 
interoperability of the DT.
3.2.2. Multi-dimensional high fidelity DT
The MiC stacking instance is a virtual mapping of physical stacking, 
synchronously inheriting the original parameters of physical stacking. 
Any changes in physical stacking will have real-time corresponding 
impacts on the respective MiC instances. DT modeling includes static 
and dynamic modeling. Dynamic modeling is the most complex stage, 
which will be comprehensive across the entire on-site assembly process 
and will need to consider a wide range of contingencies and inter asset 
interactions. It consists of four main components: (1) Dynamic envi-
ronment modeling: Environmental factors are important for stacking 
accuracy, and sensors are used to accurately capture environmental 
variations (e.g., temperature, humidity, and windiness) and map them 
to the virtual environment. (2) Asset dynamic sensing: dynamic infor-
mation of modules, workers, and cranes is transmitted to the cloud 
data via MGOS and reflected on the corresponding asset models in the 
virtual environment in real-time. (3) Asset interaction: The interaction 
and collaborative behaviors among assets are crucial for achieving 
installation accuracy. Three main interactions are considered in this 
section. The first one is the interaction between cranes and modules. 
The sensors of the crane and module need to provide their respective 
dynamic information at the same time stamp to facilitate the stacking 
adjustment based on the position of the module. The second one is 
the interaction between workers and cranes. The workers need to 
communicate with crane drivers in real-time to identify and deal with 
dynamic problems. The third one is the interaction between workers 
and modules. Workers need to assist in installing the module to the 
designated position and make necessary fixes and connections accord-
ing to the module installation progress. (4) Emergency response: The 
DT system simulates and evaluates various possible risk scenarios, such 
as sudden change of wind, equipment failure, etc., as well as provides 
an emergency response plan. This helps to identify potential risks in 
advance and take responsive measures to mitigate losses.

4.2.2. Spatial structural relationship modeling
In a set of trajectory points 𝑋 = { 𝑋1, 𝑋2, … , 𝑋𝑁 }, there exists 
an inherent spatial structure relationship between the points, which 
is crucial for accurately predicting the subsequent trajectory points. 
Traditional methods may overlook these intricate spatial dependencies, 
leading to suboptimal predictions. To address this, we employ a graph 
model to capture the spatial relationships among the trajectory points, 
enabling a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pat-
terns. As shown in Fig.  6, the trajectory points are encoded into a graph 
structure, where each point 𝑋𝑖 is represented as a node in the graph. 
The edges between these nodes, which signify the spatial relationships, 
are defined by an adjacency matrix 𝐴. Unlike fixed adjacency matrices, 
we utilize an adaptive approach where 𝐴 is a learnable parameter 
matrix. This allows the model to dynamically adjust the connections 
between nodes, thus better capturing the complex spatial dependencies 
present in the data. The encoded trajectory points are then fed into an 
adaptive GCN with multiple layers. The graph convolution operation at 
each layer can be expressed as: 
𝐻 (𝑙+1) = 𝜎 (̃𝐴𝐻 (𝑙)𝑊 (𝑙)) (16)
where 𝐻 (𝑙) is the feature matrix of the trajectory points at layer 𝑙, ̃𝐴 is

representations, enabling the extraction of spatial structure features:

The adaptive nature of the adjacency matrix ensures that the model can 
effectively learn and capture the spatial dependencies, leading to more 
accurate trajectory predictions.

5. Experiments
5.1. Experimental setting
5.1.1. Experimental scenarios and data preparation
To verify the feasibility of the DTPTS an experimental platform 
for hoisting pose estimation and trajectory prediction is conducted in 
a real laboratory setting, as shown in Fig.  7. The setup comprises a 
physical stacking site, a digital stacking site, and an intelligent data 
management system. The physical stacking site mainly includes a buffer 
zone for storing modules, a building under construction, cranes, mod-
ules, workers, gateway, and base stations. The buffer zone measures 
0.7 m in length and 0.35 m in width, capable of storing up to 16 
modules. The building model follows the mainstream Podium System 
construction method, standing 1.35 m tall with 15 floors, including a 
podium section at the bottom and a framework section above. Base 
stations are strategically deployed around the building. Each room is 
modular and demountable, closely resembling real construction instal-
lation scenarios. Additionally, every module embeds intelligent RFID 
tags, UWBs, and IMUs. The smart crane has an operating radius of 
1 m, a maximum payload of 10 kg, and a lifting speed of 0.1 m/s. A 
gateway is mounted on the crane hook to capture real-time data from 
modules and transmit control commands to the tower crane. The digital 
stacking site is developed by WebGL, which serves as the DT of the 
aforementioned physical assets. To facilitate unified management of di-
verse structural data, a ‘‘Trinity’’ platform is developed, encompassing 
intelligent modules, workers, and cranes.
The entire trajectory from hoisting the module in the buffer zone 
to placing it at the designated position is sampled at 2 Hz. A sliding 
window of fixed length is used to trim the trajectory starting from 
every alternate time step of the original trajectory (specifically, 5 s, 
10 frames). Each trimmed trajectory is divided into two parts: the 
historical trajectory (input) and the predicted trajectory (output or la-
bel), totaling 1200 trimmed trajectories. In the experiment, the dataset 
is split into training and evaluation sets in a 7:3 ratio. The training 
set is utilized to optimize the parameters of the trajectory prediction 
model, while the evaluation set monitors the convergence of the model. 
Additionally, the module storage and movement status, construction 
progress of each building layer, and occupancy status of the tower 
crane are automatically updated at the same timestamp during this 
process. Dynamic activity data of workers, such as their movement 
paths and actions on the construction site, are captured via smart 
wristbands and helmets. All the aforementioned data is integrated into 
the SIFC database and subjected to data calibration and synchronization 
to ensure consistency and accuracy among various data sources. To 
address possible jitter and anomalies in the collected data, Kalman 
filtering is employed for smoothing and noise removal. Polynomial 
interpolation is utilized to handle missing values. Finally, to standardize 
the scale of position and orientation data for model training, the data 
is normalized.
5.1.2. Data processing
In this section, we describe the process of augmenting the lifting 
dataset through data augmentation techniques to ensure that various 
possible lifting scenarios are covered during model training, including 
normal lifting trajectories as well as anomalous situations (e.g., sen-
sor malfunctions). As a result, the dataset size was expanded from a 
few hundred trajectories to 6000 trajectories. The data augmentation 
methods primarily include two techniques: using Time-Series Genera-
tive Adversarial Network (TimeGAN) to simulate real lifting data and 
injecting anomalous trajectory points into certain trajectory data using 
noise.
Real normal stacking data simulation. TimeGAN is used to gen-
erate samples that resemble the distribution of real lifting data. The 
generator of TimeGAN learns the temporal patterns of the real data, 
thus simulating new trajectory data. This approach effectively enhances 
the diversity of the dataset, especially when the original data is limited. 
Specifically, we input real lifting data into the TimeGAN model and 
train the generator to produce trajectory point sequences similar to 
the real data. After several iterations, the generator can output a large 
amount of new data that is temporally similar to the real data. To 
validate the effectiveness of the generated data, we compared the

5.5.1. Model hyper-parameters and training
All baseline models and the proposed PosTraFormer network were 
executed on a server equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce 4090 GPU. All 
models were constructed using the PyTorch deep learning framework. 
The baseline models include the FairMOT (Zhang and Ge, 2022), LSTM, 
Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory 
(BiLSTM), as well as LSTM-Attention, GRU-Attention, and BiLSTM-
Attention. The hidden layer dimension for all models was set to 64, 
and the batch size was set to 32. The dropout layer with a dropout 
probability of 0.1 was employed to prevent overfitting. The ReLU 
activation function was used for all models. The epoch size for all 
methods was set to 300. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001, 
and the Adam optimizer was utilized, resulting in dynamic adjustment 
of the learning rate at each epoch based on the model’s performance 
and loss function. All methods employed the smooth L1 loss as the 
loss function for model training. For the PosTraFormer, the number of 
encoder and decoder layers was set to 2, the number of self-attention 
heads was set to 2, and the dimension of the feedforward network 
within each layer was set to 64.

faster, the oscillations are more significant in the early stage of training.

5.5.2. Comparative experiments in different scenarios
During the actual module installation process, workers assist the 
crane operators in securing the hook onto the module. The crane lifts 
modules from the buffer zone to a certain height to avoid obstacles. 
Subsequently, the crane rotates to transfer the module above the target 
area, where the module undergoes translation and rotation. The winch 
mechanism then controls the gradual descent of the module. At the tar-
get position, construction workers rotate the module to the appropriate 
angle and assist the crane in moving the module to its final position. 
Consequently, the hoisting process involves different scenarios with 
distinct trajectories. To enhance the adaptability of the DTPTS system 
to various lifting processes and improve the accuracy and robustness of 
module trajectory prediction, we conducted real-time pose estimation 
and trajectory prediction tests for different scenarios. In this study, four 
stacking scenarios were selected: (1) Module lifting process, (2) Module 
rotation and translation process, (3) Module descent process, and (4) 
Module self-rotation and adjustment process. The schematic diagrams 
for the four scenarios are illustrated in Fig.  13. To fully evaluate the 
effectiveness and robustness of the models, visualization 3D module 
lifting trajectory comparison and quantitative analysis were performed 
separately.

5.5.3. Complexity analysis
To assess the feasibility of deploying the PosTraFormer in actual 
applications, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of its compu-
tational efficiency relative to advanced methods. Our evaluation fo-
cused on critical performance metrics that are paramount in scenarios 
where computational resources and runtime efficiency are essential. 
The experimental framework encompassed various tests designed to 
measure key indicators such as the number of parameters (Params), 
floating point operations (FLOPs), and frames per second (FPS). FLOPs 
represent the number of floating point operations executed during 
task performance. FPS measures the number of frames processed per 
second. This metric is particularly critical for applications requiring 
real-time processing. In our context, given that the data involves three-
dimensional position coordinates and poses, FPS specifically refers 
to the number of samples the model processes per second. This is 
quantified by measuring how many samples are processed by the model 
within a stipulated timeframe. Our findings, presented in Table  5, 
reveal that the PosTraFormer possesses a significantly larger number 
of parameters compared to other models evaluated. This extensive 
parameterization, while crucial for capturing complex spatio-temporal 
relationships, does impose a notable impact on the model’s compu-
tational efficiency. Specifically, the increased number of parameters 
correlates with a reduction in the FPS (frames per second) rate, making 
the PosTraFormer the slowest among the tested models in terms of 
data processing speed. Despite this limitation, it is noteworthy that the 
PosTraFormer still manages to achieve an FPS of 288.8f/s. Although 
this is lower compared to other models, it remains sufficient for meeting 
the real-time processing demands of many applications, while also 
providing a high level of accuracy. Thus, while the large parameter 
count of the PosTraFormer presents certain challenges, particularly in 
terms of speed, its ability to fulfill real-time requirements alongside 
maintaining accuracy renders it a considerable option for deployment 
in practical settings where complex data relationships are prevalent.

5.6. Ablation study
To verify the effectiveness of the various components of the pro-
posed PosTraFormer model, we conducted an ablation study. This 
study involved comparing the performance of the full version of the 
model with two ablated versions: one without spatial structure mod-
eling (W/O Spatial structure modeling) and another without tempo-
ral relationship modeling (W/O Temporal relationship modeling). The 
comparison was made using the FDE and ADE metrics across the four 
stacking scenarios. Table  6 presents the comparison results, showing 
that the full version of PosTraFormer achieved the best performance. 
Especially, the module rotation and translation process and self-rotation 
and adjustment process involve complex spatial movements, requiring 
precise spatial structure modeling to capture the module’s orientation 
and position changes. The version without spatial structure modeling 
(W/O Spatial structure modeling) performed poorly here, highlighting 
the need for accurate spatial understanding to predict the trajectory 
effectively. Furthermore, the module lifting and descending process 
heavily depends on temporal factors, with minimal spatial complexity. 
The absence of temporal modeling led to higher errors, confirming 
that temporal relationship modeling plays a critical role in this stage. 
These results underscore the importance of both spatial and temporal 
modeling components in accurately predicting lifting trajectories and 
demonstrate the superiority of the complete PosTraFormer architecture.

7. Conclusions
In this paper, we proposed a framework for MiC onsite assembly 
process services based on DT. On the one hand, real-time pose esti-
mation information of modules is obtained through UWBs and IMUs 
sensors. On the other hand, a novel spatial–temporal feature fusion 
modeling network named PosTraFormer is proposed for trajectory pre-
diction during the module installation process. This framework aims 
to provide guidance and monitoring services for relevant stakeholders 
during the stacking process. Furthermore, due to the scarcity of tra-
jectory datasets in existing literature for module stacking processes, 
we collected data based on physical simulation models, constructing 
a dataset of module pose and trajectory during the installation pro-
cess, and enhancing it through sliding window techniques. Through 
the comparative experiments and case studies, it can be observed 
that our proposed PosTraFormer achieves higher trajectory prediction 
performance. This renders it suitable for construction environments, 
enhancing accuracy and minimizing collision risks during the module 
stacking process. Additionally, real-time pose estimation based on IMUs 
and UWBs can reflect the position and orientation of modules in 
building scenarios, enabling construction site workers and managers 
to monitor building dynamics in real-time, and facilitating operational 
management.
