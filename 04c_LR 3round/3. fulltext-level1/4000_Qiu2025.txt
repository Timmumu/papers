LiDAR-Simulated Multimodal and Self-Supervised
Contrastive Digital Twin Approach for Probabilistic Point
Cloud Generation of Rail Fasteners
Shi Qiu, Ph.D. 1; Qasim Zaheer, S.M.ASCE 2; S. Muhammad Ahmed Hassan Shah, S.M.ASCE 3;
Syed Faizan Hussain Shah 4; Weidong Wang, Ph.D. 5; Chengbo Ai, Ph.D. 6; and Jin Wang, Ph.D. 7
Abstract: This study presents a novel deep-learning framework designed to efficiently generate high-fidelity three-dimensional (3D) point
clouds of rail fasteners. The proposed method overcomes limitations associated with traditional light detection and ranging (LiDAR)
technology, including high cost and incomplete data acquisition. The framework utilizes transformers and Bayesian neural networks for
self-supervised contrastive learning, enabling real-time 3D point cloud generation from depth and grayscale images. This approach fosters
automation and efficiency in maintenance tasks by producing accurate geometric reconstructions of rail fasteners. Beyond 3D generation, the
framework learns latent representations of two-dimensional (2D) data, potentially impacting downstream tasks. This research contributes
significantly to the development of digital twins in infrastructure monitoring by providing a cost-effective and efficient pathway for real-time
3D data generation. The proposed methodology holds promise for enhancing safety, reliability, and cost-effectiveness across a broad spectrum
of infrastructure applications. DOI: 10.1061/JCCEE5.CPENG-6137. © 2025 American Society of Civil Engineers.

Introduction
In recent years, the fields of deep learning and 3D reconstruction
have seen remarkable advancements, leading to significant break-
throughs and practical applications across various industries. One
sector that has notably reaped the benefits of these innovations is
civil infrastructure inspection, where regular monitoring of railway
infrastructure is paramount for ensuring construction quality,
safety, and maintenance ( Spencer et al. 2019 ). Central to this as-
surance is the regular inspection of critical components, among
which rail fasteners play a pivotal role (see Fig. 1). These fasteners
secure rails to the base and are crucial for maintaining track gauges.
Any failure in these components can lead to catastrophic derail-
ments and accidents (Akbari and Cammi 2024). In this regard, light
detection and ranging (LiDAR) technology has played a crucial
role in this domain by providing high-accuracy 3D point clouds
for inspection (Prieto et al. 2024). Despite its effectiveness, the high
cost and complexity of LiDAR systems have limited their wide-
spread use, particularly in smaller railway networks. Consequently,
there has been growing interest in exploring alternative solutions,
such as image-based approaches and deep-learning techniques, to
overcome these limitations ( Chastre et al. 2023 ).
The journey of 3D reconstruction technology began with foun-
dational developments in computer vision and graphics, aimed at
modeling and visualizing three-dimensional objects and environ-
ments. This technology extends beyond mere visual representation,
offering precise measurements, positional data, and range informa-
tion (Kim et al. 2022 ). Such detailed information is invaluable not
only for inspection and analysis but also for creating “digital
twins”—highly accurate 3D models integrated with supplementary
data ( Hoskere et al. 2022 ). These digital twins enable human in-
teraction and even predictive analysis through simulations via
3D replicas of the main component ( Agarwal et al. 2021 ). Histor-
ically, techniques such as structure from motion (SfM) ( Nassar and
Jung 2012) and multiview stereo (MVS) (Ma et al. 2023) have been
instrumental in 3D reconstruction. These methods have continually
improved in terms of accuracy and robustness, supported by a range
of open-source and commercial software tools ( Hosken 2021 ;
Kattan et al. 2022) that provide comprehensive solutions for image-
based 3D reconstruction ( Maalek and Lichti 2021 ; Nyimbili et al.
2016; Pepe and Costantino 2021 ; Sani et al. 2022 ; Xie et al. 2023 ).

The integration of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) post-
2015 marked a significant shift, with MVSNet exemplifying the
impact of deep learning on 3D reconstruction ( Yao et al. 2018 ).
The integration of object detection and semantic segmentation
within reconstruction processes promises further improvements
in accuracy and efficiency ( Han et al. 2021 ). Challenges remain,
including achieving high accuracy in complex scenes and scaling
for large data sets. Real-time reconstruction for dynamic scenes,
crucial for augmented reality and robotics, is a key research focus.
Integration with sensor fusion (LiDAR, radar) and semantic under-
standing is expected to expand 3D reconstruction ’s applications
across various fields.
Deep learning has revolutionized image-based 3D reconstruction,
overcoming the limitations of traditional methods ( Lu et al. 2024 ).
Early CNN-based techniques such as temporally invariant learned
detector (TILDE) ( V erdie et al. 2015 ) and DeepDesc ( Simo-Serra
et al. 2015 ) initially focused on feature extraction and matching
but were limited in accuracy and efficiency. To enhance point cloud
quality, supervised learning approaches such as PointCleanNet
(Rakotosaona et al. 2020 ) emerged, effectively denoising point
clouds—critical for accurate reconstructions. However, these
methods rely on large labeled data sets, which are costly and
time-consuming. Unsupervised alternatives such as total denoising
(Casajus et al. 2019) offer promise but struggle to match supervised
performance. Advances in deep learning have also improved seman-
tic and instance segmentation through techniques such as PointNet
(Chen et al. 2019 ) and Pix2V ox ( Xie et al. 2019 ), enhancing the
understanding of object shapes and relationships within scenes
for more precise 3D reconstructions. Y et, challenges remain in
generalizing these techniques to diverse object shapes and materials,
especially in complex environments (Xu et al. 2021). The extraction
of 2D information from images has further strengthened
reconstruction methods, with techniques such as 3D-R2N2 ( Leibe
et al. 2016 ; Hong et al. 2022 ) utilizing neural networks to generate
3D representations from 2D data. However, reconstructing occluded
or challenging viewpoints remains difficult, prompting the need for
better integration of geometric priors and multiview data. Implicit
neural representations such as NeRF ( Remondino et al. 2023)s h o w
promise for efficient, continuous geometry modeling, prioritizing
speed, quality, and scalability, though they require substantial com-
putational resources. Data fusion, integrating information from
multiple sensors such as cameras and LiDAR, offers another avenue
for enhancing 3D reconstruction accuracy and robustness ( Ma and
Liu 2018; Li et al. 2023). While this approach compensates for indi-
vidual sensor limitations and enriches the data, it also introduces
complexity, requiring powerful hardware and advanced algorithms
to resolve conflicts between sensor data ( Cabral et al. 2023 ).
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have automated 3D
reconstruction processes, significantly enhancing accuracy and
efficiency in infrastructure inspection. Image-based 3D models en-
able engineers to inspect bridges, buildings, and other structures
with greater precision, detecting damage that might elude visual
inspection ( Zhang 2020 ). Additionally, 3D reconstruction facili-
tates construction monitoring, allowing for progress tracking,
deviation detection, and optimization of schedules ( Son and Kim
2010). In the aftermath of natural disasters, these models are crucial
for rapid damage assessment, enabling swift response and recovery
(Zhou et al. 2016 ). The integration of 3D models with sensor data
into digital twins offers valuable insights into infrastructure health,
supporting predictive maintenance and asset management ( Torok
et al. 2014 ). However, challenges such as real-time generation
speed, high costs, sensor noise, and complex parameter require-
ments persist. To address these, continued research into data fusion
and deep learning is essential.
Therefore, this paper presents a novel deep-learning methodol-
ogy for generating high-fidelity 3D point clouds of rail fasteners.
This framework transcends the limitations of traditional LiDAR by
effectively utilizing ubiquitous data sources —depth and grayscale
images. This shift toward readily available data offers a significant
cost advantage over existing methods. The core of our contribution
lies in the synergistic application of transformers ( Islam et al. 2024;
Martin-Gutierrez et al. 2021 ) and Bayesian neural networks
(Lampinen and V ehtari 2001 ; Mullachery et al. 2018
; Xue et al.
2022). This powerful combination facilitates self-supervised con-
trastive learning ( Jaiswal et al. 2021 ; Liu et al. 2023 ), enabling the
network to learn robust representations directly from unlabeled
data, a prevalent challenge in civil infrastructure inspection. This
innovative approach empowers the framework to remarkably trans-
form low-dimensional depth and grayscale images into real-time,
high-resolution 3D point clouds of rail fasteners. Beyond immedi-
ate benefits in inspection automation and efficiency, this research
offers broader ramifications for the civil engineering computing
community. The ability to generate accurate geometric reconstruc-
tions paves the way for significant advancements in digital twin
technology, a cornerstone of modern infrastructure monitoring.
Our framework provides a cost-effective and efficient pathway
for generating real-time 3D data, a critical component for building
and maintaining accurate digital twins of infrastructure assets. This,
in turn, has the potential to revolutionize infrastructure monitoring
by enabling proactive maintenance strategies. By facilitating the
development of digital twins, the proposed methodology holds im-
mense potential to enhance safety, reliability, and cost-effectiveness
across a broad spectrum of civil engineering applications.

Methodology
This paper proposes a novel deep-learning framework for generat-
ing high-fidelity 3D point clouds of rail track fasteners, leveraging
depth images and grayscale image data. This framework has the
potential to revolutionize infrastructure monitoring applications
by enabling real-time, accurate 3D data acquisition for proactive
maintenance and informed decision-making.
Proposed Framework
The core of this framework as illustrated in Fig. 2 lies in a multi-
modal architecture designed to process two data types simultane-
ously: depth images and corresponding grayscale images. This
combined approach allows the model to extract comprehensive in-
formation about the fasteners, capturing both visual details and
depth. However, a significant challenge arises in acquiring a large
and diverse data set of fastener images. Traditional methods often
require extensive manual labeling, which can be time-consuming
and expensive. To overcome this limitation, we leverage a tech-
nique known as self-supervised contrastive learning ( Falcon and
Cho 2020 ). This approach enables the model to learn effectively
from augmented data, where variations such as rotations and flips
are introduced to artificially expand the data set, thereby improving
the model ’s generalization to unseen examples. Self-supervised
contrastive learning then directs the model to focus on recognizing
similarities and differences between these augmented representa-
tions, allowing it to extract meaningful features without extensive
manual labeling. Through this enriched data set, the framework
achieves real-time, accurate 3D point cloud generation, enhancing
infrastructure monitoring systems. The framework ’s success is
largely dependent on a robust data augmentation stage, where tech-
niques such as random rotations, Gaussian noise injection, contrast
adjustments, and color jittering are employed. These strategies en-
rich the training data, improving the model ’s ability to generalize
under varying real-world conditions such as lighting, noise, and
object orientations, leading to superior performance in generating
3D point clouds.
This model accomplishes the generation of 3D point clouds
solely from 2D image data by capitalizing on several advanced
techniques, including self-supervised contrastive learning, Fourier
transform-based neural networks ( Li et al. 2020 ), vision transform-
ers (Khan et al. 2022 ), attention mechanisms ( Niu et al. 2021 ), and
Bayesian distribution modeling ( Aguilera et al. 2010 ).

are then processed by the predictor. The predictor functions

Vision Transformer. We augment the traditional Siamese network
(Guo et al. 2017 ; Melekhov et al. 2016 ) by integrating a vision
transformer and a Fourier network. Vision transformers (ViTs),
as shown in Fig. 3, are a new wave in computer vision, inspired
by the success of transformers in natural language processing. Un-
like traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) that process
images pixel by pixel, ViTs divide images into smaller patches.
Each patch is embedded into a higher-dimensional space and
undergoes positional encoding to preserve its location within the
original image. This enables the ViT to grasp the relationships be-
tween different parts of the image. The core of the ViT is its trans-
former encoder, which analyzes the interactions between patches,
capturing both local details and global features. This processed in-
formation is then utilized for tasks such as image classification or
object detection. ViTs offer a robust alternative to CNNs, demon-
strating impressive performance across various image analysis
benchmarks.
Concurrently, the Fourier network specializes in processing
depth images, employing a Fourier transform-based approach to
extract depth-specific features. By processing both image and depth
data through these specialized networks, our framework achieves a
more comprehensive understanding of rail track fasteners.
Fourier Network. The system utilizes a module called the Fourier
network (FN) for in-depth analysis of the point cloud data as shown
in Fig. 4. While it shares the name with Fourier neural networks
used in language processing, this FN focuses on extracting features
from the depth information (3D structure) using the Fourier trans-
form (FT). The FT helps the FN identify underlying patterns within
the depth data. This FN acts as a feature extractor in two key ways.
First, it utilizes various convolution techniques to pull out a rich set
of features. These techniques involve breaking down convolutions
for efficiency (separable), preserving data at edges (padded),
extracting features from individual channels (depth-wise), and cap-
turing spatial relationships (simple). Second, after feature extrac-
tion, the FN employs an attention mechanism to filter the
features. This mechanism assigns importance weights based on
relevance to the task, essentially focusing on the most informative
features and discarding less important ones. Finally, the FN uses a
technique called “dot attention ” to combine all the extracted and
filtered features. This creates a single, unified representation that
captures important information from both the image and depth data
of the point cloud.
Following this initial feature extraction and fusion stage, the
framework employs a multilevel fusion network (Fig. 5) to further
refine the representation. This network performs a series of fusion
steps using dot attention. There are three primary representations
obtained at this point: image-based features from the transformer,
depth-based features from the Fourier network, and the fused rep-
resentation generated by the MFN. The multilevel fusion network
takes all three of these representations as input and refines them
through a series of fusion steps using dot attention. This process
ultimately yields a single, unified global representation that cap-
tures the most salient features from both the image and depth data.
This global representation is then fed into the predictor, which is a
residual network comprised of four consecutive transformations.
The predictor’s role is to refine and reconstruct the global represen-
tation. This process yields two sets of representations: one from the
encoder and the other from the predictor. The objective is to min-
imize the distance between these two representations through a loss
function. Essentially, the predictor acts as a teacher, guiding the
encoder to learn more effective feature representations for point
cloud generation.

the rail track fasteners.

Siamese Predictor
The predictor network as shown in Fig. 7 plays a critical role in the
Siamese architecture, refining the global representation learned
through the multilevel fusion process. This network is a residual
network comprised of four consecutive transformation blocks.
Each block typically consists of a convolutional layer followed
by a batch normalization layer and a rectified linear unit (ReLU)
activation function. These residual blocks allow the network to
learn complex, nonlinear relationships within the data. The predic-
tor takes the single, unified global representation generated by the
multilevel fusion network as input. It then applies the aforemen-
tioned transformations within each block to refine and reconstruct
this representation. This process essentially attempts to recreate the
original input data (both image and depth information) based on the
learned global representation. By comparing the reconstructed rep-
resentation with the original global representation, the framework
can assess the effectiveness of the encoding process.
A key objective of the predictor is to minimize the distance be-
tween the encoded representation from the encoder (i.e., the global
representation before entering the predictor) and the reconstructed
representation generated by the predictor itself. This distance min-
imization is achieved through a loss function. The loss function
calculates the difference between the two representations and
guides the training process by penalizing the network for signifi-
cant discrepancies. As the training progresses, the predictor be-
comes adept at refining the global representation, ensuring that
it accurately captures the essential information required for point
cloud generation. In simpler terms, the predictor acts as a “teacher”
for the encoder within the Siamese network. By striving to recon-
struct the global representation, the predictor guides the encoder
toward learning more efficient and effective feature representations
that are well-suited for the task of generating 3D point clouds from
2D image data.
Bayesian Neural Network for Point Cloud Generation
The ultimate goal of the proposed framework, as shown in Fig. 8,i s
to generate 3D point clouds of rail track fasteners based on the
learned representations. This section delves into the intricacies
of the Bayesian neural network employed for this crucial task.
The core idea behind this approach lies in acknowledging the in-
herent uncertainty associated with the point cloud generation pro-
cess. Real-world data often exhibit variations and noise, and the
mapping from 2D representations to a 3D point cloud is inherently
complex and may not have a single, definitive solution. To address
this uncertainty, we leverage a Bayesian learning framework.
Our framework utilizes four distinct representations as input for
the Bayesian neural network:
Image Representation: This representation captures visual de-
tails extracted from the image data by the transformer network.
Depth Representation: This representation encapsulates depth-
related information obtained from the depth maps through the
Fourier network.
Combined Representation (MLN): This representation inte-
grates features from both image and depth domains, generated
by the multilevel fusion network (MLN).

 The depth representation captures information about the spatial

To account for the inherent uncertainty in point cloud genera-
tion, the framework employs a Bayesian approach. Instead of pre-
dicting a single, deterministic point cloud, the Bayesian neural
network learns the probability distribution underlying the point
cloud generation process. This distribution captures the range of
possible point clouds that could be generated based on the input
representations.
Bayesian Small Networks: We utilize separate small neural
networks for each of the four input representations. These networks
are tasked with learning the probability distribution of the point
cloud for each representation. This allows the network to capture
the unique contribution of each representation to the overall point
cloud generation process.
Distribution Fusion: The individual probability distributions
learned from each small network are not directly used for point
cloud generation. Instead, a complex, nonlinear fusion process
combines these distributions to create a single, unified global dis-
tribution that encapsulates the combined knowledge from all four
representations. This fusion step leverages techniques such as deep
amortization, allowing the framework to learn a more comprehen-
sive and informative distribution for generating high-fidelity 3D
point clouds.
Point Cloud Sampling: Once the global distribution is estab-
lished, the framework utilizes a sampling technique to generate a
point cloud. This sampling process essentially draws a sample
from the learned distribution. Since the distribution captures the
inherent uncertainty in the data, multiple samples can be drawn,
resulting in a set of potential poi nt clouds that are all plausible
given the input representations . This ability to generate multiple
point cloud samples reflects the inherent variability present in
real-world data.
By leveraging a Bayesian framework (as illustrated in Fig. 8)
and sampling from the learned distribution, the proposed approach
effectively manages uncertainty in point cloud generation. This
probabilistic method offers a nuanced understanding of potential
variations in the generated point clouds, providing valuable insights
for infrastructure monitoring. Rather than producing a single “best
guess” point cloud, the framework generates multiple potential
point clouds that represent the range of possibilities based on
the input data and associated uncertainties. This capability is par-
ticularly beneficial for tasks such as anomaly detection in rail track
maintenance. By comparing generated point clouds to a reference
or identifying significant deviations among samples, the framework
can reveal potential issues with rail track fasteners, facilitating pro-
active maintenance strategies.

Segmentation
The initial step, segmentation, focuses on isolating specific features
of interest from the overall point cloud. Imagine a point cloud rep-
resenting a bridge with fasteners attached. Segmentation allows us
to separate the bridge, the main plane structure, from the individual
fasteners such as bolts or screws. This isolation facilitates the indi-
vidual characterization and analysis of each component. The Ran-
dom Sample Consensus (RANSAC) algorithm serves as a powerful
tool for achieving this segmentation objective ( Li et al. 2017 ).
RANSAC is adept at identifying outliers within a point cloud.
In our bridge example, the bridge itself represents the dominant
plane. RANSAC effectively identifies points deviating from this
dominant plane, effectively pinpointing the location of the fasten-
ers. Fig. 16 visually depicts the segmentation process. The first col-
umn showcases the raw point cloud encompassing both the bridge
and the fasteners. The second column presents the isolated bridge
region identified as an outlier by RANSAC. Finally, the third col-
umn displays the extracted fastener, now separated from the bridge
structure.
Having isolated the desired features, we can delve into the sec-
ond step: surface reconstruction. This process aims to create a more
detailed and visually informative representation of the spatial rela-
tionships within the point cloud. Here, cubic interpolation comes
into play. Interpolation essentially estimates missing data points
based on the surrounding known values. In the context of point
clouds, this translates to estimating Z values for each point, effec-
tively constructing a continuous surface that reflects the underlying
3D structure.
The X, Y , and Z coordinates of the original data points provide
the foundation for this interpolation. By estimating missing Z val-
ues, we create a smooth surface that bridges gaps and eliminates
irregularities present in the raw point cloud data. The resulting sur-
face offers a more comprehensive and visually intuitive depiction of
the 3D scene captured by the point cloud.

noteworthy number of parameters, indicating a substantial compu-
tational load. The encoder module, a composite structure incorpo-
rating the transformer, Fourier network, and MLF modules, has a
total parameter count of 885,049, with 884,921 being trainable. The
predictor module, on the other hand, is characterized by a total of
2,315,520 parameters, all of which are trainable. It is important to
note that the parameters within the encoder module represent a
combined contribution from its constituent parts.
The overall model parameter count reaches a significant
7,203,883. However, it is crucial to acknowledge that the model ’s
performance might be impacted by the relatively limited data set
size of just 30 samples. To mitigate this potential limitation, the
framework employs a self-supervised learning approach. This ap-
proach allows the model to extract optimal representations from the
available data, even with a smaller data set size. Despite the inher-
ent challenges associated with a limited data set, the self-supervised
learning approach is a novel and effective strategy for deriving
meaningful insights from such data constraints.
When assessing the model ’s performance, it is important to rec-
ognize the difficulty of achieving exceptionally high results with a
limited data set. However, the presented empirical results (in
Table 2) demonstrate the model ’s efficacy across diverse data aug-
mentation techniques. While the limited sample size necessitates
a cautious interpretation of performance metrics, the overall
approach’s novelty and effectiveness are evident in the obtained
results. The model ’s intricate architecture, with each module con-
tributing distinct functionalities, underscores its potential for
addressing complex tasks and capturing intricate patterns within
the data.

Step toward Digital Twin
The civil engineering landscape is undergoing a transformative
shift with the emergence of real-time 3D point cloud generation,
a crucial step toward realizing digital twins ( Yan et al. 2023 ;
Zaheer 2024). This research takes center stage in this advancement
by introducing a novel framework for the rapid and accurate gen-
eration of 3D point clouds specifically for rail fasteners. This
framework leverages cutting-edge technologies such as transform-
ers and Bayesian neural networks, akin to a Lidar simulation but
with a key distinction: it utilizes a self-supervised contrastive learn-
ing approach. This translates to the conversion of low-dimensional
data, consisting of images and grayscale depth maps of rail fasten-
ers, into high-fidelity 3D point clouds in real-time. The significance
lies not only in the precise geometric reconstructions achieved but
also in the framework’s ability to learn latent representations, which
benefit downstream tasks.
The concept of a digital twin involves creating a virtual rep-
resentation of a physical system to enhance monitoring, simula-
tion, and optimization processes. In railway engineering, point
cloud data are crucial for various applications such as track in-
spection, maintenance, and infrastructure management. Tradition-
ally, LiDAR systems are employed to collect high-resolution point
cloud data of railway tracks and their components. However, the
use of LiDAR systems can be both expensive and complex. To
address these challenges, we propose a digital twin approach
using deep-learning models to generate point cloud data from
2D images and depth information, effectively mimicking the capa-
bilities of a physical LiDAR system.
In the first part of the proposed framework as shown in Fig. 22,
illustrated on the left side of the image, a traditional LiDAR system
used in the railway domain is depicted. This system comprises
several key components: a laser scanner that captures precise
3D measurements of the railway environment, a panoramic camera
providing a wide-angle view of the track, a tablet and an inertial
measurement unit (IMU) integrated for system control and orien-
tation data, and a track inspection car that serves as a mobile plat-
form equipped with various sensors, including a GNSS receiving
antenna for accurate positioning and an odometer for distance
measurement. This setup enables comprehensive data collection
of railway tracks, capturing detailed point clouds essential for
analysis and maintenance. On the right side of the image, the pro-
posed digital twin methodology is illustrated. This approach lever-
ages deep learning to generate point cloud data from 2D images and
depth information. The process begins with the preparation of input
data, which includes depth images and RGB images. Depth images
provide distance information for each pixel, while RGB images of-
fer visual details. Both types of images undergo data augmentation
to improve the robustness and generalization of the model. The
augmented depth and RGB images are processed through separate
multimodal fusion networks (MFNs), which effectively learn to
fuse information from both modalities. The fused data from the
MFNs is then fed into a Bayesian neural network, which generates
the point cloud data. This network incorporates uncertainty estima-
tion, enhancing the reliability and accuracy of the generated point
clouds. The model is trained using a composite loss function that
includes terms for the outputs of the MFNs, the predictions of the
Bayesian neural network, and an additional Bayesian term to han-
dle uncertainty. Moving to the bottom section of the image, we sug-
gest a digital twin methodology for future work, which involves the
use of advanced foundation models such as depth estimation mod-
els (e.g., MiDaS) to generate depth maps from 2D images. This
approach would enable the extraction of depth information without
the need for physical LiDAR equipment, significantly reducing
costs and complexity. The generated depth maps, combined with
RGB images, would then be processed through the described
deep-learning pipeline to generate point cloud data. This architec-
ture employs an online network for training and a target network for
stabilizing the learning process, with networks being updated pro-
gressively to ensure consistent learning. This proposed future work
aims to utilize state-of-the-art depth estimation models to further
improve the efficiency and accuracy of point cloud data generation.
The applications of the generated point clouds in the context of
railway engineering are extensive. Specifically, for railway fasten-
ers, the generated point clouds can be used for fastener tightness
analysis, ensuring that fasteners are properly secured by analyzing
the point cloud data. Additionally, wear and tear detection can be
conducted to identify signs of damage on fasteners, enabling timely
maintenance. The generated point clouds can also be used for in-
stallation verification, checking that fasteners are correctly installed
according to predefined standards. Beyond fasteners, point clouds
can be employed for alignment and leveling of tracks, rail profile
measurement to detect wear and deformation, and erosion monitor-
ing to prevent potential hazards near the tracks. These insights have
the potential to enhance safety, reliability, and cost-effectiveness
across various civil engineering applications.

Conclusion
The proposed framework marks a significant step forward in civil
engineering and infrastructure monitoring. It overcomes the limi-
tations of traditional LiDAR-based methods by introducing an in-
novative approach to quickly generate real-time 3D point clouds
of rail fasteners. By using advanced deep-learning techniques, spe-
cifically transformers and Bayesian neural networks with self-
supervised contrastive learning, it effectively converts 2D images
and grayscale depth maps into detailed 3D models. One of the key
strengths of this framework is its practicality and cost-efficiency. It
uses easily accessible 2D images, bypassing the need for costly
LiDAR equipment, and addresses common issues such as noise
and incomplete data. Its ability to create accurate geometric recon-
structions and understand latent data patterns not only enables
real-time monitoring but also supports further automated tasks,
streamlining civil engineering processes. Moving forward, it is
essential to conduct a thorough evaluation of the framework ’s
performance, including comparisons with traditional methods
and a detailed analysis of the results. Additionally, incorporating
anomaly detection algorithms to measure improvements in the
early detection of rail fastener failures will further validate its ef-
fectiveness. This framework has the potential to revolutionize infra-
structure maintenance by providing real-time insights into the
condition of critical components. This paves the way for the devel-
opment of digital twins, significantly enhancing safety, reliability,
and cost-efficiency across various applications. Real-time monitor-
ing supports proactive maintenance strategies, reducing the risk of
unexpected failures and accidents. However, there are still chal-
lenges to address. The accuracy of the 3D point clouds depends
on the quality of the input images and depth maps, and performance
may vary with different types of rail fasteners and environmental
conditions. Future research should focus on integrating additional
data sources and refining the learning algorithms to improve ro-
bustness and adaptability. Long-term field tests are also necessary
to fully validate the framework’s effectiveness in diverse real-world
scenarios.
