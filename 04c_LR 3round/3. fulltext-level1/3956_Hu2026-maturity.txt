ABSTRACT
Smart construction machinery enabled by digital twin (DT) technology has significant potential to enhance 
construction safety and efficiency. However, the lack of a dedicated maturity assessment model for construction 
machinery DT (CMDT) reveals a critical gap in existing research. This study proposes a structured framework for 
assessing CMDT maturity, designed to evaluate the readiness and developmental stage of DT implementations in 
construction machinery. The framework was developed based on a comprehensive literature review and expert 
interviews, yielding a maturity assessment model comprising five dimensions and 24 indicators across five 
maturity levels. The model adopts a two-stage assessment approach that integrates expert competency-based 
grouping and weighting with an improved bi-objective optimization model. To enhance the robustness of 
expert opinion aggregation, a penalty-weight mechanism is embedded in the objective function, effectively 
balancing consensus and confidence. The proposed framework was validated through a real-world case study 
involving an automated construction system (ACS). The results demonstrate the capability of the framework to 
identify CMDT maturity levels and inform improvement pathways. Overall, this study provides an evidence- 
based tool to accelerate DT adoption in the construction sector.
1. Introduction
As the construction industry advances toward mechanization and 
digitalization, sophisticated machinery such as tunnel boring machines 
(TBMs) for shield tunnels [ 1 ] and automated construction systems 
(ACSs) for high-rise buildings [ 2 ] has been increasingly deployed across 
a wide range of projects. These machines offer substantial advantages, 
but their structural complexity, harsh on-site environments, and sig -
nificant fluctuations in load conditions pose considerable challenges, 
which necessitate the adoption of digital technologies to enhance 
operational safety and efficiency [ 3 ]. Among the available digital 
technologies, digital twin (DT) technology has proven instrumental in 
facilitating the operation of construction machinery [ 4 ]. DTs can inte -
grate geometric and non-geometric data at both component and system 
levels, enabling closed-loop processes for monitoring, prediction, and 
optimization [ 5 – 6 ]. These capabilities have generated growing interest 
in DT applications within the construction sector, as evidenced by 
several review studies [ 7 – 9 ].
As DT technology continues to mature, evaluating the maturity of DT 
implementation has become essential for assessing the current state of 
applications and for devising targeted improvement strategies [ 10 ]. 
Several maturity models have been proposed to address this need. For 
instance, Uhlenkamp et al. [ 11 ] developed a general maturity model 
encompassing multiple dimensions, including model, data, context, 
control, human-machine interaction, computing capabilities, and inte -
gration. Liu et al. [ 12 ] proposed general maturity models applicable 
across multiple domains. However, such generalized models often lack 
the technical granularity required to capture domain-specific 
characteristics.
Recognizing the distinction between general and domain-specific DT 
applications, Hu et al. [ 13 ] developed a quantitative maturity model for 
high-end equipment, featuring 27 rubrics across three primary di -
mensions. Chen et al. [ 14 ] and Li et al. [ 15 ] designed maturity assess -
ment frameworks for building and infrastructure DTs, respectively. 
Despite these advancements, existing DT maturity models do not suffi -
ciently account for the unstructured nature of construction environ -
ments or the inherent complexity of large construction machinery. The 
absence of tailored maturity models has left the improvement pathways 
of these applications largely unclear.

2. Literature review
To structure the existing literature on DT maturity, a targeted liter -
ature search was conducted. The Scopus and Google Scholar databases 
were queried using the combined keywords "Digital Twin" AND "Matu -
rity", covering publications from 2010 to 2025. The review is organized 
into two subsections. Section 2.1 synthesizes existing DT maturity 
models, focusing on their core attributes, evaluation dimensions, and 
evolutionary pathways. Section 2.2 summarizes qualitative and quan -
titative approaches to DT maturity assessment.
2.1. DT maturity models
Technology maturity denotes the stage of development that a tech -
nology has reached within its life cycle [ 16 ]. As the scope of DT appli -
cations continues to expand, research on maturity models has attracted 
growing attention. A general DT maturity model provides a structured 
framework for identifying the attributes of DT systems and defining 
corresponding evaluation criteria. The key attributes of DTs include 
integration, connectivity, analytics, security, and scalability. Integration 
refers to the ability to aggregate data from multiple sources and is 
commonly evaluated using metrics such as data completeness, consis -
tency, and integration latency [ 17 ]. Connectivity denotes the capability 
to seamlessly link physical and digital environments, which can be 
assessed through connectivity uptime, interoperability scores, protocol 
compatibility, and integration complexity [ 18 ]. Analytics reflects pro -
ficiency in data analysis and decision support, with indicators including 
decision-making speed and effectiveness [ 19 ]. Security concerns the 
protection of data privacy, which can be evaluated using metrics such as 
the frequency of security incidents, adherence to security standards, and 
the robustness of encryption protocols [ 20 ]. Finally, scalability assesses 
the ability to handle increasing data volumes and complexities while 
maintaining stable performance [ 21 ].
Building on these foundational attributes, several DT maturity 
models have been proposed. For example, Liu et al. [ 12 ] introduced a 
five-level maturity model, progressing from digital models to federated 
DTs. Similarly, a six-level DT maturity model has been introduced, 
advancing from asset replication to interoperability [ 22 ]. Beyond gen -
eral models, domain-specific DT maturity models cater to the unique 
requirements of different sectors. In the aerospace industry, Medina 
et al. [ 23 ] proposed a 10-dimensional DT maturity model with four 
maturity levels per dimension, covering aspects such as the analytical 
capability and data collection frequency. In the manufacturing industry, 
Villegas et al. [ 24 ] introduced a DT maturity model, focusing on prod -
uct, production, and service dimensions. For human-robot collaboration 
in assembly tasks, Liu et al. [ 25 ] designed a maturity model featuring 
five dimensions and 24 indicators. Domain-specific models have also 
been developed for healthcare [ 26 ], smart cities [ 27 ], and underground 
infrastructure [ 15 ], each presenting tailored dimensions, indicators, and 
maturity levels. However, considering the inherent complexity of large 
construction machinery, a tailored DT maturity model is urgently 
required. In the absence of such a model, project managers will face 
challenges in formulating effective DT implementation strategies, 
identifying improvement priorities, and leveraging competitive 
advantages.
2.2. Maturity assessment methods
Ensuring the reliability of DT maturity assessments necessitates the 
selection of appropriate methods. Generally, maturity assessment 
methods can be divided into qualitative and quantitative categories.
In the qualitative category, online questionnaires [ 28 ], face-to-face 
interviews [ 29 ], and site investigations [ 30 ] are commonly used to 
collect expert opinions on maturity indicators. The selection and 
grouping of decision-makers (DMs) play a critical role in this process. 
Previous studies have engaged varying numbers of experts, such as 9 in 
[ 25 ], 15 in [ 31 ], and 22 in [ 15 ]. According to group decision-making 
theory, when the number of DMs exceeds 11, their aggregated opin -
ions yield more accurate and reliable evaluations [ 14 ]. Additionally, 
DMs are often grouped based on their homogeneity in knowledge and 
experience with DT implementations. Higher weights are assigned to 
groups with greater expertise [ 32 ]. For example, Chen et al. [ 33 ] applied 
the K-means clustering algorithm to group 20 DMs by expertise level and 
employed the Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal 
Solution to assign higher weights to expert groups. Other studies have 
also employed K-means clustering for expert grouping [ 34 ]. However, 
traditional K-means clustering relies on manually specified group 
numbers, which may introduce bias [ 35 ]. Moreover, in addition to 
expertise levels, factors such as attentiveness and the quality of re -
sponses warrant consideration when assigning weights [ 36 ]. For sub -
sequent quantitative analysis, maturity levels in questionnaires are 
typically measured using data points (e.g., 0 or 1) [ 27 ] or intervals (e.g., 
[0, 1] or [1, 2]) [ 33 ]. Interval-based measurements mitigate ambiguities 
in human judgment, yielding more objective evaluations [ 37 ].
In the quantitative category, individual opinions are first quantified. 
Traditionally, these opinions are represented numerically, as demon -
strated in [ 38 ]. However, because human judgment inherently involves 
imprecision, probability distribution functions (PDFs) are increasingly 
employed. For instance, by observing the characteristics of data distri -
bution, each opinion regarding the maturity level of an indicator can be 
fitted to a Gaussian [ 33 ] or Beta distribution [ 39 ]. The average value of 
the PDF represents the expert ′ s opinion, and the standard error quan -
tifies the uncertainty associated with the judgment. Once individual 
opinions are quantified, aggregation methods maximize the total over -
lapping area of PDFs, converting it into an optimization problem, as 
shown in [ 40 ] and [ 41 ]. To further enhance the reliability and objec -
tivity of the results, Ji et al. [ 39 ] proposed a bi-objective optimization 
model that considers both the overlapping area of the PDFs and the 
confidence level of the collective PDF. Subsequent adaptations, such as 
those applied to building information modeling (BIM) maturity assess -
ment [ 31 ] and human-robot collaboration maturity assessment [ 25 ], 
illustrate the model ′ s utility. However, in the original bi-objective 
optimization model, maximizing the overlapping area of PDFs and 
minimizing the confidence level of the collective PDF are weighted 
equally, which can lead to biases that undervalue consensus accuracy.

As summarized in Table 1 , although existing maturity models have

First, most existing models are either cross-domain or primarily tailored

To address these limitations, this study proposes a domain-specific 
maturity assessment framework for CMDT. The framework combines a 
customized 24-indicator evaluation system with a bi-objective optimi -
zation model that incorporates PDFs, thereby yielding more objective 
and reliable maturity assessment results.
3. Framework for assessing the maturity of CMDT
The proposed CMDT maturity assessment framework contains two 
phases, as illustrated in Fig. 1 . Phase 1 focuses on maturity model 
development, in which domain-specific indicators were identified 
through systematic indicator selection and subsequently prioritized 
through importance ranking. This phase is completed by defining 
discrete maturity levels for evaluation. Phase 2 establishes a rigorous 
maturity evaluation methodology that integrates Louvain-based com -
munity detection for expert grouping with an improved bi-objective 
optimization model for opinion aggregation. By balancing consensus 
accuracy and confidence levels through a penalty-weight mechanism, 
the inherent uncertainty associated with expert assessments can be 
mitigated. Consequently, the proposed framework can identify current 
CMDT maturity levels and support strategic DT implementation.

After determining the maturity indicators, a five-level maturity 
structure was developed based on established DT maturity models 
[ 13 – 15 , 22 , 47 , 50,51 ]. The five maturity levels are defined as follows:
Level 1 . Basic monitoring
At this foundational level, construction machinery exhibits minimal 
digital integration. Sensors, if installed, are rudimentary, and data are 
collected manually in spreadsheets or static formats. Real-time virtual 
representations of the machinery are absent, with any 3D models being 
static and used solely for visualization. Machinery operates indepen -
dently, without remote-control capabilities or digital services, relying 
entirely on manual oversight and intervention.
Level 2 . Connected machinery
At this level, construction machinery incorporates basic sensors to 
track fundamental metrics, such as fuel consumption and engine tem -
perature. A rudimentary DT reflects the real-time status of the ma -
chinery for a limited set of parameters. Structured data integration 
supports basic monitoring and control capabilities. Digital services are 
introduced, including notifications for low-fuel or maintenance alerts, 
allowing for minimal remote intervention by operators.
Level 3 . Predictive machinery
At this level, construction machinery is equipped with advanced 
sensors and actuators that capture real-time data on key operational 
parameters, such as load, pressure, and component wear. This level 
signifies the achievement of perception intelligence, wherein machines 
are capable of task-specific recognition and basic contextual awareness. 
Centralized data integration supports the application of conventional 
machine learning algorithms for predictive analytics. Key functionalities 
at this level include predictive maintenance and elementary fleet 
management.
Level 4 . Semi-autonomous machinery
At this level, construction machinery demonstrates cognitive intel -
ligence by dynamically adjusting operational parameters in response to 
real-time data and environmental conditions. This cognitive capability is 
supported by continuous simulations performed by the DT model, which 
can optimize performance in real time by utilizing more advanced ma -
chine learning methods. Advanced data management and predictive 
analytics enable services such as automated fleet management, opti -
mized scheduling, and remote operation.
Level 5 . Fully autonomous machinery
At this highest level, construction machinery achieves autonomous 
intelligence with minimal or no human intervention. The DT model 
matures into a fully self-adaptive system, leveraging advanced AI tech -
nologies, including large language models and multi-agent coordination 
frameworks. These technologies enable machinery to continuously learn 
from both historical data and real-time feedback. Machinery autono -
mously adjusts parameters to enhance efficiency and mitigate risks. 
Additionally, data management becomes fully automated, supporting 
seamless integration of processes. This level introduces advanced digital 
services such as autonomous fleet management and lifecycle 
optimization.
After establishing the five levels of CMDT maturity, the maturity 
levels for each individual indicator were further specified. Detailed 
definitions for the five-level maturity scale at the indicator level are 
provided in Appendix A to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the 
assessment.

similar opinions clustered together. Within each group, a composite

4. Case study
This section presents a case study to demonstrate the application of 
the proposed CMDT maturity assessment framework. Section 4.1
introduces the background of the target construction machinery. Section 
4.2 details the implementation of the framework, from the initial 
collection of expert judgments to the quantification of overall maturity 
level.
4.1. Case description
A real-world DT-driven ACS construction project was selected to 
evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The ACS repre -
sents an advanced form of construction machinery that can accelerate 
high-rise building construction. However, the operations of the ACS are 
accompanied by substantial technical challenges, particularly during its 
ascent alongside the primary building structure. For example, the hy -
draulic cylinders used for climbing often struggle to maintain synchro -
nized movements owing to factors such as excessive loads and high- 
speed winds. These challenges increase the risk of instability and, in 
extreme cases, structural collapse owing to uneven postures during 
operation.
Stakeholders involved in this project expressed interest in assessing 
the maturity level of DT implementation. As shown in Fig. 4 , the ACS is 
equipped with a range of sensors designed for both safety and opera -
tional monitoring. These include safety-related devices such as in -
clinometers and leveling instruments, as well as operational sensors such 
as oil pressure sensors and laser rangefinders. The BIM-based virtual 
representation of the ACS functions as a centralized platform for data 
storage, analysis, and simulation. In the presence of potential risks, 
multi-objective optimization algorithms calculate the optimal opera -
tional parameters to balance safety with efficiency. These optimized 
parameters are subsequently transmitted to the ACS control system, 
enabling automated adjustments while retaining human oversight. This 
feedback loop ensures operational optimization and predictive safety 
measures.
4.2. Assessment results
Questionnaires were distributed to experts directly involved in the 
ACS DT system, as well as other specialists familiar with the project 
background. For the latter group, detailed project descriptions and 
technical information were conveyed through face-to-face communica -
tion to ensure informed responses. Of the 17 collected responses, 15 
were deemed valid based on their completeness and quality. Two re -
sponses were excluded owing to substantial incompleteness and in -
consistencies in the expert competency section, suggesting 
inattentiveness.
The first section of the questionnaire assessed the respondents ′ 
expertise in the DT, including relevant certifications or honors. Using the 
community detection algorithm with a threshold θ = 0.7, the experts 
were grouped, and each group was assigned a weight based on the 
opinion group weighting strategy, with α = 0.7 and β = 0.3. The four 
resulting groups and their weights are summarized in Fig. 5 :
Group 1 . Respondents 1, 4, 9, and 10, with an assigned importance 
weight of 0.433.
Group 2 . Respondents 2, 6, 8, 11, and 14, with an assigned importance 
weight of 0.486.
Group 3 . Respondents 3, 5, and 15, with an assigned importance 
weight of 0.531.
Group 4 . Respondents 7, 12, and 13, with an assigned importance 
weight of 0.652.

5. Post-assessment validation and discussions
To verify the practical relevance and credibility of the assessment 
results, a post-assessment validation was conducted using user feedback. 
Specifically, semi-structured interviews were conducted with key 
stakeholders involved in the case study project, including three system 
developers, three system operators, and two project managers. The in -
terviews focused on checking the alignment between the maturity 
assessment outcomes and users ′ actual experiences with the ACS DT 
system. The interviewees consistently corroborated the relatively low 
maturity score assigned to the "Services" dimension, as well as the 
comparatively higher maturity levels for the "Virtual Model" and 
"Communication and Interaction" dimensions. These findings reveal a 
critical developmental imbalance. Although the DT-driven ACS project 
has achieved high-fidelity virtual representations, the lack of autono -
mous "Services" indicates that the current DT system primarily functions 
as a monitoring platform rather than as a proactive decision-support 
system. The clustering of results at Level 3 suggests that, while real- 
time sensor integration and data-driven alerts are operational, the 
transition to Level 4 is constrained by the persistent need for human 
intervention within the control loop.
The proposed framework offers several managerial insights. First, it 
enables evidence-based readiness diagnosis, allowing managers to 
identify the current CMDT maturity level across multiple dimensions, 
rather than relying on fragmented technical indicators or subjective 
judgments. Second, the integration of expert competency-based 
grouping and weighted opinion aggregation allows managers to align 
the criticality of DT-supported decisions with the assessed maturity 
level, thereby enhancing managerial confidence. Third, by revealing 
specific capability gaps at each maturity level, the framework supports 
the development of strategic implementation roadmaps. For DT systems 
at lower maturity levels, the results highlight the need to strengthen 
foundational capabilities, such as data acquisition, data quality, and 
system connectivity. For more mature systems, attention can shift to -
ward advanced analytics, closed-loop interaction, and autonomous de -
cision-making.
Of note, the practical adoption of the framework is designed for high 
usability through an automated pipeline. Complex backend processes 
for expert grouping and bi-objective optimization can be fully abstracted 
from end users. Practitioners only need to provide primary survey data, 
upon which the maturity scores and diagnostic insights can be auto -
matically generated to support strategic improvement planning. Future 
work may also develop standardized software tools to streamline 
deployment across diverse construction machinery contexts.
6. Conclusion
This study developed a comprehensive framework for evaluating the 
CMDT maturity. Based on an extensive literature review and expert 
consultations, 24 maturity indicators were identified across five di -
mensions. Maturity levels were systematically defined for each indicator 
as well as for the overall CMDT maturity model.
To enhance the accuracy and reliability of maturity assessment, this 
study introduced a opinion grouping and weighting strategy integrated 
with an improved bi-objective optimization model for aggregating 
expert opinions. Expert opinions were grouped using community 
detection algorithms, and weights were assigned to these groups based 
on both expert competency and the quality of their responses. The bi- 
objective optimization model was further refined through the intro -
duction of an adjustable penalty weight λ , which balances the overlap of 
PDFs with the confidence level of the collective opinion. Beyond 
maturity assessment, the resulting evaluations provide guidance for 
identifying the evolutionary pathways of CMDTs.
Although the proposed framework provides practical guidance for 
improving DT solutions in construction machinery, opportunities for 
further research remain. Future studies should consider incorporating 
additional criteria to enhance the precision of maturity levels. For 
example, introducing thresholds for DT model update frequencies can 
refine the granularity of the assessment. Furthermore, the integration of 
more objective data, such as sensor logs, can reduce the dependence on 
expert evaluations. Moreover, extending the application of the frame -
work to other related sectors will enhance its versatility.
