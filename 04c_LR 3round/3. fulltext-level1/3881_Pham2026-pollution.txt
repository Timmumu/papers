ABSTRACT 
Pollution created by building renovation activities poses a hazard to human health 
and the proximate environment. Integrating building information modelling (BIM) 
with digital twin (DT) technology offers a promising means for real-time monitoring 
and enabling collaboration among project participants. However, it is challenging to 
transform the BIM models of a renovated facility into a DT platform for monitoring 
and reporting construction pollution on-site. This paper proposes a BIM-enhanced DT 
architecture for managing construction pollution in building renovation projects. The 
platform entails five key components: physical entity (PE), virtual model (VM), DT data 
(DD), services (Ss), and connections (CNs). To resolve ambiguities in information 
requirements, a novel Level of Information Need (LOIN) framework for BIM-enhanced 
DT was established. The interplay among LOIN, Level of Development (LOD), and DT 
functionalities defines the minimum information needed in the information containers 
and the metadata structure required for pollution hotspots, compliance assessment, 
and automated analytics. To illustrate its efficacy and practicality, the DT architecture 
was applied to an auditorium renovation project, where static BIM data were inte -
grated with dynamic pollution parameters captured by IoT sensors and managed in a 
Common Data Environment (CDE). The results showed its capability to systematically 
and efficiently acquire, integrate, treat, and analyze important data.

Introduction
Modern construction projects always face numerous challenges, especially resource depletion and esca -
lating construction costs, which prompt all stakeholders to adopt various sustainability concepts, such 
as circular economy (Haruna et al. 2024 ). Building renovation promotes circular design by extending 
the structural lifespan of a building and minimizing resource consumption. However, pollution created 
by building renovation activities often poses a hazard to human health and the proximate environment. 
Among the pollutants, particulate matter (PM) is widely recognized as the most critical (Yan et al. 
2023 ), alongside gaseous contaminants (Nazif et al. 2025 ), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and 
harmful chemicals (Ismaeel et al. 2023 ). Elevated noise levels disrupt patient recovery and building 
occupants, and increase occupant stress (Zou et al. 2020 ), while machinery vibrations may compromise 
structural integrity, producing wall fissures and damaging electrical and plumbing systems (Longinow 
and Mohammadi 2022 ; Choi et al. 2023 ). Collectively, these impacts contribute to unacceptable indoor 
environmental quality (IEQ) concerns.
Environmental laws of many countries regulate the installation of pollution monitoring systems on 
construction sites to detect and report pollutant exposure for building occupants and surrounding com -
munities. Yet traditional environmental measures like proper ventilation, gap sealing, and noise barriers 
cannot adequately control the impact of construction pollution. This is primarily attributed to non- 
systematic, fragmented data and inefficient information management (Doukari et al. 2024 ; Ghanbari

on paper-based documentation further increases the likelihood of errors, omissions, and inconsistencies

management, and IEQ (Salvalai et al. 2026 ). This leads to data loss during project execution. 
Furthermore, assigning responsibilities for design, construction, and facility management (FM) to dif -
ferent parties often causes information fragmentation (Bjørnskov and Jradi 2023 ), hindering project 
owners’ ability to maintain operational continuity and make informed decisions (Sacks et al. 2018 ). 
Traditional hierarchical stakeholder relationship structures cause decision-making bottlenecks, leading 
to delays and poor decision-making (Pan and Zhang 2021 ).
Building information modelling (BIM) has emerged as a pivotal enabler to mitigate these issues by 
enhancing direct stakeholder communication and empowering workers to make informed decisions 
(Wijeratne et al. 2024 ). When strategically integrated and combined with real-time data streams from 
Internet of Things (IoT) devices, organized and managed within a Common Data Environment (CDE), 
the BIM model evolves into a foundation for a BIM-enhanced Digital Twin (DT) platform that pro -
motes dynamic, bidirectional interaction between physical and virtual systems (Revolti et al. 2024 ).
Despite its potential, establishing a BIM-enhanced DT for pollution management in building renova -
tion projects remains technically challenging due to fragmented building data, heterogeneous informa -
tion sources, and complex synchronization requirements (Eneyew et al. 2022 ; Pauwels et al. 2024 ). It 
requires a rigorous alignment with international standards (e.g. ISO 19650), accurate calibration and 
deployment of IoT sensors, consistent data structures for real-time exchange, and seamless interoper -
ability across heterogeneous digital platforms (Madubuike et al. 2022 ). The synchronization of dynamic 
sensor data with BIM-enhanced semantic databases introduces substantial complexity in ensuring 
model fidelity, data granularity, and contextual interpretation (Oliveira et al. 2024 ).
Moreover, information management in the CDE remains persistent challenges such as information 
overload and insufficient data curation in the CDE (Du et al. 2020 ), which are exacerbated when pro -
ject owners or facility managers fail to identify and manage their specific information needs 
(Werbrouck et al. 2019 ). To achieve this, project owners must carefully identify relevant BIM data 
aligned with their objectives by establishing clear information requirements for data production and 
storage, ensuring an efficient information flow (Oliveira et al. 2024 ). Exchange information require -
ments (EIR), under ISO 19650, outlines necessary information, timing, and format for the information 
delivery among the parties; clarifies stakeholders’ roles; minimizes ambiguity, and standardizes data 
exchange protocols (Montoya and Roberto 2022 ). These are supported by the Level of Information 
Need (LOIN) and the Level of Development (LOD) framework defined in the BIM Execution Plan 
(BEP), which ensure consistent geometric and semantic information delivery aligns closely with the pre -
defined metadata structures necessary for DT development (Brumana et al. 2022 ).
Because advanced DT applications require highly specific alphanumeric data and documentation 
rather than just detailed 3D geometry, a systematic strategy LOIN concept enables project teams to 
define the precise information requirements for the DT without over-modelling the geometry (El Sibaii 
et al. 2025 ). Nevertheless, there is still a lack of formally defined BIM modelling and metadata struc -
tures in the CDE’s information containers that enable the systematic integration of static BIM datasets 
with real-time IoT data for DT applications in building renovation projects. The conceptual framework 
for LOIN, which directly defines virtual models of a BIM-enhanced DT remains underexplored.
To fill this research gap, we propose a BIM-enhanced DT architecture tailored for managing con -
struction pollution during building renovation projects. Defining the LOIN is the first and essential 
step toward developing a fit-for-purpose BIM model for a BIM-enhanced DT. The proposed architec -
ture identifies essential information required for structuring BIM models that can process both static 
and dynamic data. We explore the relationships among the five dimensions in the CDE of a 
BIM-enhanced DT framework in building renovation. To support the analysis, we conducted a compre -
hensive literature review and semi-structured interviews to examine the evolution from conventional 
construction practices to BIM-enhanced DT systems.
These findings helped us delineate the architecture of the BIM-enhanced DT and the LOIN frame -
work, which were subsequently implemented in an auditorium renovation project as a proof of concept.

Background
BIM-enhanced DT in construction pollution monitoring
Low-cost sensor networks (LCSNs) and low-cost IoT sensors (LCSs) were increasingly prominent in 
environmental sciences for their capacity to produce extensive real-time monitoring data (Silva et al. 
2026 ). However, conventional dashboard solutions often cannot represent spatial relationships or pin -
point the exact locations of monitored hotspots. Operating independently from advanced CDEs and 
construction workflows, these systems offered limited spatial context and data integration (Zhou and 
El-Gohary 2021 ; Nadeem et al. 2023 ). Although the integration of IoT and geographic information sys -
tems (GIS) significantly enhanced construction monitoring through real-time location tracking and spa -
tial visualization (Liu et al. 2025 ; Valinejadshoubi et al. 2025 ), these technologies often operated in silos 
and lacked seamless integration with construction-specific digital ecosystems.
Recent studies have advanced IoT-BIM integration for real-time data acquisition on construction 
sites and automated systems for data transmission and storage through interactive DT platforms (Boltsi 
et al. 2025 ; Valinejadshoubi et al. 2025 ). Modular IEQ sensing approaches have also been developed, 
including DT-based KPI-driven smart building platforms (Piras et al. 2025 ), IoT-ML-BIM-integrated 
DT frameworks for indoor air quality (IAQ) monitoring (Qian et al. 2024 ), and HBIM-XR-enabled DT 
platform for IEQ analysis in heritage building (Banfi et al. 2025 ). Despite these advances in sensing 
infrastructures and digital monitoring concepts, methodologies for aligning these platforms with regula -
tory frameworks and BIM systems remain underdeveloped.
Managing environmental pollution in renovation sites calls for a cost-effective, portable, and easily 
deployable solution, which can adapt to changing spatial arrangements and unpredictable pollution 
sources (Francisco et al. 2020 ). Most current research on BIM and DT enablers focused on new con -
struction projects (Lu et al. 2020 ; Qian et al. 2024 ). Other studies relied on proprietary hardware and 
complex deployment processes (Seabra et al. 2021 ; Hong et al. 2022 ), which were impractical for the 
transient nature of renovation projects.
A DT for construction was defined through the harmonious integration of static data from BIM 
models and dynamic data from cognitive technologies into a CDE (Sacks et al. 2020 ). While defining 
LOIN facilitates this physical-to-digital data exchange (Liu et al. 2021 ; Godager et al. 2022 ), excessive 
information requirements can burden project management. Despite prior studies having examined 
scan-to-BIM processes and defined BIM information requirements for specific use cases (Banfi et al. 
2022 ; Nguyen et al. 2023 ), limited research has explicitly specified how such BIM models should be 
structured for DT implementation in the CDE. Although previous studies clarified the flows of static 
and dynamic information within CDE solutions and linked them to stakeholders’ roles in the CDE 
workflow (Pham et al. 2025 ), a systematic process for developing and managing different information 
categories in the architectural framework remains missing. From this perspective, the value of a clearly 
structured BIM-enhanced DT architecture supported by well-defined LOIN specifications is evident. 
Such an approach ensures the consistent integration of BIM, IoT, and CDE workflows, while improving 
alignment between project services and stakeholder needs.
From non-BIM to BIM ecosystems
BIM offers a structured data environment that ensures consistency between graphical and non- 
graphical information throughout the project life cycle, thereby reducing information fragmentation 
and mitigating data exchange inefficiencies (BSI 2020 ; Ali et al. 2021 ). Its interoperability is enabled

through standardized formats such as Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) and Construction-Operations 
Building Information Exchange (COBie) (NBIMS-US 2015 ; ISO 2024 ). Harnessing the digital method -
ology made it possible to optimize and avoid problems related to large-scale works and construction 
sites located in central residential areas (Muzi et al. 2023 ).
BIM progression is governed by hierarchical standards, enabling the structured information delivery 
through standardized model evolution pathways, as illustrated in Figure 1 . The term ‘level of X’ is a 
generic way to represent BIM progression (Succar and Poirier 2020 ). This term collectively refers to 
various specifications that define geometric representation (Level of Detail), modelling maturity (Level 
of Development), fidelity of the as-built representation (Level of Accuracy and Level of Reliability), 
semantic richness, and data granularity (Level of Information and Level of Information Need).
Digitalizing renovation projects using BIM usually follows the integration of pre-existing BIM 
archives or the digitization of undocumented ‘as-is’ conditions. In cases where legacy BIM data is avail -
able, project information is typically organized and adhered to a framework such as the UK’s Level of 
Definition (LoD), which disaggregates graphical representation from semantic attributes (i.e. Level of 
Information, LoI) (BSI 2012 , 2018b ). These frameworks have evolved to support extended FM function -
alities and operational workflows through refined LOD hierarchies (Salzano, Miano, et al. 2023 ).
Existing LoD and LoI frameworks frequently fall short of addressing task-specific or phase-depend -
ent information requirements. To bridge this gap, the British Standards Institution (BSI) introduced the 
LOIN, which is a purpose-driven methodology for defining project-specific information contents based 
on stakeholder roles, life-cycle phases, and intended uses (BSI 2020 ). The LOIN (formally defined in 
ISO 7817-1, which replaced EN 17412) advances interoperability by embedding contextual metadata 
protocols and clarifying exchange requirements, thereby strengthening traceability and inter-organiza -
tional coordination (BIMForum 2024 ; ISO 2024 ).
In projects lacking digital data, BIM initiation begins with the acquisition of spatial and semantic 
data through survey-grade protocols. The fidelity in such workflows is governed by classification

States of BIM models across the building life cycle
The amount of information contained in the BIM model classified under the level of X is directly 
related to the definition of BIM states throughout the project life cycle. As BIM model states are digital 
expressions that evolve to support specific decision-making phases (Tuhaise et al. 2023 ), the level of X 
acts as the mandatory data baseline for each state; it ensures that the model possesses the requisite 
detail to support the analytical functionalities of a DT at any given stage. Figure 2 illustrates the six 
main states of BIM models along a facility’s life cycle, namely, as-is, as-designed, as-planned, as-con -
structed, as-built, and archived. The evolution of BIM model states from as-designed to archived fol -
lows established lifecycle definitions. Table 1 presents the key characteristics of each BIM model state. 
These states reflect progressively enriched model conditions informed by context-specific data and exe -
cution requirements. Since BIM models mature as projects progress, both modelling complexity and 
resource requirements increase substantially due to the need for cross-domain information reconcili -
ation and real-time operability (Salzano, Miano, et al. 2023 ). Achieving high-fidelity LOD and LOIN 
requires a balance between representational granularity and implementation feasibility (Deng et al. 
2021 ). Without this balance, modelling costs might be excessive without a proportional improvement 
in an operational utility.

organizing real-time data collected from the data acquisition layer, allowing shared information con -

Integrating BIM with DT in a CDE introduces an additional layer of complexity across technological, 
organizational, and cognitive domains (Camposano et al. 2021 ). Ali and Bandi ( 2024 ) reported a mod -
erate inverse correlation between operation and maintenance (O&M) dataset usability and big data

works investigated BIM-integrated asset management platforms, enabling proactive maintenance, con -
textual decision support, and structured information retrieval (Likhitruangsilp et al. 2022 ). Nevertheless, 
they remained limitedly adopted by the industry due to insufficient stakeholder engagement, ambiguous 
performance validation frameworks, and the lack of standardized protocols for embedding BIM into

of BIM, IoT, and analytics to enable real-time, data-informed decision-making throughout the asset life

semantically aligning IFC-based BIM models with IoT-derived time-series data, these systems create 
unified, machine-interpretable knowledge bases. This convergence enables AI-assisted reasoning, real- 
time situational awareness, and data-informed decision-making throughout the asset lifecycle (Pauwels 
et al. 2024 ).
Research methodology
In this paper, we developed the architectural configuration of a BIM-enhanced DT for real-time con -
struction pollution management in building renovation projects. The primary objective is to delineate 
the minimum BIM modelling requirements, particularly those related to LOD and LOIN, which are 
necessary for real-time environmental monitoring within the context of building renovation projects.
The system architecture was initially derived from our literature-based synthesis, establishing a the -
oretical foundation for developing a BIM-enhanced DT from unstructured non-BIM elements. This 
process revealed key gaps in current practices, CDE components, and minimum modelling require -
ments (LOIN-LOD for renovation-specific monitoring), particularly in how these gaps hinder effective 
real-time environmental monitoring and integration with existing systems. The findings consolidated 
fragmented knowledge into structured concepts, defining core components of the BIM-enhanced DT 
architectural framework. We further evaluated the functional and technical characteristics of digital rep -
resentation systems, including bidirectional data flow, semantic metadata layering, interoperability 
standards (e.g. ISO 19650 and IFC), and feedback mechanisms for dynamic environmental performance 
control.
Building upon the aforementioned insights, we introduced a novel LOIN schema. This schema inte -
grates geometric modelling levels (LOD-G) with corresponding informational layers (LOD-I), facilitat -
ing structured information delivery throughout the project life cycle, from initial modelling to real-time 
monitoring and decision-making. The proposed LOIN framework supports systematized pollution 
information capture, enabling enhanced traceability, sensor placement optimization, and regulatory 
compliance tracking.

To ensure practical relevance and stakeholder alignment, the framework was refined through semi- 
structured interviews (45–90 min) (Fellows and Liu 2015 ) with nine experts across three distinct 
cohorts. Table 3 summarizes the interviewees’ profiles. The transcripts were analyzed to extract 
domain-specific requirements concerning platform design, interoperability challenges, and real-time 
data exchange protocols. The insights from the interviews identified BIM-enhanced DT components, 
clarifying their roles in pollution monitoring as well as deepening our understanding of BIM and its 
usage for the DT development. The integration of academic and practitioner perspectives guided the 
CDE design, addressing major challenges in data governance, interoperability, and platform applicabil -
ity. These verifications revealed key implementation challenges of the preliminary framework, including 
the different alignment of information protocols in new construction and renovation projects, divergent 
CDE workflows, and stakeholder-specific expectations regarding data granularity and integration. 
Herein, we primarily focused on the EIR and bidirectional data workflows of the proposed system to 
ensure semantic consistency across all platforms.
The derived platform was then applied to an actual renovation project on Chulalongkorn University 
campus, Bangkok, Thailand. Due to its location in a high-density academic complex, the project 
required strict environmental quality control to minimize pollution, ensure comfort for university staff 
and students during teaching activities, safeguard the health and safety of on-site construction workers, 
and preserve the structural integrity of the building. A pilot test was implemented by installing six IoT 
sensor clusters in sensitive areas (e.g. corridors, construction zones, and mechanical rooms) to gather

et al. 2022 ), the proposed BIM-enhanced DT architecture was defined by five interoperable compo -
nents: physical entities (PEs), virtual models (VMs), services (Ss), DT data (DD), and connections 
(CNs), as illustrated in Figure 3 . This DT architecture was designed to systematically and efficiently 
process both static data provided by BIM models and dynamic data from IoT sensors for real-time pol -
lution monitoring at renovation sites. The details of each platform component are as follows.

PEs constitute the tangible elements of the construction environment, including building components, 
construction equipment, mechanical systems, human agents, and site-specific environmental factors,

Virtual models (VMs)
VMs are the digital surrogates of PEs, capturing geometric, behavioural, and semantic attributes to sup -
port real-time environmental simulation. In building renovation contexts, VMs enable dynamic visual -
ization of pollutant dispersion, simulate air flow, and inform zone-specific risk analysis. They can be 
constructed by using a suite of tools (e.g. BIM, GIS, MR, and CAD) and structured into the following 
four modelling layers (Tao and Zhang 2017 ; Tao et al. 2018 ; Sepasgozar et al. 2023 ):
� Geometric models represent the spatial configuration of built assets, which enables pollutant visual -
ization and sensor placement planning.
� Physical models simulate environmental interactions based on material properties and pollutant 
emission dynamics.
� Behavioural models capture the temporal change and interaction of pollution sources and environ -
mental conditions.
� Rule-based models encode thresholds, standards, and expert knowledge to inform compliance-driven 
simulations.
This layered modelling architecture enhances system interpretability and supports scenario-based 
analysis aligned with regulatory and operational constraints.
DT data (DD)
DD integrates static and dynamic data derived from PEs, VMs, expert knowledge, and fusion data to 
offer a comprehensive understanding of on-site pollution behaviours. This integration encompasses 
forecasting, automated alerts, and visual dashboards (e.g. time-series graphs and heatmaps). It also

� Static data includes BIM-derived attributes, CAD documents, regulatory standards, and historical 
pollution datasets. This type of data defines baseline modelling and scenario simulation without 
real-time synchronization.
� Dynamic data comprises live sensor feeds and contextual inputs (e.g. resident complaints), streamed

� Knowledge data encodes expert rules, compliance thresholds, and historical trend logic to guide pre -

� Fusion data integrates heterogeneous inputs from static and dynamic sources to enhance analytic

To integrate all the data above, the DT system employs VMs to conduct scenario-based simulations and 
generate predictive outputs. These outputs support environmental compliance, operational risk mitigation, and 
stakeholder communication. In the CDE workflow, both static and dynamic information flows are systematic -
ally structured and organized in information containers. Each represents the state in data management, valid -
ation, and dissemination (Pham et al. 2025 ). Static information, such as material properties, directly influences 
the type and quantity of pollutants released during renovation activities, whereas demolition timing determines 
when specific areas are exposed to potential emissions. By organizing this data within the construction work -
flow, each object in the model can be linked to sensor locations and environmental thresholds in the DT. This 
linkage allows the system to continuously compare predicted pollutant levels to real-time measurements, gener -
ate alerts, and support mitigation strategies like ventilation adjustments or sequence optimization.
Furthermore, the organized BIM data enables dynamic scenario simulations, allowing stakeholders to 
evaluate alternative materials and demolition sequences as well as to predict their environmental 
impacts before implementation. In this way, integrating BIM static information with workflow design 
directly improves the DT’s predictive and adaptive capabilities for effective pollution management. The 
comprehensive structure of DD guarantees that the system remains context-aware, interpretable, and 
scalable across renovation scenarios.
Services (Ss)
Ss represent the actionable layer of DT systems, translating integrated data into environmental insights, 
mitigation strategies, and compliance documentation. They rely on effective DD metadata usage to 
deliver actionable insights for pollution management. Tao, Qi, et al. ( 2019 ) and Hu et al. ( 2022 ) defined 
three categories of services:
� Application services constitute the core operational capabilities of the DT, enabling a performance 
assessment through functionalities such as simulation, verification, monitoring, optimization, diag -
nostics, and predictive analytics.
� Third-party services augment core specialized analytical capabilities by integrating external algorithms, 
datasets, and expert knowledge to enhance decision-making in pollution mitigation scenarios.
� Platform services provide the systemic interface between BIM-enhanced models and IoT-enabled 
sensor networks, supporting real-time data flow, resource optimization, and environmental perform -
ance tracking.
Each service tier operates on the DD architecture and supports stakeholder-specific applications 
across the renovation life cycle.
Connections (CNs)
The DT platform can be interconnected through six bidirectional connection (CN) pathways: (1) CN 
between PE and VM (CN_PV), (2) CN between PE and DD (CN_PD), (3) CN between PE and Ss

(CN_PS), (4) CN between VM and DD (CN_VD), (5) CN between VM and Ss (CN_VS), and (6) CN 
between Ss and DD (CN_SD). They facilitate real-time data flow between the core components, as 
shown in Figure 3 . These CNs ensure semantic consistency, support automated alerts, and maintain 
system responsiveness under variable site conditions. They connect not only the other five core compo -

LOIN for an as-needed BIM model
Data drift between analytical knowledge bases and the authoritative BIM model presents a significant 
challenge, particularly when large volumes of information accumulate (Alsehrawy et al. 2025 ). 
Addressing this issue requires a transition toward an as-needed BIM model. In the DT application, the 
LOIN is preferred over the traditional LOD/LOI as it provides a purpose-driven methodology that 
avoids information redundancy (Collado-Mariscal et al. 2025 ). The information defined by LOIN for 
an as-needed BIM model serves as a fundamental reference for additional functions in the proposed 
platform. Within this approach, the LOIN framework ensures that only precise, task-specific informa -
tion is generated and maintained for complex reasoning, advanced data analysis, and informed deci -
sion-making (Kone and Mahesh 2025 ). This ensures information reliability at key milestones and 
prevents stakeholders from over-relying on immature data.
Effective pollution monitoring requires BIM models within the DT environment to evolve beyond 
static geometric representations and support real-time responsiveness and data-driven decision-making. 
Although LOD and LOIN serve distinct functions, they are inherently complementary. As depicted in 
Figure 5 , LOD provides geometric fidelity necessary for sensor deployment and spatial simulation, 
while LOIN governs semantic requirements such as information type, delivery format, and use-case 
relevance across project phases. To operationalize these capabilities, a hybrid LOIN-LOD structure was 
introduced to incorporate both geometric (LOD-G) and informational (LOD-I) dimensions. The pro -
posed framework synthesizes the insights derived from the expert interviews and the previous platforms 
(e.g. G €okg €ur 2015 ; Helander and Singh 2016 ; BSI 2018b , 2018c ; Daniotti et al. 2020 ; Elagiry et al. 2020 ; 
Bolpagni and Hooper 2021 ; Daniotti, Lupica Spagnolo, et al. 2022 ; Daniotti, Masera, et al. 2022; 
Ismaeel et al. 2023 ; Pham 2023 ; Pham and Likhitruangsilp 2023 ; Salzano, Parisi, et al. 2023 ; Spudys 
et al. 2023 ; Pham et al. 2024 , 2025 ). Detailed discussion on LOD-I and LOD-G can be found in Pham 
et al. ( 2024 ).

� The stakeholders emphasized the necessity of real-time alerts, zone-specific visualization, and thresh -

� The BIM-IoT experts highlighted the importance of metadata, including sensor calibration logs,

Their guidance directly shaped the structure of connection pathways, especially CN_PV, CN_VS,

Application of the BIM-enhanced DT platform
The proposed BIM-enhanced DT platform was applied to the renovation project of an auditorium at 
Chulalongkorn University campus, Bangkok, Thailand. This application aimed to verify the CDE, IoT- 
BIM integration, and rule-based automation of the proposed system. To evaluate the system’s efficacy, 
the following capabilities were assessed:
� Continuous transmission of reliable environmental data with real-time notifications to ensure timely 
alerts when pollutant levels (e.g. PM

� Supporting comprehensive data handling, including the acquisition, transmission, visualization, simu -

informed decision-making.
� Provision of spatial context, ensuring each monitoring device was accurately placed and its data was 
correctly contextualized within the BIM environment.
� Evidence-based decision support, enabled through the integration of real-time environmental data 
into the project management workflow, which supports informed decision-making and requires con -
struction parties to propose and implement data-driven mitigation strategies to reduce pollution 
sources.
We first defined the project information requirements, the definition of information exchange proto -
cols, and the clarity of EIR among the involved stakeholders. Based on the proposed LOIN, we applied 
the schema to the case study. An as-needed BIM model of the facility was developed with the proposed 
LOIN to support the BIM-enhanced DT. A pilot test for the CDE solution was then carried out. The 
CDE workflow and solution were adapted from those of Pham and Likhitruangsilp ( 2023 ), Pham et al.

present the definition of LOIN-LOD for the as-needed BIM model, which is subsequently utilized for 
digital twin development, rule-based automation, and live environmental monitoring services. 
Collectively, they document the platform’s capability to bridge the gap between physical site conditions 
and digital twin functionalities, demonstrating the practical implementation of LOIN specifications and 
real-time IoT–BIM synchronization within a unified monitoring interface.

Discussion
Traditional methods, such as manual inspections or periodic sampling with handheld devices, offer low 
temporal resolution and often involve delays of several days between data collection and reporting. 
This leads to prolonged exposure due to undetected exceedances between inspections. On the contrary, 
the BIM-enhanced DT solution provides automatic alerts that trigger immediate mitigation actions. It 
delivers immediate feedback through continuous monitoring at 1-s intervals via the IoT solution and 
provides near-real-time updates on the website. Automated updates improve coordination across shift- 
based teams, reducing delays, enhancing communication, and ensuring smoother transitions.
In this case study application, the system enabled continuous monitoring during renovation, with 
sensors transmitting data every second to a live database. This automated flow supported real-time pro -
gress tracking via the monitoring web and dashboard. Gauge charts provided instant visual feedback 
on safe limits for faster decisions, while line charts revealed historical trends, anomalies, and correla -
tions. Visualized data on dust, noise, and vibrations supported early risk detection and proactive work -
flow adjustments. The combined use of gauge and line charts with spatial information enhanced 
situational awareness, enabled early warnings, and informed interventions based on both real-time con -
ditions and emerging patterns.
The evolution from 3D BIM to integrated BIM (iBIM) and DT can be evaluated based on how con -
sistently and dynamically a VM reflects its corresponding physical asset. In this context, LOIN serves as 
a standardized mechanism to ensure that the digital twin contains the precise information required to 
achieve these advanced levels of maturity. Enhanced BIM uses LOIN principles to ensure that specific 
end users receive only the precise quality and quantity of information needed for their roles. Our

manage physical assets long after construction is finished. This approach ensures that asset information 
is directly relevant to daily operational tasks and strategic decision-making.
For the IoT integration in CDE, defining the minimum LOIN for BIM-based DT usage facilitates 
the identification of information requirements for sensor data schemas, defines the scope of IoT sensor 
networks, and specifies standards for representing data. It ensures that dynamic IoT data and static 
metadata from IoT systems are integrated coherently into the DT, supporting real-time monitoring, 
decision-making, and the extraction of IoT-related concepts for analytical or operational purposes. This 
structured approach is further operationalized through Information Delivery Specifications (IDS) and 
standardized data dictionaries like bSDD, creating machine-readable, interoperable datasets (Wang Y 
et al. 2024 ; Fischer et al. 2025 ). This standardization is essential for achieving higher DT maturity, ena -
bling advanced analytics, and supporting predictive operational decision-making, moving beyond mere 
geometric representation to ensure the model’s operational integrity and analytical utility.
In the development of an IFC-based system in a BIM workflow, LOIN defines the creation of accur -
ate geometric representations and supports semantic and textual mapping across different systems. 
Defining LOIN and corresponding LOD for BIM and IFC models gives semantic technologies 
(semantic ontologies and knowledge graphs) a strong base to build on for future uses. Such a semantic 
integration enables the development of cognitive DT systems capable of supporting automated, system- 
of-systems decision-making, thereby linking high-level architectural frameworks with real-time, context- 
aware operational intelligence. It enables the system to interpret, reason, and adapt to complex 
operational scenarios, creating opportunities for leveraging AI to support predictive analytics, auto -
mated decision-making, and intelligent management of construction and renovation processes. In this 
sense, the architecture represents a step toward a more cognitively aware and AI-enabled DT paradigm, 
bridging the gap between conventional BIM-DT applications and advanced, context-sensitive intelligent 
systems.

Contribution and limitation
The maturity of DT systems is determined not only by the integration of real-time data but also by the 
consistency between BIM states, nD dimensions, and structured information requirements. While 
existing implementations often extend BIM models closer to the as-is state by incorporating 
multi-dimensional data (e.g. 3D geometry to 8D occupational safety and nDs iBIM model for DT appli -
cations), sensor-driven data acquisition, and expanded data connectivity, they frequently lack mecha -
nisms to control information relevance and quality. Consequently, the progression toward an intelligent 
DT is not solely driven by data acquisition but by the ability to structure and constrain information 
effectively. This study addresses this limitation by introducing a LOIN-based framework that governs 
the specification, structuring, and management of information within the BIM database, thereby ena -
bling a more robust transition from static BIM representations to intelligent, data-driven DT 
environments.
The proposed system fills the research gap between static building documentation and operational 
responsiveness by incorporating real-time environmental data into the BIM environment. Firstly, it 
transforms pollution management from reactive diagnostics to proactive control enables automated 
mitigation through spatially linked dashboards, threshold-triggered alerts, and regulatory reporting pro -
tocols. The proposed BIM-enhanced DT platform delivers actionable tools integrating semantically 
enriched BIM models, IoT-integrated data streams, and context-sensitive response mechanisms suitable 
for real-world renovation applications.
Secondly, our DT platform advanced the conceptualization of information requirements across the 
project life cycle by distinguishing the needs of new construction projects from those of renovation 
projects. This knowledge is valuable in enhancing the adoption of digital asset management in the built 
environment. The hybrid LOIN-LOD framework supports the transition from any existing state of a 
BIM model to an as-needed BIM model, reducing unnecessary data generation and mitigating the 
problem of information for information’s sake. This approach ensures that only the information neces -
sary to support specific project objectives, environmental monitoring, or regulatory compliance is cap -
tured and maintained.
The technology implementation enables stakeholders to make decisions promptly while reducing 
risks concerning structural integrity and workers’ safety. The alignment of BIM LOIN-LOD with DT 
classes and data maturity has increased the understanding of how BIM contributes to DT development 
and clarifies the LOIN requirements for DT implementation. The integration of BIM, IoT, and cloud- 
based automation verified the platform’s potential for broader deployment across similarly constrained 
retrofit scenarios, especially where pre-digital building environments and strict environmental compli -
ance intersect. This platform can reduce the burden on data acquisition and model development. It can 
also enhance transparency among stakeholders by clarifying the scope of work and responsibilities from 
the outset. The early definition of appropriate LOD-G provides a practical basis for BIM implementa -
tion, particularly when integrating IoT systems within the BIM model.
Even though our research can successfully fill several crucial research gaps, it entails several limita -
tions that have not yet been addressed, such as complaint management and participatory mitigation 
strategies. The proposed framework includes an HVAC-MEPF system integration and actuators 
designed to mitigate dust pollution during construction activities by automating responses when pollu -
tion exceeds the thresholds. Yet the implementation in this research remains limited. Due to time and 
budget constraints, the system cannot perform construction progress tracking and the complaint recep -
tion channel. Besides, while LOIN for the as-needed BIM model and the architectural framework has 
been defined, the semantic representation and ontology-based integration have not been fully explored, 
leading to limited applications in automation data preprocessing, semantic interpretation, and intelli -
gent control execution.
User interface and decision support are also our major challenges. For example, visualizing large 
datasets on Autodesk Forge created performance bottlenecks. Site managers preferred simplified key 
performance indicators over raw sensor feeds. To improve usability, dashboards were optimized to dis -
play aggregated KPIs such as compliance rates and exceedance frequency, with drill-down options for 
detailed data. Pre-rendered hotspot visualizations further reduced loading times. While these

Other limitations undermining the overall trustworthiness of this DT system are the reliability of 
low-cost sensors, the absence of a clear workflow with coding techniques for front-end and back-end 
integrations, and the lack of a calibration loop. Implementing low-cost sensors requires advanced skills 
in algorithm development, coding, and calibration to ensure the system’s accuracy. Processing the large 
volume of real-time data also demands substantial hardware investment.
Conclusion and recommendation
In this paper, we presented a BIM-enhanced DT architecture tailored for pollution management in 
building renovation projects. The platform was designed for real-time environmental monitoring 
and regulatory responsiveness, thereby clarifying how to establish a BIM-based DT for renovating 
existing buildings. By defining minimum information requirements and aligning them with project 
objectives, we can resolve major uncertainties in the technical and practical implementation of 
BIM-based DTs, offering a pathway for more effective environmental and safety management in 
construction.
The proposed DT architecture was characterized by a semantically layered decomposition strategy. It 
was represented by five interdependent dimensions, namely, PEs, VMs, DD, Ss, and CNs. Each dimen -
sion contributes to the system’s competence to support dynamic, data-driven decision-making in oper -
ationally sensitive contexts. The LOIN framework was leveraged to define as-needed BIM models, 
delineating baseline information structures necessary for dynamic pollution monitoring. This platform 
aligned LOIN and LOD with project objectives to optimize resource allocation, sensor placement, and 
project stakeholder engagement.
Future research should prioritize the adoption of semantic ontologies and IFC standards to enhance 
interoperability, support spatial reasoning, and enable automated hotspot identification. A key evolution 
involves expanding the current five-dimensional architecture into a CDT framework. This expansion 
requires integrating cognitive essentials as default components, such as a dedicated knowledge layer 
and reasoning engines, to enable the cognitive aspects of the DT.
Another promising direction is the advancement of cognitive DTs that move beyond monitoring to 
reasoning, predicting, and recommending mitigation strategies. AI-driven analytics, anomaly detection, 
adaptive rule engines, and automated workflows, such as dust suppression or schedule adjustments trig -
gered by exceedances, can significantly improve responsiveness and efficiency. Other future research 
includes complaint management modules and participatory strategies to support user-driven feedback 
and adaptive responses. The integration of AI and camera systems can also extend the functionality of 
BIM-based DTs.
