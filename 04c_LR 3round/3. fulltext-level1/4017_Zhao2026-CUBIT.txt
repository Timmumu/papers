 A B S T R A C T
High-rise building façade inspection is essential but hazardous and labor-intensive. Although UAV imaging 
and defect segmentation methods can recognize defects, most studies stop at 2D masks and lack the scale and 
instance structure needed for grade quantification. Public façade datasets remain scarce, often low-resolution, 
crack-only, and annotated for semantic segmentation. This paper introduces CUBIT-InSeg, a high-resolution 
UAV building façade defect instance segmentation dataset. It contains 6996 images at 4800 × 3200 with 62,187 
annotated instances of two safety-critical defects, cracks and spalling, in realistic multi-instance scenes. Over 
80 models were benchmarked under a unified protocol, and transferability was assessed via zero-shot testing 
on two cross-domain datasets. A deployment workflow was further demonstrated that registers defects to an 
as-is digital twin and converts segmentation masks into metric measures (e.g., crack width/length, spalling 
area) for standard-aligned severity evaluation. In real-world validation, the workflow based on CUBIT-InSeg 
dataset enables reproducible research toward scalable, quantitative façade condition assessment.
1. Introduction
Civil infrastructure is vulnerable to damage induced by environmen-
tal exposure, external loads, material deterioration, and construction 
deficiencies. For high-rise buildings, façade defects such as cracks and 
concrete spallings not only degrade durability but may also pose safety 
hazards, motivating periodic inspections for condition assessment and 
maintenance planning. In practice, close-range visual inspection and 
non-destructive testing (NDT) devices (e.g., optical imaging [1], laser 
scanning [2], impact echo [3], and ground-penetrating radar [4]) 
are commonly used for defect investigation. However, human-centric 
inspection is subjective, labor-intensive, and potentially hazardous, 
especially for high-rise façades and large inspection areas. To improve 
safety and efficiency, robotic platforms such as unmanned aerial ve-
hicles (UAVs) have been increasingly adopted to facilitate façade data 
collection and automated defect analysis [5]. Meanwhile, deep learning 
has enabled rapid progress in visual recognition [6], outperforming 
many traditional feature-based pipelines [7], and has been increasingly 
adopted in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) do-
main [8]. For façade inspection, pixel-level understanding is crucial 
since defects are often thin, irregular, spatially distributed, and strongly 
multi-scale, while multiple defects can co-exist in the same view. In 
this context, instance segmentation is particularly suitable due to pro-
viding both instance-level localization and pixel-accurate masks, which 
not only separate multiple defect instances but also directly support

Despite this progress, deep learning models are notoriously data-

reliably to building façade defects due to substantial differences in 
viewpoint, surface texture, scale distribution, and background clutter, 
while dense pixel-level annotations are expensive in real inspection en-

inspection, the ultimate objective is not merely visual recognition, but 
severity grading for condition evaluation and maintenance prioritiza-
tion, which in turn requires a reliable defect modeling pipeline based 
on physically interpretable defect attributes. Public defect datasets 
are therefore scarce and predominantly focus on pavement scenar-
ios; many are crack-only, low-resolution, and annotated for binary 
semantic segmentation rather than instance segmentation [11]. This 
limitation is particularly restrictive for our task, because semantic 
labels alone are insufficient when downstream engineering analysis 
requires defect-wise localization, physical quantification, and aggrega-
tion at the façade-component level. In particular, UAV-based façade 
inspection must maintain a non-negligible standoff distance from the 
wall for flight safety and coverage; at such distances, low-resolution 
imagery often fails to resolve fine-grained defects (e.g., tiny cracks),

provide RGB images and visual labels only, without controlled acqui-

To address the gap, we present CUBIT-InSeg, a UAV-collected high-
resolution building façade defect dataset with instance-level annotations 
tailored for high-rise inspection and downstream physical quantifica-
tion. The starting point of this paper is therefore not simply to release 
a new dataset, but to establish the necessary data foundation for a 
severity-grading-oriented technical route. During the development of 
our defect physical modeling workflow, we found that existing pub-
lic datasets could not adequately support this objective due to their 
limited resolution, annotation granularity, scene realism, and lack of 
compatibility with controlled UAV inspection geometry. CUBIT-InSeg 
contains 6996 high-resolution images at 4800 × 3200 acquired by 
UAVs, with 62,187 annotated defect instances. The dataset covers two 
safety-relevant defect types, cracks and spallings, and includes realistic 
multi-instance inspection scenes with complex textures, illumination 
changes, and aerial viewpoints. Moreover, the dataset is primarily 
collected under an approximately equal-distance imaging strategy, and 
is compatible with multi-UAV collaborative acquisition in practice, bet-
ter reflecting the operational geometry of high-rise façade inspection. 
In this sense, CUBIT-InSeg is characterized not merely by its dataset 
scale, but by its deliberate design as an engineering bridge from high-
resolution façade defect instance segmentation to downstream physical 
quantification, defect modeling, and DT-oriented severity assessment.
Beyond releasing the dataset, we establish reproducible baselines 
through systematic benchmarking to provide a unified reference for 
model comparison and to examine the usability of CUBIT-InSeg as a 
benchmark resource. We evaluate more than 80 representative state-
of-the-art (SOTA) models under a unified training and evaluation 
protocol, spanning (1) End-to-end frameworks covering both CNN-
based and transformer-based paradigms, and (2) Single-stage, real-time, 
deployment-oriented YOLO-style variants. To assess robustness and 
external validity, we further conduct cross-domain zero-shot eval-
uation by directly testing models trained on CUBIT-InSeg on two 
public infrastructure crack datasets without target-domain fine-tuning 
(Highway-Crack [14] and Crack-Seg [15]).
Finally, while many studies of defect recognition only report in-the-
lab recognition metrics (e.g., AP/IoU), practical inspection workflows 
require more than visual recognition alone. Accordingly, we connect 
instance-level predictions to a downstream deployment-oriented, digi-
tal twin (DT) quantification workflow. Under the equal-distance acqui-
sition assumption, pixel masks are converted into physically meaningful 
measures such as crack width/length and spalling area, while instance-
level localization (bounding boxes) supports defect registration and 
aggregation at the façade component level. These component-level 
indicators enable standard-aligned severity grading consistent with BS 
ISO 15686-7:2017 [16] and local façade inspection practice in Hong 
Kong [17,18]. Based on the quantified crack and spalling metrics, 
we derive component-level severity ranks and store the results in 
the DT to support reporting, visualization, and maintenance decision-
making. This establishes a closed-loop workflow from visual sensing 
and instance segmentation to metric computation, component-level 
assessment, and DT-enabled decision support, as illustrated in Fig.  1. 
To further demonstrate the practical applicability of this pipeline, we 
select YOLOv9 [19] as the best-performing model based on compre-
hensive results across CUBIT-InSeg and two cross-domain test sets, and 
present a real high-rise façade inspection scenario with DT-oriented 
registration, metric quantification, and severity rating.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• A high-resolution façade defect instance segmentation
dataset. We curate and annotate 6996 high-resolution façade

is provided in Table  1 to give a quick overview of the dataset 
characteristics.
• Systematic benchmarking of more than 80 models to vali-
date dataset usability. We conduct unified training and eval-
uation on more than 80 representative models, including end-
to-end instance segmentation families (covering both CNN-based 
and transformer-based paradigms) and a broad set of single-

the community.
• Cross-domain zero-shot evaluation to assess transferability.

2. Related work
We reviews the related research that is relevant to this paper from 
three perspectives. Section 2.1 surveys public infrastructure defect 
datasets, with particular attention to collection scenarios and annota-
tion granularity, in order to clarify the remaining gap in UAV-based 
façade instance segmentation. Section 2.2 then summarizes represen-
tative defect segmentation methods, spanning semantic segmentation, 
end-to-end instance segmentation, and deployment-oriented single-
stage frameworks. Section 2.3 further reviews recent efforts on defect 
quantification and DT integration for UAV-based inspection, thereby 
linking visual defect recognition with downstream physical assessment 
and management-oriented deployment.
2.1. Infrastructure defect datasets
Existing infrastructure defect datasets can be understood from two 
complementary perspectives: (1) collection scenario and (2) annotation 
granularity/task type. From the scenario perspective, public datasets 
are dominated by pavement surfaces (such as GAPs series [20,21] and 
its variants GAPs-10m [22], Crack500 [23] and EdmCrack600 [24]), 
followed by the deck of bridge scenarios (Xu [25] and Philipp [26]), 
while datasets targeting buildings [27] are the scarcest. More im-
portantly, a substantial domain gap often exists across infrastructure 
types: even for the same defect category (e.g., cracks), the foreground 
morphology (width, orientation, and scale distribution) and the back-
ground appearance (material textures, coatings, and stains) can differ 
markedly with the asset type and viewing geometry, making cross-
scenario reuse of data and models non-trivial [11]. Many pavement 
crack datasets [22,24] are captured under near-planar viewpoints with 
relatively homogeneous textures and predominantly focus on crack-
type defects, which inherently limits their transferability to façade 
inspection where textures are more complex, viewpoints are oblique, 
illumination varies significantly, and defect scales span a wider range. 
Even among unmanned-system captured datasets, Highway-Crack [14] 
is one of the few UAV-acquired datasets; however, it remains con-
strained to post-earthquake highway surfaces and does not reflect the 
operational geometry and visual characteristics of high-rise façade 
inspection. Public datasets for building façades are even more limited: 
Crack-Seg [15] contains partial building scenes but lacks UAV-faithful 
viewpoints and sufficient resolution, while UAV75 [28] provides UAV 
imagery but includes only a small number of low-resolution samples, 
making it inadequate for training and benchmarking modern deep 
segmentation models.
From the annotation perspective, a clear granularity gap also exists. 
Early resources often adopt image-level classification annotations for 
defect presence or coarse categories, like CCIC [29], GAPs series [20,
21], Xu et al. [25] and Philipp et al. [26] proposed datasets. Later 
datasets introduce box-level detection labels for coarse localization, 
like RDD series [30,31] and Zhao et al. [27] proposed datasets. More 
commonly, datasets provide semantic segmentation mask, typically bi-
nary (one defect type vs. background), especially for cracks, such as 
GAPs-10m [22], Crack500 [23], Highway-Crack [14], Crack-Seg [15], 
EdmCrack600 [24] and UAV75 [28]. While useful for pixel-level delin-
eation, semantic masks do not separate multiple instances of the same 
defect type and are less suitable for instance-wise statistics, tracking, 
and component-level registration. In contrast, instance segmentation 
datasets that jointly support object-level localization and pixel-accurate 
boundaries are much rarer, particularly under UAV viewpoints and for 
building façades.
To further expose the practical gap in unmanned system-captured 
datasets for segmentation-oriented tasks, Table  2 compares recent de-
fect segmentation datasets acquired by unmanned platforms
(UAV/UGV). This comparison shows that UAV-acquired datasets are 
scarce, with only two such datasets identified, and only one of them 
targets building scenarios. Moreover, these UAV datasets provide pixel-
level semantic masks rather than instance-level annotations. Quanti-
tatively, compared with UAV75 dataset, which is the only prior UAV 
building dataset listed in Table  2, CUBIT-InSeg increases the image res-
olution from 512 × 512 to 4800 × 3200 and the number of images from 
75 to 6996, while additionally providing 62,187 instance-level annota-
tions. Together with the commonly observed limitations in resolution, 
defect density, which often corresponds to single-defect images, and 
defect-type diversity, existing resources remain insufficient for realistic 
high-rise façade inspection and downstream engineering assessment. 
Based on Table  2, several representative datasets are further compared 
qualitatively in Fig.  2, which more intuitively illustrates that these 
existing datasets not only focus on a single defect type, namely cracks, 
but also contain annotation errors, highlighted by yellow ellipses.
Overall, these observations call for a public benchmark that is 
simultaneously high-resolution, UAV-faithful, façade-centric, multi-type, 
and instance-level, enabling reproducible training and evaluation, cross-
domain validation, and subsequent workflows such as defect regis-
tration, metric quantification, and standard-aligned severity grading. 
CUBIT-InSeg is constructed to fill this gap.
2.2. Defect segmentation methods
Defect segmentation methods can be broadly categorized into se-
mantic segmentation and instance segmentation. Semantic segmen-
tation models (e.g., U-Net and DeepLab families) have been widely 
used for crack/spalling region extraction in civil infrastructure [32,33]. 
These methods are effective for pixel-wise defect region delineation 
and have played an important role in infrastructure inspection, espe-
cially for crack-focused studies. Recent works have further explored 
task-specific designs for thin and elongated crack patterns under com-
plex backgrounds. For example, DSYOLO [34] incorporates dynamic 
snake convolution into a YOLOv8-seg framework to better adapt to 
tortuous crack morphology and preserve fine structural continuity in 
challenging tunnel environments. Although developed for tunnel lining 
cracks, this line of work is highly relevant to façade inspection because 
façade cracks also exhibit slender geometry, low contrast, and strong 
background interference. At the same time, façade inspection is not 
limited to cracks alone, but also involves other defect categories such

Despite their usefulness, semantic segmentation methods typically 
cannot separate multiple defect instances in the same image, which 
limits instance-wise counting, tracking, registration, and quantitative 
reporting. This limitation is particularly relevant in UAV-based façade 
inspection, where multiple defects of different categories may co-exist 
within a single high-resolution image. Instance segmentation provides 
both instance-level localization and pixel-accurate masks, making it 
more suitable for engineering-oriented defect representation and down-
stream quantification. Mainstream approaches include (1) end-to-end 
two-stage or unified frameworks (e.g., Mask R-CNN [35] and its ex-
tensions) that often achieve strong accuracy but with higher com-
putational cost; (2) transformer-based instance/panoptic segmentation 
frameworks that leverage global context for robustness under complex 
textures, such as Deformable-DETR [36] and MaskFormer [37]; and (3) 
deployment-oriented single-stage instance segmentation, often repre-
sented by YOLO-style variants, which emphasize efficiency and real-
time capability, such as YOLOv8 [38] and Hyper-YOLO [39]. Multi-
modal large vision model-driven segmentation (e.g., SAM [40]) has also 
attracted attention, but reliable fine-defect delineation and consistent 
instance outputs in engineering settings still depend on domain-specific 
data and evaluation benchmarks [41]. Given the rapidly evolving 
model space and the strong dependence on data properties, we con-
duct unified benchmarking on more than 80 representative models to 
establish reproducible baselines and support practical model selection.
2.3. Defect quantification and DT integration for UAV-based inspection
With the widespread adoption of UAVs in façade and infrastruc-
ture inspection, vision-based defect detection has become a de facto 
solution in both research and practice. However, many current UAV 
deployments still rely on largely manual or experience-driven acqui-
sition strategies, where image quality, viewing geometry, and data 
consistency are difficult to control [42]. Such practices limit not only 
the achievable detection accuracy, but also the reproducibility, scala-
bility, and traceability of inspection results across buildings and over 
time [43]. As a result, there is an increasing need to couple UAV-
based visual inspection with data- and management-oriented require-
ments, including controlled image quality, consistent acquisition ge-
ometry, and reliable data provenance, to support systematic inspection 
workflows rather than isolated visual surveys [44].
Alongside data acquisition, a more critical challenge lies in how 
high-accuracy detection and segmentation results are utilized for in-
spection management and engineering decision-making. While ad-
vances in learning-based perception have gradually shifted research 
attention from defect detection toward physically meaningful defect 
modeling and condition assessment [45], many existing studies still 
emphasize visual outputs or geometric reconstruction without deliver-
ing actionable quantitative indicators. Earlier works focused primarily 
on identifying defect regions in images, such as road damage detec-
tion [46] and building defect recognition [47], whereas more recent 
efforts aim to recover geometric and metric attributes to support 
engineering evaluation. Two complementary directions have emerged: 
integrating multi-view photogrammetry or depth-enhanced imaging to 
reconstruct cracks or damaged regions in 3D for shape-aware [48] 
or volume-aware assessment [49], and leveraging point clouds with 
graph-based learning to build defect representations enriched with 
geometric and semantic attributes [50]. In parallel, aligning UAV 
observations with DT representations has been explored as a prac-
tical route toward component-level localization and life-cycle asset 
management [13].
Despite this progress, real-world façade inspection remains con-
strained by the lack of reproducible physical scale and standard-aligned 
severity indicators in public datasets and evaluation protocols [51]. In 
high-rise inspection scenarios, UAVs must operate at a safe standoff dis-
tance; without controlled acquisition geometry or explicit scale assump-
tions, pixel-level predictions cannot be reliably converted into crack 
width, length, or spalling area, nor can they be consistently registered 
and aggregated at the component level for assessment [52]. CUBIT-
InSeg is therefore collected under an approximately equal-distance 
acquisition strategy to facilitate metric conversion from instance-level 
predictions, while enabling defect registration and DT-oriented aggre-
gation for quantitative façade condition assessment.

3. Dataset establishment and analysis
In this paper, we develop an equal-distance UAV imaging frame-
work for the establishment of a high-quality façade defect instance 
segmentation dataset, as illustrated in Fig.  1. The sample images of 
CUBIT-InSeg are shown in Fig.  3. The central idea is to capture façade 
images from a prescribed, nearly constant standoff distance to the 
building surface, so as to maintain a stable image scale across view-
points and façades. This acquisition strategy is important for improving 
annotation consistency, defect visibility, and cross-image comparability 
in UAV-based façade inspection. To achieve this, our framework ex-
tends recent explore-then-exploit multi-UAV coverage schemes (shown 
in Fig.  4) for infrastructure inspection [12] and integrates them with 
equal-distance viewpoint generation and façade-oriented inspection 
planning, forming the basis for reproducible dataset collection and 
subsequent analysis.
3.1. Path planning for data collection
We adopt an explore-then-exploit paradigm for multi-UAV façade 
inspection, following recent hierarchical coverage frameworks. Dur-
ing the exploration stage, each UAV performs depth-visual SLAM to 
estimate its six-degree-of-freedom pose and incrementally reconstruct 
the façade surface and surrounding obstacles. The environment is dis-
cretized into a 3D occupancy grid, where each voxel stores occupancy 
probability, distance to the nearest obstacle, and a reconstructability 
score reflecting multi-view observability and safety margins. This yields 
a density map 𝑅(𝐩) that guides coverage toward regions with high 
reconstruction potential.

3.2. Data cleaning and annotation
After data collection, we perform data cleaning to remove unneces-
sary or visually unreliable samples, which not only reduces annotation 
workload but also improves the overall dataset quality. In total, we 
collected over 8000 raw images. We removed images that contain no 
defect instances, as well as samples with severe motion blur, defocus, 
overexposure, underexposure, or other visual conditions under which 
defect boundaries cannot be reliably judged by human inspection. After 
cleaning, 6996 images were retained for annotation.
We then conduct instance-level defect annotation using the widely-
used open-source annotation tool LabelMe [53], where each defect 
object is delineated by an independent polygon (i.e., one polygon 
per instance). The upper part of Fig.  5 illustrates the LabelMe user 
interface. The left panel displays the image, where annotators click 
sequential points to form a polygon that tightly encloses a target defect 
instance. The right panel provides the customized defect category list, 
the instance list, and the image list to be annotated. During annotation, 
we follow two strict principles: (1) each defect instance must be fully 
contained within its corresponding polygon, and the polygon boundary 
should fit the defect contour as closely as possible; (2) every defect 
instance that can be reliably identified and delineated in an image 
should be annotated without omission.
To ensure high-quality and consistent labels, our annotation work-
flow consists of three stages. First, three student annotators carry 
out the initial labeling of the images. Second, they cross-check each 
other’s annotations to identify samples with ambiguous or questionable 
labels. Third, the remaining ambiguities are resolved by the first author

Typical borderline cases arise when defect boundaries are affected 
by non-uniform light-shadow transitions, noisy or stain-like background 
interference, low-contrast texture patterns, or partial overlap between 
different defect types. Rather than applying a single fixed size thresh-
old as a universal inclusion rule, we determine inclusion according 
to whether a defect instance can be clearly recognized and reliably 
delineated in the native high-resolution image. Defect-like patterns 
that are extremely faint, visually indeterminate, or highly ambiguous 
are excluded. In particular, fine-scale cracks are annotated only when 
their extent can still be visually traced with sufficient confidence. 
In addition, when crack and spalling regions overlap, we prioritize 
spalling annotation to avoid inconsistent double labeling of the same 
damaged area.
Since LabelMe exports annotations in JSON format, which is not al-
ways directly compatible with common training and validation
pipelines, we further convert the labels into both PNG mask images and 
YOLO-style polygon TXT files while preserving the instance boundaries.

subsequent training and evaluation.

3.3.1. Statistics and target position distribution analysis of CUBIT-InSeg

Fig.  6 summarizes the dataset statistics and instance distribution

instances, including 51,865 crack instances (83%) and 10,322 spalling

inspection. Notably, spalling is typically associated with more severe

by their pixel-area ratio within the image (Fig.  6(b)): small (<5%),

dataset (41,299; 66%), followed by small instances (14,334; 23%) and

Fig.  6(c) reports the background scenarios demonstrated in Fig.  3.

followed by noisy backgrounds (2433 images), with the remainder 
belonging to locally high-contrast conditions induced by illumination 
and shadows. Overall, the diverse and challenging scene settings in 
CUBIT-InSeg dataset provide a solid basis for cross-domain evaluation 
and real-world deployment tests.
Beyond instance counts, the spatial distribution of targets is an-
other critical factor in evaluating an instance segmentation dataset. 
The placement of defects within images influences a model’s spatial 
awareness, which represents its ability to recognize objects across 
different locations, and directly affects robustness to target offset or 
positional shifts. In practical UAV inspection scenarios, defects often 
appear sparsely and unpredictably across the façade. Models trained 
on datasets with well-distributed targets are therefore more capable 
of detecting defects located near image borders, in corners, or in 
unconventional positions. Fig.  6(d) visualizes the scatter plot of ground-
truth bounding-box centers for all defect instances. The distribution is 
reasonably uniform across the image plane, with a moderate concentra-
tion near the central region: approximately 63% of all targets (39,078 
instances) fall within the normalized range [𝑥, 𝑦] ∈ {0 .5 ± 0.175}. Such a 
distribution ensures that models trained on our CUBIT-InSeg dataset 
develop strong spatial generalization capabilities and exhibit higher 
robustness when deployed in real-world UAV inspection scenarios.

To further characterize the geometric variability of CUBIT-InSeg,

challenge for both detection and segmentation.

occupy less than 0.01% of the image while simultaneously handling 
large-scale structural defects. Together, these characteristics underscore 
the geometric richness and real-world complexity embodied in the 
CUBIT-InSeg dataset.
4. Physical quantification and DT integration
While the previous section focuses on dataset establishment and 
analysis, the value of CUBIT-InSeg also lies in its compatibility with 
physically grounded façade assessment. In this section, we describe 
how pixel-level defect segmentations can be converted into physically 
interpretable measurements and further integrated into a DT-oriented 
façade representation. Benefiting from the equal-distance imaging setup 
and calibrated geometric conversion, defect attributes such as crack 
length, crack width, and spalling area can be estimated in consistent 
physical units and then registered to corresponding façade elements 
in the reconstructed model. This enables the transition from image-
level defect recognition to component-level condition description and 
severity-aware defect management.

5. Benchmark evaluation experiments of the proposed CUBIT-
InSeg dataset
To comprehensively evaluate the proposed CUBIT-InSeg dataset, 
we trained an extensive suite of deep learning models, encompassing 
17 model families and more than 80 individual networks, including 
convolution-based architectures (Starnet [54], FasterNet [55],
MobileNetV4 [56], EMO [57], ConvNeXtV2 [58]), transformer-
based architectures (Swin-Transformer [59], CSwin-
Transformer [60], RepViT [61], EfficientViT [62]), and YOLO 
variants (YOLOv8 [38], YOLOv9 [19], YOLOv10 [63], YOLOv11 [64],
YOLOv12 [65], YOLOv13 [66], Mamba-YOLO [67], Hyper-YOLO
[39]). These SOTA approaches cover a wide spectrum of design
paradigms and represent the leading techniques in modern object detec-
tion and instance segmentation. Leveraging such architectural diversity 
allows us to establish a comprehensive benchmark and to compare 
how different model families behave under a unified evaluation setting 
on CUBIT-InSeg. This helps position CUBIT-InSeg as a standardized 
benchmark resource rather than attributing performance differences to 
the dataset itself.
For evaluation metrics, we utilize mean Average Precision (mAP) 
by following the widely adopted MS COCO [10] instance segmentation 
task, reporting bounding GT-Box mAP0.5 and mAP0.5∶0.95 for objects 
localization, as well as mask mAP0.5 and mAP0.5∶0.95 for objects seg-
mentation. Adopting these widely accepted metrics also aligns our 
dataset with common object-centric benchmarks, thereby enhancing its 
comparability and general applicability within the broader community.

Among the aforementioned SOTA models, both convolution-based and 
transformer-based architectures serve as powerful feature extraction 
backbones. To ensure a fair and controlled comparison, we integrate 
all these backbones into the Ultralytics [38,64] framework, which is 
also used by the YOLO series and its variants. In our unified benchmark, 
only the backbone is replaced across different model variants, while the 
neck and detection/segmentation heads are kept consistent under the 
same Ultralytics (YOLOv11-style) design. Specifically, the multi-scale 
feature maps extracted by each backbone are aligned and connected 
through the corresponding P3, P4, and P5 levels to the same shared 
neck and head for subsequent feature fusion, detection, and instance 
segmentation. As a result, the primary variation among compared mod-
els comes from the backbone itself, whereas the neck, head, prediction 
pipeline, training recipe, and inference protocol remain unified. Such 
a design is intended not to fully preserve every original architecture-
specific component, but to establish a controlled, reproducible, and 
horizontally comparable benchmark setting for CUBIT-InSeg. For a 
dataset work, this unified protocol is important because it reduces 
confounding factors introduced by heterogeneous implementations and 
allows performance differences to more clearly reflect how different 
backbone families respond to the dataset. Unless otherwise noted, data 
preprocessing settings (including image resizing, normalization, and 
anchor settings) follow the default Ultralytics pipeline implementation. 
Accordingly, the benchmark results should be interpreted primarily as 
differences among model architectures under a common protocol, while 
the contribution of CUBIT-InSeg lies in providing the high-resolution, 
instance-level, and reproducible evaluation setting (see Fig.  10).

5.3.3. Zero-shot validation on cross-domain datasets
One practical way to further examine the usefulness of a new dataset 
is to evaluate whether models trained on it can transfer to other related 
datasets without additional fine-tuning. Therefore, after completing the 
benchmark evaluation of over 80 individual networks on the proposed 
CUBIT-InSeg dataset, we further assess the cross-domain generalization 
ability of our models under a zero-shot setting. Specifically, we directly 
apply the trained models to the test set parts of two publicly available 
unmanned systems collected datasets, Highway-Crack [14] for pure 
highway data and Crack-Seg [15] for building and pavement mixed 
data, without any training and fine-tuning.
To quantitatively and visually analyze the domain shift between 
datasets, Fig.  13 presents a t-SNE computed from color-histogram de-
scriptors. For each image, 32-bin RGB histograms are extracted, con-
catenated, and normalized to form a compact appearance descriptor. 
These histogram features are standardized and optionally compressed 
using Principal component analysis (PCA) for improved stability, and 
then projected into a two-dimensional embedding space via t-SNE. 
The resulting distributions reveal clear structural differences between 
CUBIT-InSeg and the external datasets. To further quantify the discrep-
ancy, we compute the Euclidean distance between the cluster centers 
of the two t-SNE embeddings as a simple domain-gap metric. The 
distances are 16.63 for Highway-Crack and 7.92 for Crack-Seg. These 
discrepancies in feature-space geometry highlight the inherent chal-
lenges of achieving robust defect instance segmentation under diverse 
real-world conditions, while also suggesting that models trained on 
CUBIT-InSeg exhibit promising transferability to related infrastructure 
inspection scenarios under the zero-shot setting.
Zero-Shot on Highway-Crack Dataset Following the default eval-
uation protocol of Highway-Crack [14], all input images are resized 
to 512 × 512 during zero-shot testing. Table  8 reports the results of 
end-to-end models. Notably, the transformer-based EfficientViT-
M5 achieves the strongest zero-shot generalization, ranking first across 
all four metrics. For box-level evaluation, EMO-5M and FasterNet-l
rank second under Box_AP0.5 and Box_AP0.5∶0.95, respectively, whereas
FasterNet-m and ConvNeXtV2-Large obtain the second-best per-
formance on Mask_AP0.5 and Mask_AP0.5∶0.95.
Table  9 summarizes the zero-shot performance of single-stage real-
time models. In addition to the consistently strong YOLOv9-e, the
YOLOv8 family shows competitive cross-domain robustness: YOLOv8-
l ranks second on Box_AP0.5, while YOLOv8-x ranks second on 
Box_AP0.5∶0.95 and Mask_AP0.5.
Overall, combining the results from Tables  8 and 9, we observe 
that Highway-Crack exhibits typical UAV-view highway scenes with 
pronounced illumination variations and small, distant crack targets. 
Under this challenging cross-domain setting, larger-capacity variants 
(m, b, l, x) generally achieve more stable zero-shot performance across 
both end-to-end and single-stage architectures, while the performance 
differences among the strongest models of each model families remain 
relatively moderate.
Zero-Shot on Crack-Seg Dataset For the Crack-Seg dataset, we 
follow its default setting and resize all input images to 416 × 416 
during zero-shot testing. Table  10 summarizes the results of the end-to-
end models. CSwin-Transformer-Large and EfficientViT-
M5 achieve the highest Box_AP0.5 and Box_AP0.5∶0.95, respectively. Inter-
estingly, despite being the smallest variant, CSwin-Transformer-
Tiny obtains the best performance on two mask-based metrics, sug-
gesting that compact transformer architectures can remain competitive 
under certain cross-domain conditions.
Table  11 shows the results for single-stage real-time models. Un-
der IoU = 0.5, Mamba-YOLO-L and YOLOv10-x deliver the highest 
Box_AP and Mask_AP, respectively. Under the more demanding IoU 
(from 0.5 to 0.95), YOLOv13-x, despite its relatively modest in-
domain performance, outperforms all competitors, achieving the best 
Box_AP and Mask_AP, with the latter being 0.22% higher than the 
second-best model YOLOv9-s.
Taken together, Tables  10 and 11 indicate that, unlike Highway-
Crack, the Crack-Seg dataset does not strictly follow the trend where 
larger models generalize better. Several lightweight models (n, s), 
such as CSwin-Transformer-Tiny, YOLOv9-s, YOLOv10-s, and
Hyper-YOLO-s, achieve top-two performance on multiple metrics, 
revealing a different behavior for this cross-domain ground vehicle-
captured data.

Zero-Shot Performance Comparison between Two Cross-
Domain Defect Datasets Fig.  13 shows that Crack-Seg is closer to 
CUBIT-InSeg in the t-SNE space, indicating a smaller distribution gap 
under the adopted histogram-based descriptors. This observation is 
broadly consistent with the zero-shot instance segmentation results of 
the selected models, where Crack-Seg yields slightly better overall per-
formance than Highway-Crack. Specifically, under the easier IoU = 0.5 
criterion, the selected models perform marginally better on Highway-
Crack in both Box_AP and Mask_AP, as evidenced in Fig.  16(a) and (c), 
where the solid line with ▽ markers lies slightly above the dashed line 
with △ markers. In contrast, under the stricter IoU = 0.5:0.95 criterion, 
the Box_AP and Mask_AP on Highway-Crack decrease more noticeably 
and become lower than those on Crack-Seg. This trend is shown in Fig.

the solid line with ▽ markers, with a visible margin.
This behavior may be related to several dataset-level characteristics. 
Beyond the coarse appearance gap reflected by the t-SNE embeddings 
in Figs.  13, 17 further compares the target-size and target-location 
statistics of the two external datasets. Fig.  17(a) and (c) show that 
crack instances in both datasets are predominantly elongated, while 
Crack-Seg contains relatively more targets whose width and height are 
closer to each other. Fig.  17(b) and (d) show that Crack-Seg targets 
are concentrated more strongly around the image center, whereas 
Highway-Crack targets are distributed more evenly across the image 
plane. Together with the smaller t-SNE distance between Crack-Seg and 
our CUBIT-InSeg, these dataset-level differences likely help explain why 
zero-shot transfer on Crack-Seg is slightly more favorable, especially 
under the stricter IoU = 0.5:0.95 criterion. They also indicate that the 
reported zero-shot evaluation is non-trivial, since both external datasets 
differ from CUBIT-InSeg not only in overall appearance statistics but 
also in instance-level spatial and scale-related characteristics.

related unseen datasets without additional fine-tuning. The qualitative 
results therefore provide encouraging evidence of zero-shot transfer-
ability, although broader cross-domain validation would still be needed 
to support stronger generalization claims.

a mask delineating the defect region at pixel resolution and (2) an 
associated axis-aligned bounding box. As illustrated in Fig.  19(a), these 
instances are overlaid on the original images as green contours and 
their labels.

higher-severity defects in maintenance planning.

defect severity grading and maintenance prioritization as shown in Fig.

where inspection outcomes are ultimately used to support safety as-
sessment, repair scheduling, and lifecycle management within a DT 
environment.

with good robustness. The strong agreement between ground-truth-
based and prediction-based SI results further confirms the practical 
suitability of the proposed physically based quantification pipeline for 
façade inspection and decision support applications.

7. Conclusions
In summary, recent advances in vision-based façade inspection have 
demonstrated the potential of deep learning for defect recognition, yet 
practical deployment remains constrained by limited dataset represen-
tativeness, weak cross-domain generalization, and insufficient linkage 
between pixel-level predictions and building-scale asset representa-
tions. The results presented in this paper indicate that these gaps can 
be jointly addressed by combining transferable instance-level percep-
tion with spatially grounded DT integration. Within this context, our 
contribution includes both an instance-level façade defect dataset and a 
validated pathway for translating recognition results into geometrically 
consistent, building-scale inspection outcomes.
First, we introduce CUBIT-InSeg, a multi-class instance segmen-
tation dataset for building façade defects, mainly captured by UAV 
platforms in realistic urban environments and annotated at the pixel-
instance level following established façade defect standards and profes-
sional inspection guidelines. To validate the usability and benchmark 
value of CUBIT-InSeg, we conduct large-scale training-from-scratch 
experiments on more than 80 models, together with zero-shot inference 
on two public defect datasets without additional fine-tuning. These 
results provide a broad benchmark view of how different architectures 
perform on CUBIT-InSeg and suggest encouraging transferability of 
models trained on it. Building on this dataset and benchmark founda-
tion, we further bridge pixel-level defect perception with geospatially 
consistent representation on a building-scale DT. Starting from high-
resolution RGB imagery acquired by an off-the-shelf drone, the frame-
work integrates instance-level defect segmentation, photogrammetry-
based reconstruction, and GIS-oriented registration to project detected 
defects from image space onto the DT surface. Real-world experiments 
on a multi-storey logistics building in Hong Kong, together with veri-
fication by building engineers using small-sample façade annotations, 
show that the proposed system can generate geometrically consistent 
defect maps across multiple façades and achieve centimetre-level reg-
istration accuracy, which is adequate for infrastructure-scale condition 
assessment and maintenance planning.
Several limitations of the present work should also be acknowl-
edged. First, the physical quantification workflow relies to some extent 
on an approximately equal-distance imaging assumption. This assump-
tion is partially supported by the explore-then-exploit path planning 
strategy, in which an initial surface model is reconstructed and then 
used to generate viewpoints approximately along local outward nor-
mals. As a result, the framework is not restricted to strictly planar 
façades and can also accommodate moderately curved or irregular sur-
faces when local geometry can be reconstructed reliably. Preliminary 
observations on a curved-surface case also suggest that the workflow 
remains applicable when local geometry is sufficiently regular. How-
ever, its performance may degrade in scenes with highly complex 
geometries, such as dense steel-frame structures, severe self-occlusion, 
or strongly fragmented surface features, where both surface modeling 
and stable equal-distance imaging become more difficult. Second, the 
current dataset and experiments focus only on cracks and spalling. 
Although these defect types are highly relevant and representative 
in façade inspection practice, other common façade defects, such as 
corrosion stains, efflorescence, delamination, and water leakage, are 
not yet included. Third, the data are collected within a specific regional 
inspection context and primarily on concrete façades, meaning that the 
current distribution is inevitably shaped by local building typologies, 
material systems, climatic exposure conditions, and inspection practice. 
Accordingly, the generalizability of the dataset and the associated 
workflow to other geographical regions and façade material systems 
still requires further validation.
Future work will therefore focus on extending the dataset and work-
flow along several directions. A larger volume of UAV imagery will be 
collected across more diverse façade geometries, material types, defect 
categories, and environmental conditions to strengthen both dataset 
coverage and model robustness in real-world deployment. In parallel, 
we will explore quantification strategies that are less dependent on 
approximately equal-distance imaging and local planar assumptions, so 
as to improve applicability in more geometrically complex inspection 
scenarios. Beyond spatial diversity, enriching the temporal dimension 
of the data is also important. Long-term and repeated observations of 
the same buildings would enable the analysis of defect progression 
over time, thereby supporting a deeper understanding of deterioration 
mechanisms and providing stronger evidence for maintenance prior-
itization and intervention planning. Nevertheless, the present work 
establishes a solid foundation for instance-level façade defect recog-
nition, engineering-oriented quantification, and DT-integrated building 
inspection, with clear value for both future research and practical asset 
management.
