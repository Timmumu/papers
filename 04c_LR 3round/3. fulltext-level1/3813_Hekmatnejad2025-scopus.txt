ABSTRACT
The safe, cost-effective advance of deep tunnels increasingly hinges on real-time forecasts of structurally 
controlled rock-block failure — capabilities that lie beyond classic Key-Block Theory (KBT) and remain compu -
tationally prohibitive for full-physics numerical models. We introduce the Universal Discontinuity Index (UDi), a 
single probabilistic score that fuses four physically grounded components — physical damage, effective-stress 
intensity, active-fracture ratio, and kinematic feasibility — into a scalable indicator of block instability. The index 
embodies a complete-system view: it treats the fracture network, local stress field, and block release kinematics 
as an integrated, dynamically updating subsystem within a Digital-Twin (DT) workflow.
Three levels of validation are presented. (i) Theoretical equivalence: UDi ’ s kinematic term reproduces War -
burton ’ s vector criteria while providing a continuous risk scale. (ii) Numerical benchmarks: in controlled wedge- 
formation tests, UDi captured progressive batch failure with the same temporal hierarchy recorded by Bonded- 
Particle DEM and hybrid FDEM, yet required milliseconds rather than hours. (iii) Field proof: 52 
photogrammetry-mapped faces from Chile ’ s El Teniente mine were analysed via a stochastic DFN – KBT engine 
( > 5 300 realizations); the normalized UDi predicted the mean number of unstable blocks with R2 = 0.93 and 95 
% accuracy.
Leveraging its speed, we embed UDi in a Poisson-regression early-warning model that converts each index 
update into an expected block count per advance round, validated via jack-knife resampling. Fracture- 
propagation-informed refinement extends the method to blocks still in formation, enabling continuous hazard 
maps as excavation proceeds.
UDi therefore bridges the gap between deterministic kinematic checks and high-fidelity simulations, deliv -
ering DT-ready, probabilistic, and progressive failure forecasts that unlock proactive support design and truly 
adaptive tunnel operations.

1. Introduction
Underground tunneling is a fundamental yet highly expensive and 
delay-prone activity in modern mining and civil engineering projects. 
Large-scale operations such as the El Teniente mine in Chile, which 
spans ≈ 3,000 km of tunnels, exemplify the logistical and economic 
challenges involved. Studies have shown that the costs associated with 
underground excavation and tunnel failures can represent between 10 % 
and 30 % of the total production costs in major projects ( Avendo Castillo 
et al., 2008 ). Moreover, underground construction projects are more 
prone to delays and cost overruns compared with surface construction . 
A significant source of risk and cost in tunneling arises from structurally 
controlled failures, where rigid rock blocks detach along pre-existing 
discontinuities. Characterizing these discontinuities accurately is 
particularly challenging owing to the inherent danger and time con -
straints of geotechnical surveys in active headings ( Hazrathosseini & 
Mahdevari, 2019; Lu et al., 2022; Hekmatnejad et al., 2021a, Hekmat -
nejad et al., 2021b ). In addition, the presence of epistemic uncertainties 
in fracture geometry significantly affects the reliability of stability pre -
dictions ( Spross et al., 2018 ; Spross et al., 2020 ). This situation un -
derscores the need for robust, quantitative, and rapid tools that can 
dynamically evaluate instability potential as new geological data are 
collected.
Current approaches to rock-mass stability assessment encompass 
direct mechanical testing on core or large in-situ samples, empirical 
classification systems, and numerical modeling techniques. While direct 
testing provides accurate results, it is impractical for continuous 
assessment during tunnel advance ( Li et al., 2014 ). Empirical systems 
such as the Rock Mass Rating (RMR) and the Q-system ( Bieniawski, 
1978; Barton, 2002 ) are favored for their simplicity and speed, yet they 
remain qualitative, heavily operator-dependent, and predominantly 
based on empirical correlations rather than mechanistic principles. 
Analytical and numerical models, including Key Block Theory (KBT) 
( Warburton, 1981; Goodman & Shi, 1985 ), Discontinuous Deformation 
Analysis (DDA), the Discrete Element Method (DEM), the Finite Element 
Method (FEM), the Finite Difference Method (FDM), the Boundary 
Element Method (BEM), and hybrid Finite-Discrete Element Method 
(FDEM) ( Deb & Das, 2010; Yang et al., 2018; Kang et al., 2022a ), have 
become increasingly sophisticated and capable of simulating complex 
geomechanical behaviors. However, these methods often require expert- 
driven parameterization, complex geometric modeling, and significant 
computational resources, which limits their practicality for real-time 
application.
Recent advances in artificial-intelligence (AI) – assisted rock-slope 
analysis further highlight the move toward automated, data-driven 
workflows. For example, Zhu et al. (2022) proposed a deep-learning 
framework that classifies key, trapped, and stable blocks directly from 
slope imagery, achieving ≈ 95 % accuracy. Similarly, Azarafza et al. 
(2020a) introduced a fuzzy-logic decision-making algorithm that 
rapidly assigns a “ degree of reliability ” to wedge and planar blocks 
without exhaustive parameter input. These developments demonstrate 
that AI techniques can supplement traditional KBT by delivering near- 
instantaneous classifications and probabilistic scores.
Key Block Theory remains widely used because of its geometric 
simplicity and low computational cost ( Kulatilake et al., 2011 ). Never -
theless, KBT ’ s deterministic nature limits its capacity to model pro -
gressive or batch failures, manage large fracture datasets, or integrate 
efficiently with automated data pipelines ( Cai et al., 2022 ). A compre -
hensive review of limit-equilibrium formulations for discontinuous 
slopes underscores these shortcomings and calls for hybrid approaches 
that couple KBT with probabilistic inference and numerical verification 
( Azarafza et al., 2017; Azarafza et al., 2021 ). Beyond classic wedge and 
planar kinematics, engineers must also contend with complex toppling 
scenarios. Azarafza et al. (2020b) developed a Python-based algorithm 
that unifies block-flexural-toppling mechanics within a semi-distinct- 
element framework, offering higher processing speed than full DEM 
while preserving block-level fidelity. Most recently, Mao et al. (2025)
presented the BTSTSA method, an extended block-theory formulation 
that yields conservative factors of safety for secondary toppling and has 
been validated against the Aydan-Kawamoto benchmark.
The emergence of Digital-Twin (DT) technologies — dynamic, bidi -
rectional digital replicas of physical assets — presents an opportunity to 
enhance geomechanical risk assessment. DTs have already transformed 
manufacturing ( Rosen et al., 2015 ) and are increasingly applied in en -
ergy infrastructure ( Wang et al., 2021 ). In tunneling, prototype DT 
frameworks incorporate photogrammetry, LiDAR, and microseismic 
data to enhance spatial awareness and monitoring ( Naderi and Shojaei 
2023; Nini ´c et al., 2024 ). However, a critical gap remains: the lack of a 
risk-analysis engine capable of integrating diverse data streams, quan -
tifying damage probabilistically, and updating swiftly enough to guide 
operational decisions ( Tuhaise et al., 2023 ).
To address this gap, this study introduces the Universal Discontinuity 
Index (UDi), a fully quantitative, probabilistic index that consolidates 
four physically grounded components — Physical Damage (PD), Effective 
Stress Intensity Factor (ESIF), the ratio of Active to Total Fractures 
(NAF/NTF) and a Kinematic Factor (Kine) — into a single instability 
measure. This composite index provides a scalable, real-time and 
computationally efficient assessment of rock-mass instability, tailored 
for integration within DT workflows and aimed at reducing the subjec -
tivity inherent in empirical ratings ( Hekmatnejad et al., 2022; Hek -
matnejad et al., 2025 ).

2.1. Physical damage (PD)
PD accounts for the inherent structural degradation within the rock 
mass and is computed as the product of volumetric fracture intensity 
(P32), fracture size factor, and orientation entropy. The P32 parameter, 
which represents the cumulative area of fractures per unit rock volume, 
is central to PD and can be estimated reliably through linear surveys 
regardless of the surface morphology ( Hekmatnejad et al., 2017 ). The 
value of P32 aligns with the concept of fracture energy and can be 
correlated with damage accumulation and seismicity trends during 
multi-event failure processes. Additionally, PD captures the scale- 
independent geometric likelihood of block formation, thus enabling a 
physically grounded damage representation that evolves dynamically 
with new fracture data ( Hekmatnejad et al., 2021b ).

and the surrounding stress field. It is derived from the reduction in stress

These components reflect the differences between the applied and

flaw is more aligned with failure under the prevailing stress conditions, 
thereby contributing more significantly to material degradation. The 
accurate calculation of ESIF requires knowledge of the stress regime and 
flaw characteristics, including orientation and fill material. Studies have 
shown that ESIF correlates strongly with the reduction in strength due to 
crack propagation mechanisms ( Miao et al., 2018 ; Lemon, 2016, 2021).
2.3. Active fracture ratio (AFR)
The AFR component captures the proportion of fractures that are 
actively failing under the current stress conditions. The activation is 
assessed via classical failure criteria such as Mohr-Coulomb for shear 
(Mode II) and Griffith for tensile failure (Mode I), or via a critical stress 
intensity approach for mixed-mode failure.
This term facilitates a transition-based damage classification: when 
AFR = 0, the rock mass is in the elastic stage; when AFR > 0, it pro -
gresses to stable or unstable crack growth. This indicator allows differ -
entiation between pre-failure and coalescence stages and has been 
validated through experimental observations showing that the ratio of 
Mode II to Mode I activity increases as failure approaches (Mahabadi, 
2012; Miao, et al., 2023 ).
2.4. Kinematic factor (Kine)
Kine evaluates the likelihood of post-peak block movement and 
quantifies the contribution of individual or intersecting fractures to ki -
nematic instability. It reflects the ability of blocks formed by intersecting 
discontinuities to mobilize under gravity or excavation-induced 
unloading. This factor is derived from a vector-based assessment 
consistent with Warburton ’ s criteria, allowing continuous quantification 
of the release tendency of fractured blocks. UDi ’ s kinematic term en -
ables a probabilistic interpretation of post-peak behavior and differen -
tiates between block formation and actual failure, which depends on 
directional feasibility. Experimental and numerical validation confirms 
that this term effectively distinguishes between stable and unstable 
block configurations across both lab and tunnel-scale scenarios.
The novelty of UDi lies in its complete system perspective and deep 
model integration, both conceptually aligned with philosophies from 
enterprise security system design ( Cava, 2013; Hekmatnejad et al., 
2025 ). Just as integrated security platforms unify subsystems — alarms, 
access controls, and surveillance — into a scalable and interoperable 
whole, UDi treats the rock mass as a dynamic system whose structural 
response depends on the configuration, relevance, and interaction of 
flaws under varying stress regimes. This system-level modeling 
approach enables UDi to adjust for the contextual importance of dis -
continuities, recognizing that features critical to stability in one engi -
neering application (e.g., tunneling) may be negligible in another (e.g., 
slope design or fluid flow). Importantly, UDi supports cross-domain 
interoperability by offering a unified, semantically meaningful damage 
metric interpretable across disciplines, thereby facilitating more 
coherent communication and decision-making throughout the various 
engineering units. In its construction, UDi integrates a topological model 
of the discontinuity network, vector-based kinematic analysis using 
Warburton ’ s criteria, Effective stress intensity factor, and the Mohr- 
Coulomb, Griffith theory framework for assessing shear failure and 
tension failure correspondingly.
The reliability and practical relevance of the Universal Discontinuity 
Index (UDi) have now been confirmed across every scale of interest to 
rock-engineering practice. Laboratory tests on gypsum and sandstone 
show that UDi tracks the combined influence of flaw orientation, spec -
imen size, in-fill material, confinement pressure and post-peak block

3. Methodology
As illustrated in Fig. 1 , the mere formation of blocks does not 
inherently imply instability; rather, it is the kinematic compatibility 
among fracture sets that governs the likelihood of block failure. In this 
study we choose the Design-objective tuning strategy. Because UDi is 
used in different engineering contexts, the weights reflect the purpose of 
the analysis. For example, to isolate kinematic feasibility in a key-block 
study, the weightings set w1 = 0, w2 = 0, w3 = 1. This approach is 
implemented in the present study.
This configuration enables a targeted probabilistic evaluation of how 
the geometric and kinematic arrangement of intersecting fractures 
contributes to block formation and potential instability, as demonstrated 
throughout the study.

all relevant components — physical damage, fracture activity, and kine -

4.1B) Bonded Particle Discrete Element Method.
The Bonded Particle Discrete Element Method (BPDEM) is a 
powerful numerical technique used to model the mechanical behavior of 
geomaterials. In this approach, the material is idealized as a collection of 
discrete particles interconnected by bonds that replicate the strength 
and deformability of a continuous solid. These interparticle bonds are 
capable of transmitting both tensile and shear forces, enabling the 
simulation of fracture initiation, crack propagation, and complex failure 
patterns. BPDEM is particularly well-suited for analyzing jointed rock 
masses and capturing progressive failure processes with high spatial and 
temporal resolution. Its versatility has made it a widely adopted tool in 
geomechanics, rock engineering, and tunneling applications, where it is 
used to investigate the stability of underground excavations and eval -
uate potential failure mechanisms under various loading scenarios (e.g., 
Zhang and Wong 2013; Chen et al., 2022; Rasmussen & Min, 2023 ).
4.1.C) Finite Discrete Element Method.
The Finite-Discrete Element Method (FDEM) is an advanced nu -
merical approach that combines the strengths of the Finite Element 
Method (FEM) and the Discrete Element Method (DEM) to model the 
mechanical behavior of fractured and discontinuous materials. In FDEM, 
the material is initially represented as a continuous medium using finite 
elements, allowing for accurate simulation of stress distribution, defor -
mation, and elastic behavior. As loading progresses and failure criteria 
are met, cohesive interactions between elements are broken, enabling 
the transition from continuum behavior to discontinuum mechanics. 
This hybrid framework allows FDEM to capture both the onset of 
damage and the subsequent development of fractures, including frag -
mentation, crack coalescence, and block separation. FDEM is particu -
larly effective in simulating dynamic fracture processes and complex 
failure mechanisms in rock masses, making it a valuable tool in rock 
mechanics, slope stability analysis, and underground excavation 
modeling ( Kang et al., 2022b; Yan et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Deng 
and Liu, 2025 ; Wang et al., 2025 ).

4.3. Probabilistic batch progressive block movement analysis
Structurally controlled failure is a complex process influenced by 
spatial variability in geological features and dynamic stress redistribu -
tion during excavation. Rock mass conditions — including discontinuity 
patterns, faults, and intact rock properties — can vary drastically over 
short distances, and the resulting stress field evolves as excavation 
progresses. These factors introduce significant uncertainty into stability 
assessments.
Traditional models, such as Warburton ’ s Key Block Theory (KBT), 
are primarily designed to identify kinematically removable blocks that 
are directly adjacent to the excavation boundary. While effective for 
detecting immediate, single-event instabilities, classical KBT does not 
account for the progressive or batch failure of rock blocks — where the 
initial detachment of key blocks can trigger the subsequent 
destabilization and failure of surrounding blocks. Capturing such 
sequential failure mechanisms requires iterative extensions of KBT, as 
developed in more recent studies ( Zhu et al., 2024 ), which go beyond its 
original deterministic formulation.
The Universal Discontinuity Index (UDi) addresses this limitation by 
providing a probabilistic framework that captures both isolated and 
sequential block instabilities. This capability has been validated through 
theoretical development and numerical modeling. As illustrated in 
Fig. 6 , UDi ’ s performance was tested against KBT, Finite Discrete 
Element Method (FDEM), and Bonded Particle Discrete Element Method 
(BPDEM) in a tunnel scenario involving two intersecting joint sets. The 
comparative results demonstrate UDi ’ s superior ability to represent the 
evolving behavior of rock blocks under progressive failure.
Fig. 7 illustrates a conceptual comparison between KBT and UDi. 
According to KBT, only blocks labeled “ 1 ″ are identified as unstable due 
to their direct exposure. However, this narrow view ignores blocks “ 2 ” 
and “ 3, ” which may become unstable as a consequence of the failure of 
block “ 1, ” highlighting the cascading nature of progressive failure. KBT, 
limited to immediate contact geometry, cannot anticipate such deeper 
instabilities.
UDi overcomes this by incorporating a kinematic factor based on the 
interaction between fracture density (P32) and the circular variance 
relative to the vertical direction (CV.tot-ver). This parameter reflects the 
spatial orientation of fractures with respect to the principal driving 
force, enabling a comprehensive assessment of instability risk. In the 
example shown, with fractures oriented at 45
◦
and 12 traces within the 
zone of interest, the calculated CV.tot-ver is 0.92 — indicating a high 
probability of kinematic failure, especially when combined with a high 
P32 value.
Unlike KBT ’ s deterministic, stepwise approach — where each 
discontinuity is assessed in sequence — UDi treats the fracture network as 
a unified system. This holistic perspective captures emergent behaviors 
and interactions that are essential for understanding failure in complex 
rock masses. Therefore, UDi provides a more realistic, scalable, and 
predictive tool for assessing structural instability, particularly in digital 
twin applications for underground excavations.

5.1. Extracting the fracture sets characteristics
When extracting characteristics from structures mapped in photo -
graphic datasets, several key features are systematically identified. The 
position of each structure is recorded as a georeferenced point in X, Y, 
and Z coordinates, corresponding to the center of a disc-shaped plane 
( Cavieres, 2019 ). The orientation is defined by the dip direction and dip 
angle relative to these axes. The exposed trace length is measured on the 
Digital Terrain Model (DTM), providing a close approximation to the 
true length, even in cases where the structure deviates from a perfectly 
flat plane. With high-resolution and clean DTMs — combined with prior 
geological knowledge of the mine — mineral fillings and lithology can 
also be accurately identified. The thickness of discontinuities is esti -
mated to the nearest centimeter, allowing for precise characterization 
even of fine structures. Size is inferred from the radius of the disc used 
during mapping, while roughness is evaluated based on surface 
morphology, following standardized methodologies. This streamlined 
and systematic approach ensures accurate mapping and detailed char -
acterization of the mine ’ s structural features. As a result, a total of 3,452 
discontinuities were identified across the 52 sections within the study 
area.

• The green zone represents a low probability of rock block failure,

• The yellow zone denotes a moderate risk, suggesting areas that

• The red zone identifies areas of high risk, where the likelihood of 
rock block failure is significant and potentially critical.

perform a hybrid DFN – Key Block analysis, which served as the basis for 
calibrating the supervised Poisson regression model. The model uses UDi

5.7. A closed-loop digital twin framework for predictive rock block 
instability assessment using the universal discontinuity index (UDi)
The predictive framework presented in this study is designed for 
seamless integration within a closed-loop Digital Twin (DT) architec -
ture, enabling real-engineering time geomechanical hazard forecasting 
at the pace of daily — or even per-round — tunnel excavation cycles. This 
DT-enabled early warning system is structured into five interconnected 
layers that form a fully automated, continuously updating data pipeline:
1 Data Acquisition (Edge Layer).
Following each blast round, high-resolution LiDAR or photogram -
metry scans of the newly exposed tunnel face are captured. Simulta -
neously, field tablets collect and transmit additional inputs such as 
drilling parameters, geologist assessments via kiosk systems, and 
microseismic event data. This multimodal data stream ensures a 
comprehensive representation of the evolving excavation front.
2 Automated Feature Extraction (Processing Layer).
The updated 3D point cloud is processed through the R-Dis-Frag 
engine, which reconstructs the surface mesh and automatically extracts 
discontinuity traces. These are sampled using pre-registered virtual 
boreholes embedded within the DT geometry. From this data, the four 
components of the Universal Discontinuity Index (UDi) — Physical 
Damage (PD), Effective Stress Intensity Factor (ESIF), Active Fracture 
Ratio (NAF/NTF), and Kinematic Feasibility (Kine) — are computed in 
near real-time.
3 Risk Analytics (Twin Core Layer).
The face-averaged UDi values are passed to a calibrated Poisson 
regression model (as detailed in Section 5 ), which generates a proba -
bilistic estimate of the number of removable rock blocks expected in the 
subsequent excavation round, including both the mean and a 95 % 
confidence interval. A Bayesian updating filter continuously refines 
model coefficients by assimilating observed block fall outcomes, thus 
enabling self-learning behavior over time.
4 Decision Support and Visualization (Application Layer).
Risk predictions are visualized on an interactive DT dashboard, built 
upon an IFC/BIM digital backbone. Each tunnel face is assigned a traffic 
light classification:
5. Green ( λ < 1): Routine condition
6. Yellow (1 ≤ λ < 3): Caution
7. Red ( λ ≥ 3):
Critical Based on the inferred hazard level ( λ ), the system auto- 
generates targeted support recommendations (e.g., additional pattern 
bolts, mesh reinforcement), which are displayed alongside the 3D mesh 
model for clear, actionable guidance.
8 Action and Feedback (Physical Layer).
The shift supervisor either confirms or modifies the recommended 
support actions. This decision, once executed, is logged and re- 
integrated into the DT pipeline — closing the feedback loop and updat -
ing the UDi computation and model predictions accordingly. If λ exceeds 
a predefined alert threshold, the system automatically dispatches SMS/ 
email notifications to the geotechnical engineering team, providing true 
early-warning capability rather than reactive post-event analysis.

6. Conclusions
This research delivers a significant advancement in underground 
geomechanical risk assessment by establishing the Universal Disconti -
nuity Index (UDi) as a robust, scalable, and real-time framework for 
evaluating probabilistic rock block instability. UDi is providing a 
quantitative, dynamic, and probabilistic alternative to traditional 
deterministic models. The key conclusions drawn from this study are as 
follows: 
Superior Predictive Capability Across Scales: UDi consistently out -
performs conventional approaches, including the Key Block Theory 
(KBT), by capturing both static and progressive failure mechanisms 
in jointed rock masses. In controlled simulation experiments, UDi 
accurately replicated the temporal evolution of instability observed 
in BPDEM and FDEM simulations for both simple and complex 
wedge geometries. Field validation at El Teniente mine further 
confirmed UDi ’ s predictive strength, with the normalized kinematic 
component achieving a high correlation (R2 = 0.93) with the mean 
number of unstable blocks derived from 5,300 DFN – KBT 
realizations.
Probabilistic, Multifactorial, and Scalable Framework: The UDi formu -
lation synthesizes four core, physically grounded components: 
Physical Damage (PD), Effective Stress Intensity Factor (ESIF), Active 
Fracture Ratio (NAF/NTF), and the Kinematic Factor (Kine). This 
integration enables UDi to probabilistically assess damage and 
instability, scalable from laboratory samples to full tunnel and 
cavern complexes. Its computational efficiency — delivering updates 
within milliseconds — makes it suitable for real-time risk assessment 
and decision support during active excavation.

Complete System View and Deep Model Integration: UDi embodies a 
complete system perspective inspired by enterprise system archi -
tectures. It deeply integrates structural, mechanical, and kinematic 
parameters into a unified risk metric. This holistic approach supports 
cross-disciplinary understanding and aligns with modern engineer -
ing integration standards, bridging geology, geomechanics, and mine 
planning. The semantic clarity of UDi facilitates communication 
among stakeholders, promoting interoperability across different 
domains within the mining and tunneling sectors.
Fracture Propagation-Informed Refinement: A key innovation of UDi is 
its ability to account for evolving fracture networks and the transi -
tional geometry of partially formed blocks — features overlooked by 
classical models. This capability enables continuous refinement of 
instability assessments as excavation progresses, supporting adaptive 
hazard mapping and proactive intervention strategies.
Field-Proven Early-Warning System: The Poisson regression model 
integrated with UDi translates the index into a predictive tool 
capable of estimating the number of unstable blocks expected per 
tunnel round. Validated through jackknife resampling, this approach 
demonstrated 95 % prediction confidence, offering practical utility 
for early warning and support design optimization in real tunnel 
operations.
System Integration and Digital Twin Readiness: UDi ’ s architecture is 
inherently compatible with Digital Twin workflows. It functions as a 
semantic integration layer that fuses heterogeneous data sour -
ces — such as photogrammetry, LiDAR, and microseismic monitor -
ing — into a coherent and quantitative risk assessment platform. The 
UDi framework thus operationalizes DT principles, enabling 
continuous model updating, real-time risk visualization, and intel -
ligent decision support in tunneling projects.
Validated Reliability Across Diverse Geological Conditions: UDi has 
demonstrated robust reliability and reproducibility across labora -
tory, numerical, and field studies under varying geological condi -
tions. Applications include tunnel damage rating ( Hekmatnejad 
et al., 2022 ), spatial modeling of rock strength heterogeneity 
( Hekmatnejad et al., 2023 ), structural controls on drill-and-blast 
overbreak (Cort ´eset al, 2025), and probabilistic hazard mapping.
Closed-Loop Geotechnical Data Cycle: The study operationalizes a 
closed-loop data cycle within the DT context: data acquisition 
(photogrammetry, LiDAR) → automated feature extraction (via R- 
Dis-Frag) → UDi computation → probabilistic forecasting (Poisson 
model) → real-time operational feedback (DT dashboard). This loop 
allows for on-the-fly updates to risk assessments and supports 
adaptive excavation management without the need for time- 
consuming remodelling.
Bridging Gaps Between Deterministic, Numerical, and Predictive Models:
UDi effectively bridges the gap between deterministic kinematic 
models, such as Key Block Theory (KBT), high-fidelity numerical 
simulations (BPDEM and FDEM), and real-time predictive analytics. 
While KBT provides only a static and geometry-based assessment, 
and numerical methods, though accurate, are computationally 
intensive and time-consuming, UDi offers a computationally efficient 
alternative. It captures progressive failure mechanisms with predic -
tive accuracy comparable to numerical models while enabling rapid, 
scalable, and probabilistic risk assessments suitable for real-time 
decision-making in tunnel operations.
UDi ’ s comprehensive, system-integrated, and predictive approach 
enhances our capacity to understand, anticipate, and mitigate struc -
turally controlled failures in complex geological settings. UDi ’ s scal -
ability, adaptability, and computational efficiency make it a cornerstone 
for safer, smarter, and more resilient underground infrastructure 
development in both mining and civil engineering applications.
