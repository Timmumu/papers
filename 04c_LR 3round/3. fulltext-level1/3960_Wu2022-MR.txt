ABSTRACT  
Spatial locations of personnel, equipment, and materials are constantly changing as construction projects 
progress. The dynamic nature of the construction industry affects workers ’ performance of identifying hazards. 
Even though a great deal of effort has been made to improve construction safety, the construction industry still 
witnesses a high accident rate. In order to complement the existing body of knowledge relating to construction 
safety, this paper integrates Digital Twin (DT), Deep Learning (DL), and Mixed Reality (MR) technologies into a 
newly developed real-time visual warning system, which enables construction workers to proactively determine 
their safety status and avoid accidents. Next, system tests were conducted under three quasi-on-site scenarios, 
and the feasibility was proven in terms of synchronising construction activities over a large area and visually 
representing hazard information to its users. These evidenced merits of the development testing scenarios can 
improve workers ’ risk assessment accuracy, reinforce workers ’ safety behaviour, and provide a new perspective 
for construction safety managers to analyse construction safety status.   
1. Introduction 
Construction safety has been considered a formidable challenge by 
the construction industry for a long time. According to the China state 
administration of work safety, the death toll in the first half of 2018 was 
1752 in the China construction industry [ 1 ]. In the US, there were 1008 
deaths in the construction industry in 2018, accounting for one-fifth of 
the recorded death toll of all industries [ 2 ]. As of now, the construction 
industry casualty rate still remains high. 
A risk can be avoided if construction workers can promptly perceive 
the risk and take the correct measure. While behavioural approaches can 
play a part, cognitive aspects have also been an alternative approach to 
improve safety. There is a cognitive process from seeing a hazard to 
taking an appropriate measure. It will affect workers ’ assessment of the 
risk and then affect their behaviour. While the state-of-the-art extended 
reality research has led to pleasing outcomes in terms of technological 
advancement, it somewhat overlooks the profound human cognitive 
processes which may neutralise the efficacy of advanced visualisation 
applications. Studies have also shown that around 50% of the risks on 
construction sites are not identified promptly [ 3 – 6 ], and human factors, 
including attention, bias, risk tolerance, physical state, and mental state, 
can influence the visual search process used by workers to determine 
safety risks [ 7 ]. Dynamic and everchanging construction environment 
can also introduce new risks to the site. While workforce training is 
being used as a proactive risk mitigation measure, there are still more 
than 25% risks that occur due to workplace negligence, oversight or lack 
of awareness [ 8 – 12 ]. 
Safety management is an effective approach to enhance construction 
safety. The present-day management practice has been well enriched by 
evolving state-of-the-art information technologies [ 13 ]. For instance, an 
integration of Geographic Information System (GIS) and Building In -
formation Modelling (BIM) systems can provide critical clues about 
hazard location, state and precaution [ 14 – 17 ]. Presenting these clues to 
onsite workers promptly can also play a part and this study aims to 
improve the way this information is presented. Conventionally, if 
someone is working and alerted of a hazard, he might have to deal with 
multiple tasks at the same time: continuing his task and attempting to 
decipher the alert. It is according to the multiple resource theory that 
conflicts are bound to arise when two or more tasks are performed 
simultaneously [ 18 ], therefore, construction workers may experience 
cognitive difficulties in this circumstance. Using 3-Dimensional (3D) 
models as information carrier is likely to be a remedy. Mixed Reality

This paper proposes a visual warning system, which combines safety 
management scenarios and MR to deliver and visualise hazard infor -
mation to onsite workers. The proposed system enables workers to 
literally see the surrounding hazardous area and the corresponding 
warning information in real-time using wearable MR devices. It enables

(DT) refers to a digital replica of a physical entity [ 19 , 20 ]. The ideal DT 
contains all the information about the physical entity and is able to 
synchronise any changes in real-time. Nowadays, the DT concept is 
gaining popularity in various application areas [ 21 – 25 ]. In our study, 
the proposed system incorporates a DT function which can simulate 
construction site resources and logistics. The proposed system comprises 
three modules: a real-time tracking module, a hazard identification 
module, and a visualisation module. The real-time tracking module 
utilises Deep Learning (DL) techniques to track the real-time location 
and movement trends of hazardous objects through live streaming 
videos taken from Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV). The hazard iden -

3D model, workers can quickly determine the source of hazards and stay 
away from dangerous areas that pose a threat to them. The modules 
communicate with each other via sockets based on the Transmission 
Control Protocol (TCP).

summarises techniques for hazard identification and hazard information 
presentation. Section 3 proposes the details of the designed visual

through preliminary experiments. Section 5 presents the results of pre -
liminary experiments and evaluates the feasibility of the proposed sys -
tem. Finally, the paper is concluded in Section 6 . 
2. Literature review 
2.1. Hazard identification 
A prerequisite of hazard information presentation is identifying 
hazards correctly, which requires tracking and analysing workers and 
objects that may cause accidents. Two categories of object tracking 
techniques have achieved great success in hazard identification, namely, 
radio-based tracking and vision-based tracking. 
2.1.1. Radio-based tracking 
Most commonly used tracking techniques, including Global Posi -
tioning System (GPS), Wireless Local Area Network (WLAN), Radio 
Frequency Identification (RFID), Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), and 
Ultra-Wide Band (UWB), are radio-based [ 26 , 27 ]. They require the 
installation of signal transceiver modules on objects [ 14 , 28 – 32 ]. These 
techniques have already been widely used to track on-site objects, such 
as construction workers [ 33 ] and mobile heavy construction equipment 
[ 34 ]. Applying these techniques is helpful to achieve automation in the 
field of construction safety management [ 34 , 35 ]. Based on trilateration 
principles, triangulation principles, and scene analysis (also known as 
fingerprinting), the positions of objects can be determined. 
For identifying hazards on construction sites, there are some issues 
should be considered. Li et al. [ 35 ] proposed that semi-indoor envi -
ronment, complex environment, and dynamic environment are three 
important characteristics of construction sites that affect object tracking. 
They revealed that obstacles could cause the attenuation and shadowing 
of signals, diffraction and scattering of signals, and multipath propaga -
tion, which can affect the effectiveness of the radio-based tracking to 
some extent. The obstacles include construction materials, heavy 
equipment, vehicles, and workers. Their impact on object tracking is 
unavoidable since they move around constantly during construction, but 
the impact can be reduced through technical means. Different features of 
radio-based tracking techniques result in that they are applied in 
different situations. GPS is commonly applied for outdoor positioning 
since there are few overhead obstructions that can block the signal 
transmission between transceivers and satellites in an open area [ 36 ]. 
According to the research results from Pradhananga and Teizer [ 37 ], the 
average error of GPS devices can reach 1.1 m in an open area, and the 
performance of GPS degrades with increasing obstacles. As for indoor 
positioning, WLAN, RFID, BLE, and UWB are commonly used because 
their base stations (three or more fixed signal transceiver modules with 
known positions) can be set on construction sites. Park et al. [ 38 ] pro -
posed a probabilistic local search algorithm for BLE. The algorithm is 
designed to find unreliable signals by calculating probabilities and then 
to estimate the positions of objects being tracked after giving the un -
reliable signals lower significances. Chan et al. [ 39 ] developed a transfer 
learning method to improve the performance of Wi-Fi fingerprinting by 
integrating Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Long Short-Term 
Memory (LSTM). The method can predict the positions of unlabelled 
fingerprints, which is helpful to reduce the accuracy drop resulted from 
the dynamic environment of construction sites. Table 1 summarises the 
accuracy and response time of the indoor positioning techniques.

RFID was integrated into the Internet of Things (IoT) to analyse whether 
a worker is entering a construction area without permission. Radio- 
based tracking can also track dynamic hazardous areas. Based on the

2.1.2. Vision-based tracking 
Vision-based tracking refers to following uniquely identified regions 
of interest in a sequence of images. It can track multiple on-site targets 
through a single camera. The development of computer vision (CV) 
turns vision-based tracking into a popular research topic [55]. Since the 
present-day DL algorithms such as CNN, Region-CNN (RCNN), Fast 
RCNN, and You Only Look Once (YOLO) are constantly evolving, vision- 
based tracking is becoming more accurate and robust [56–59]. Using 
models trained by DL algorithms, a computer can track on-site objects 
according to their features. DL has a robust capability to represent the 
complex relationship between input data (e.g., images) and output 
reasoning (e.g., object recognition). This advantage has led to reliable 
vision-based applications to identify and track construction workers and 
sources of danger [60–62]. For example, Park et al. [56], Fang et al. 
[57], and Fang et al. [58] used SVM, CNN and R-CNN algorithms, 
respectively, to estimate whether construction workers correctly use 
personal protective equipment. Fang et al. [63] developed a DL-based 
framework that can efficiently identify if workers are certified on con -
struction sites by matching their face information and documented 
identities. Ding et al. [64] integrated LSTM and CNN to extract the 
spatial and temporal information of construction videos. The outputs 
can predict whether workers would climb scaffolding unsafely. 
The abovementioned methods aim to track objects and identify 
hazards in 2D images. Such methods ignore the spatial context in the 3D 
environment and only cover limited hazard types. In order to address 
this issue, 3D image processing methods are introduced to the field of 
construction monitoring. Unlike 2D image processing, 3D image pro -
cessing contains an additional depth map to record the distance from a 
camera to an object. The depth map can let a computer understand the 
spatial relationship between tracked objects, but getting a depth map 
requires extra technologies, such as structured light and time of flight 
[65–67], and these requirements make 3D image processing more 
difficult to achieve hazard identification on dynamic and complex 
construction sites. To this end, some researchers are trying to analyse 
spatial relationships between tracked objects based on 2D images. Re -
searchers proposed two methods to obtain spatial positions. One is to 
form a stereoscopic vision by setting multiple cameras. It is similar to 
human vision in principle. If two cameras capture an object simulta -
neously, its spatial position can be calculated based on the camera pose 
(e.g., location and orientation). For example, Soltani et al. [68] proposed 
a monitoring approach to identify excavator poses using multiple cam -
eras. Chi and Caldas [69] developed a safety monitoring system to 
identify spatial safety risks based on a stereoscopic vision. The system 
can detect vehicle type, pose, and velocity to evaluate whether safety 
rules are adhered to. Such post-processing requires considerable human 
input for feature extraction and computation power for mathematical 
calculation. Another method is to create mapping functions matching 
each 2D point in an image with the corresponding 3D point in the real 
world. Son et al. [70] proposed a function to achieve the mapping. Ac -
cording to the projection principle, the function matches the 2D co -
ordinates on an image plane to the 2D coordinates on a horizontal object 
reference plane. The object reference plan is the plane in which the 
tracked objects are located, and each 2D point on the object reference 
plane refers to a 3D point in the real world. The mapping method was 
designed to provide proximity alarms to prevent collisions between 
workers and construction equipment in their research. It is a specific 
application scenario, which makes the mapping method lacks robustness 
to be used to improve workforce safety in more construction scenarios. 
On the complex construction site, a camera may capture several surfaces 
at different levels in an image, such as a roof surface and a ground 
surface. Therefore, the spatial relationship between the surfaces needs to 
be considered for tracking objects and identifying hazards. 
2.2. Presenting hazard information 
Presenting hazard information is significant for improving con -
struction workforce safety. If workers are not given enough hazard in -
formation about their surroundings, they can hardly make appropriate 
decisions, which is risky. According to the human information pro -
cessing model proposed by Wickens et al. [71], a worker needs to notice 
an accident precursor, identify it, retrieve available response options 
from their long-term memory, and select an appropriate response to 
execute. Then an accident can be avoided [72,73]. However, workers 
may underestimate risks or be unaware of hazards while working. Pre -
senting information about surrounding hazards to workers is a feasible 
way to help them reassess the risk and keep vigilant. A traditional 
approach is placing warning signs in the areas where accidents may 
occur. The Australian Code [74] recommends setting clear warning 
signs, such as “Beware Explosive”, “High Voltage”, and “Danger Falling 
Objects” on construction sites. The approach gets workers to notice the 
possible hazards around them in advance so that they can take proactive 
actions to avoid injuries. Another traditional approach is using acous -
tical warnings, for example, moving forklifts can sound the horn at an 
intersection to alert people. 
With the development of automation in construction monitoring, 
researchers developed several automatic approaches to present hazard 
information and to assist on-site workers in making optimal decisions. 
These automatic approaches integrate temporal and spatial information 
to present dynamic hazard information to on-site workers in real-time. 
Some researchers combined audio warning and automatic monitoring. 
They attach speakers to signal transceiver modules to warn workers in 
danger [53,75,76]. Other researchers applied tactile stimuli to warn 
workers [77,78]. Research revealed that the response time of human 
bodies to tactile stimuli is shorter than audio and visual ones [79,80]. 
Sakhakarmi et al. [81] developed a tactile-based wearable system to 
prevent collisions between workers and equipment. The system has ten 
vibration motors to convey different information, including the type of 
heavy equipment and the real-time location. Based on this, workers can 
make better decisions to ensure their safety. Besides, the application of 
advanced visualisation technology allows texts, images, and videos to be 
presented to on-site individuals dynamically [82,83]. Kim et al. [84] 
developed an innovative construction hazard avoidance system on 
Augmented Reality (AR) goggles. The system provides workers with 
hazard information, including a current safety level, the distance of the 
closest vehicle, and the relative location between the user and the closest 
vehicle. The utilise of AR allows workers to obtain hazard information 
intuitively [85–87]. Workers can have preliminary understandings 
about surrounding hazards to take proactive actions to avoid accidents. 
Their system makes effective use of the focal vision channel. However, 
considering construction workers’ daily tasks may occupy their focal 
vision channel, conflicts may occur [88,89]. Therefore, using ambient 
vision to provide warnings might be a great option while construction 
workers are working [71].

The reviewed research indicates that many studies on the topic of 
construction safety heavily rely on radio-based or vision-based tech -
nologies. However, radio-based tracking methods require the installa -
tion of signal transceivers on the objects to be tracked. Equipping 
multiple signal transceiver modules on each construction site is

unrealistic, given it is a tedious and costly practice. Therefore, this type 
of method is not the scope of our study. Regarding vision-based 
methods, they are hard to define safety risks in different scenarios. To 
be specific, some construction resources or workers ’ behaviours are 
considered dangerous in specific spatial contexts. For example, using a 
welding gun near acetylene or oxygen cylinders is riskier than in other 
places since it can cause an explosion. Using safety harnesses is 
mandatory when working at height, but it is unnecessary when working 
on the ground. Although many researchers have done excellent works to 
achieve the representation of complex construction safety knowledge 
through ontology modelling [ 90 – 93 ], the integration of ontology and CV 
for hazard identification in 3D spaces has not been fully achieved yet 
[ 94 ]. The real-time reconstruction of dynamic 3D construction sites 
through CV alone is still a difficult task at this stage, which challenges 
workers to identify spatial-related hazards intuitively. To this end, this 
study integrates DL and DT, which can determine the spatial location of 
tracked objects based on the geometric information of the BIM. This 
integration can not only provide spatial context information but also 
obtain the spatial relationship between objects. By inputting and 
updating BIM models, hazards can be found in the corresponding 
environment, increasing the robustness of hazard identification. 
Additionally, many hazard warning methods have been developed 
through audio and tactile ways. But this type of warning simply aims to 
make workers aware of danger without providing additional safety in -
formation. It is also difficult for workers to quickly determine their 
safety status. To this end, some studies provide workers with additional 
safety information to help workers make decisions and proactively avoid 
hazards [ 84 ]. Research has shown that AR and MR techniques merging 
virtual objects with the real world can significantly improve workers ’ 
safety awareness [ 95 , 96 ]. However, hazards are mainly shown in 
workers ’ focal view in these studies. The displays of hazards do not 
conform to the human habit of identifying hazards. Because the con -
struction tasks have already taken up workers ’ focal vision. Delivering 
safety information in their focal view can reduce their working perfor -
mance. To the best of the authors ’ knowledge, there is a lack of a spatial 
context-aware method to provide real-time safety information for on- 
site workers in their ambient vision. The integration of DT and MR 
can deal with the issue and provide visual warnings to on-site workers 
through both focal and ambient vision channels. The purpose of this 
study is to bridge the research gap mentioned above by integrating 
multiple advanced technologies. To achieve this purpose, two objectives 
are pursued: a) integrating DL and DT to increase the robustness of 
hazard identification, and b) integrating DT and MR to generate and 
provide real-time safety information. 
3. Methodology 
The proposed visual warning system focuses on proximity alerting 
and it consists of three modules. These modules are responsible for real- 
time tracking, hazard identification, and visualisation, respectively, as 
shown in Fig. 1 . The real-time camera tracking module can do object 
tracking and calibrate the coordinates. In the hazard identification 
module, information from 4-dimensional (4D) BIM models is used to 
create a VCS. By inputting the information obtained from the real-time 
tracking module into the hazard identification module, the objects can 
be reconstructed as DTs in the VCS. Then, according to safety regula -
tions, hazard information can be generated, including hazard sources, 
hazard types, and hazardous areas. The visualisation module can present 
the hazard information to a worker through an MR goggle when he 
enters a hazardous area. These modules communicate with each other 
through TCP sockets. In order to reduce the impact of network latencies, 
each module has its own processing units to ensure that the transmitted 
data is as simple as possible. 
3.1. Real-time tracking module 
The real-time tracking module combines the YOLO version 4 Tiny 
(YOLOv4-Tiny) algorithm [ 97 ] with Deep SORT [ 98 ] to detect and track 
safety-related objects in live streaming acquired from a CCTV camera on 
a construction site. 
YOLOv4 ‑ tiny is a lightweight version of the 4th generation of YOLO 
that requires less computational resources and memory and has high 
throughput. This characteristic makes it suitable for processing live 
streaming. YOLOv4 ‑ tiny consists of three main modules, which are 
CSPDarknet53-Tiny, Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) and Yolo Head. 
CSPDarknet53-Tiny is developed for feature extraction of input images. 
It comprises Convolutional layers (Conv) and CSPBlock. The Leaky ReLU 
(The Rectified Linear Unit) function is employed as the activation

3.2. TCP communication 
Since the proposed system may include multiple real-time tracking 
modules and visualisation modules, it was designed based on a server- 
client architecture. The hazard identification module is the server, and 
the other modules are the clients. Sockets for TCP communication link 
the server with the clients. The server assigns address information such 
as IP address and port number to its socket. Clients can connect their 
socket to the server socket by accessing the server address. Then, the 
information can be transmitted between the server and the clients 
through TCP communication after its carriers are encoded into byte 
arrays. In order to reduce the impact of network latencies, the infor -
mation carriers are numerical data and texts rather than images. As 
shown in Fig. 3 , data source, time, object number, object type, x-coor -
dinate, and y-coordinate are encoded in sequence when information is 
transmitted from a real-time tracking module to a hazard identification

3.3. Hazard identification module 
The function of the hazard identification module is to integrate and 
analyse information obtained from different sources to identify hazards. 
A VCS was created in the module to simulate construction activities 
based on real-time information. It was developed using the Unity game 
engine since the engine has good extensibility and rich community re -
sources [ 104 , 105 ]. A VCS can obtain 3D models of a construction project 
from 4D BIM files by imputing OBJ format files manually or using 
synchronisation plug-ins, such as Pixyz and Unity Reflect. The schedules 
in the 4D BIM can be used to set the appearance time of building com -
ponents. As the server of the proposed visual warning system, the hazard 
identification module gathers information from all real-time tracking 
modules. Each area monitored by a real-time tracking module is linked 
to an area in a VCS. When information of a tracked object is transmitted 
to the hazard identification module, a DL of the object is generated in a 
VCS through a mapping approach.

Son et al. [ 70 ]. A 2D image taken by a camera is a projection of a 3D 
space onto a plane. Based on the camera pose and the object coordinates 
in the image, the object position in the real world can be determined. A 
coordinate system and several formulae were designed to achieve the 
mapping.

The distance between a tracked object and a system user is the key to 
estimating risks. In the system, red, yellow, and blue zones are designed 
to indicate different risk levels of hazardous areas. Each hazardous area 
is associated with an object that may cause an accident. The red zone is 
where the accident may occur at any time; the yellow zone is the area 
that may become a red zone in the next few seconds, and the blue zone is 
the area that may become a yellow zone in the next few seconds. For 
example, the area adjacent to a truck within 3 m is the red zone, as 
shown in Fig. 5 , since a truck may suddenly overturn, move forward or 
move backward. Workers in this area can be injured at any time. The 
sectors in the front and rear of a truck are yellow and blue zones. Since 
the speed of a large vehicle on the construction site must not exceed 5 
km/h, the yellow zone is within 10 m, and the blue zone is within 17 m 
when assuming workers needs 5 s to get out of the hazardous areas. If a 
system user enters a hazardous area, the hazard identification module 
determines that the user ’ s safety is being threatened. Then the corre -
sponding hazard information will be generated and sent to the visual -
isation module through TCP immediately. It is worth noting that the 
latency and the speed of tracked objects can affect the identification of 
dangerous areas. Thus, a script was created to automatically move the 
generated hazardous areas forward in the direction of the object ’ s 
movement according to the speed and latency.

Three scenarios were designed to validate the proposed system. The 
hazardous situations in the scenarios include explosion, falling from 
heights, and struck-by accident. These hazardous situations involve 
tracking multiple objects in different complex 3D environments, which 
has requirements for robustness. The proposed system can track objects 
with no signal transceivers attached to them and collect context-aware 
information from BIM to generate hazard alerts related to workers ’ 
surrounding physical environment. These features make the proposed 
method more robust and accurate than conventional tracking and 
warning methods. The details of the tests are shown as follow: 
4.1. Scenario 1: Explosion 
Scenario 1 was designed to simulate two workers performing mate -
rial moving tasks on a construction site. It aims to test the feasibility and 
accuracy of object tracking and displaying functions. A worker (the first 
participant) is responsible for moving an acetylene cylinder, and the 
other worker (the second participant) moves an oxygen cylinder. The 
safety regulation says the minimum distance between acetylene and 
oxygen cylinders is 5 m. Therefore, a red zone is within 5 m of the 2nd 
participant, and the acceptable error is 41.35 cm. Since the average 
human walking speed is 1.2 m per second, the yellow zone is within 11 
m, and the blue zone is within 17 m. Based on the scenario, effective 
ranges, tracking accuracy, and transmission latencies of the proposed 
system were tested and recorded at several positions (point 1 – 9 in 
Fig. 11 ), considering the deviation of coordinates may occur on both the 
x and y axes. 
The experiment involves three steps. In step 1, the 2nd participant 
stands at the aligning point facing the camera. The 1st participant 
observing the 2nd participant uses the visual warning system to adjust 
his registration until the virtual 3D model of the 2nd participant and the 
real one is overlapped. Then, the 2nd participant stands at point 1 facing 
the camera. In step 2, the 1st participant marks the 3D model ’ s position 
on the ground and records the network latency shown by the developed 
applications. The 2nd participant marks his own position on the ground. 
After that, the 2nd participant moves to the next point. In step 3, step 2 is 
repeated until the distance between the 3D model and the 2nd partici -
pant exceeds 41.35 cm, or the 2nd participant is out of the camera ’ s 
view. The test was repeated for additional three times.

5. Results and discussion 
In scenario 1, an error of more than 41.35 cm occurred at point 9 (the 
point marked in blue in Fig. 14 ). Therefore, the effective range of the 
real-time identification module is within 8.49 m from the aligning point, 
and the effective area is more than 72 m
2 
(area marked in yellow in 
Fig. 14 ) in a conservative calculation. Within this area, the IoU is greater 
than 90%. Using a local area network created by a mobile phone hot -
spot, the average latency of data transmission was 289.508 ms, and the 
maximum latency was 424 ms. 
Two main functions should be evaluated in the proposed system. The 
first function is object detection, and the other one is information 
demonstration. When objects are correctly detected, it is called true- 
positive. If there is no object and the object detection function 
correctly identifies the situation, it is true-negative. The real-time 
tracking module may make errors in detecting objects. It may identify 
other objects as target objects (false-positive) or fail to identify the target 
objects (false-negative). There are five indicators to evaluate the pro -
posed object detection function. 
Accuracy = ( TP + TN ) / ( TP + TN + FP + FN ) (5)  
True Positive Rate ( TPR ) = ( TP ) / ( TP + FN ) (6)  
Positive Predictive Value ( PPV ) = ( TP ) / ( TP + FP ) (7)  
False Negative Rate ( FNR ) = ( FN ) / ( TP + FN ) (8)  
False Discovery Rate ( FDR ) = ( FP ) / ( TP + FP ) (9)  
where TP = True-Positive, FN = False-Negative, FP = False-Positive, and 
TN = True-Negative. 
The result is shown in Table 5 . The object detection accuracy is more 
than 96.5%, which is good enough to provide hazard information to on- 
site workers. In the view of system users, it has been observed that the 
false-negative cases have little effect on the visualisation, but false- 
positive cases are more significant. Due to the nature of object 
tracking algorithms, coordinates provided by the algorithms change all 
the time, even if a target object is not moving. It causes the shaking of 
the corresponding DT. Therefore, an anti-shake script was developed 
and applied to avoid the shaking of DTs. The side-effort is that the DTs of 
tracked objects disappear after three additional frames (about 0.4 s) in 
the visualisation module. Therefore, the failure of object detection in a 
single frame does not affect presenting hazard information. However, if 
an object is identified as two objects, two virtual models are shown in 
MR goggles simultaneously for four frames.

In Scenario 3, the proposed system successfully detected the spatial 
position of objects on different planes and identified potential hazards. 
The hazardous areas, including red, yellow, and blue zones, were 
automatically moved forward of vehicles according to the latency and 
speed of tracked objects to eliminate the demonstration error caused by 
latency. The yellow and blue zones are extended according to the 
calculated speed of the vehicle (e.g., 27 m for a 20 km/h car). The results 
show that for 84 cars out of 93 cars, the hazardous areas are well 
identified and displayed, as shown in Fig. 18 . The other cars were 
turning at the junction, which caused the red zone cannot cover them 
very well. However, the yellow and blue zones can still warn system 
users in advance to let them know the hazard sources are approaching. 
In order to cover all the hazardous areas, the hazard identification 
function should be further developed to expand the identified hazardous 
areas based on the movement trend of tracked objects. 
In the testing scenarios, participants have a tendency to move away 
from the displayed hazardous areas. The phenomenon indicates the 
proposed system has the potential to reinforce workers ’ safety behav -
iour. In the future, more experiments should be conducted to evaluate 
the influence of the displayed hazardous areas on the system users, 
especially their behaviours. The influence should be tested and analysed 
from the aspects of engineering psychology and cognitive ergonomics. 
The indicates should include participants ’ ability to recognise hazardous 
situations, participants ’ response time to specific hazards, and the 
duration of participants staying in each hazardous zone. Besides, the 
NASA Task Load Index should also be included to test whether and to 
what extent the additional information provided by MR devices in -
creases users ’ workload. 
6. Conclusion 
Presenting hazard information is helpful to support on-site workers 
in decision-making. However, conventional safety management does 
not adequately take advantage of emerging technologies to prevent 
workers from construction accidents. This study proposes a visual 
warning system to provide hazard information to workers, allowing 
workers to be aware of surrounding hazards and reassess risks. By 
integrating BIM, CV, and DL technologies, a VCS were developed in the 
system. The VCS simulates construction activities, identifies hazards, 
and generates hazard information based on BIM models and real-time 
information obtained from on-site cameras. The proposed system is 
able to present the hazard information to workers through wearable MR 
devices, letting them take proactive actions to avoid injuries. Three 
outdoor tests have been completed to verify the feasibility of the pro -
posed system. According to the results, the proposed system performs 
well in merging virtual objects with the real world and presenting 
hazard information. The effective area of the visualisation is more than 
72 m
2 
with a delay of no more than 424 ms when the camera is set at 
5.68 m height with an   25
o 
pitch angle. The hazard identification ac -
curacy is more than 96.1%, and the average hazardous area presentation 
accuracy is 11.47 cm. 
Nevertheless, some issues in this study need to be addressed in the 
future. Firstly, the function to eliminate lens distortion and the phe -
nomenon of perspective should be developed. Besides, an image-based 
prediction function will also be introduced into the proposed visual 
warning system. The hazardous areas caused by high-speed moving 
objects will be identified more accurately. More IoT devices will be 
embedded in the system to cover more hazard types. Moreover, the 
alignment of a virtual world with the real world needs to be done 
manually by users. In the future, the system will utilise spatial anchors to 
share spatial coordinates between different devices, enabling more ac -
curate and automated alignment. In addition, the application of AR and 
MR has been proven to be effective in enhancing worker performance, 
but the underlying rationale is not clear yet. Further theoretical research 
is needed to clarify the impact of advanced visualisation technology on 
workers ’ behaviours based on engineering psychology and cognitive 
ergonomics.
