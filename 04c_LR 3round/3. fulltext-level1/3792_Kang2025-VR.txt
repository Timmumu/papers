ABSTRACT Managing underground utility tunnels (UUT) poses challenges such as safety risks, limited
accessibility, and high training costs. To address these, we developed an effective VR-based UUT
management training simulator, integrating digital twins with a multi-degree-of-freedom (DoF) motion
platform to provide an immersive, cybersickness-reducing experience. Advances in actuator and computer
graphics technology have increased the research and development of virtual reality (VR)-based training
simulators that use multi-DoF motion platforms. In this study, the proposed system provides precisely
synchronized visual feedback through a head-mounted display and kinematic feedback via four-DoF
motor-based actuators, realistically simulating UUT conditions, including momentum shifts such as falls
and micro-swaying. To mitigate cybersickness, visual and kinematic acceleration control techniques are
employed, ensuring users can comfortably and safely experience the UUT without stability concerns.
We used a LiDAR sensor to model the actual UUT for realistic visualization in the VR environment and
created various scenario-based content, such as facility installation, inspection, and device repair, based on
the physical characteristics inside the UUT. This integrated approach enables users to effectively manage
and operate UUT facilities in a safe, controlled virtual setting, bridging the gap between traditional training
and real-world operations.

substantial economic losses. Consequently, numerous studies
have been conducted to ensure the sustainable operation and
maintenance of UUTs, with most countries implementing
design standards and regular maintenance guidelines [2], [3].
Past incidents associated with condensation and corro-
sion have underscored the need for effective prevention
systems, focusing on improved fire prediction, detection,
and response, as well as better environmental management

under challenging conditions, including narrow passages

to physical and psychological distress, and even severe
health issues over time [9], [10], [11]. Therefore, addressing
the human factor—ensuring worker safety, training, and
preparedness—is just as critical as improving infrastructure
itself. However, existing approaches focus on improving
disaster response and predictive systems but do not address
worker safety and training through immersive solutions.
In this study, we introduce a novel virtual training method
for UUTs by integrating digital twins with a four-degree of
freedom (4-DoF) motion platform, thereby enhancing immer-
sion and enabling more realistic emergency response and
maintenance training. Unlike existing VR training systems,
our proposed simulator couples a motion platform, motion
recognizer, and high-resolution content while incorporating
cybersickness reduction techniques to ensure prolonged
user comfort. We leverage threshold-based control—derived
from vestibular–visual differences and actual treadmill
velocities—to further mitigate cybersickness. This approach
is complemented by tracking natural walking patterns via
motion capture sensors to estimate and adjust treadmill
velocity, ultimately providing a stable and engaging VR
experience. We adopted methods from previous studies that
identified minimized visual–vestibular gaps as critical for
reducing cybersickness and conducted a usability evaluation
to comprehensively verify the system’s effectiveness. As a
result, this solution not only provides a safer and more
controlled training environment but also has the potential to
advance VR training technology and safety practices in UUT
maintenance and operation, ultimately contributing to the
broader field of sustainable urban infrastructure management.

II. RELATED WORKS
The proposed VR training systems face two critical chal-
lenges: providing naturalistic, immersive motion experiences
and mitigating the resulting cybersickness that can arise
when visual and vestibular cues are not perfectly aligned.
Addressing these issues requires examining both the evo-
lution of motion platforms designed to enhance realism
and the methods developed to reduce cybersickness in VR
environments.
A. MOTION PLATFORMS
Motion simulation performance is a key factor in the develop-
ment of realistic VR training simulators. To simulate multiple
DoF motion, most motion platforms adopt motion-cueing
algorithms (MCAs) based on a hexapod motion system [12],
[13], [14], [15].
Because of their geometric configuration and independent
control of each actuator, hexapods can precisely control
6-DoF motion, rendering them versatile for applications that
require high accuracy, such as flight and vehicle simulations.
Furthermore, modern commercial hexapod systems are
equipped with advanced control algorithms and user-friendly
interfaces that render integration into various simulation
settings facile [16], [17]. However, these systems are driven
only within a limited translational range in the space of the
structure. They cannot simulate walking, running, or other
forms of movement, which considerably reduces immersion
and realism when used in VR applications such as physical
rehabilitation and sports training.
Treadmill simulators are used to simulate walking and
running movements in physical rehabilitation, sports train-
ing, and military training. Although treadmills that enable
users to enjoy physically active VR adventures have been
developed [18], [19], [20], they require users to slide across
the floor, wearing specially designed shoes with their bodies
strapped to the device, resulting in an unnatural walking
experience. A high-speed two-dimensional (2D) treadmill
with a power transmission mechanism called ‘‘Geared Omni-
pulley’’ has been devised [21]; however, the system is

is challenging. Disney developed a treadmill using holotile

B. CYBERSICKNESS REDUCTION METHODS
V ection is a sensation of illusory self-motion without actual
movement and has been studied comprehensively. Visually
induced motion sickness (VIMS) resembles conventional
motion sickness but occurs without physical movement [23].
It is categorized into subcategories, namely cybersickness
in virtual environments and simulator sickness in driving
or flight simulators. When individuals are exposed to
virtual environments on large or curved screens, they
experience cybersickness. This condition is characterized by
nausea, oculomotor disturbances, and disorientation caused
by sensory discrepancies between visual, somatosensory, and
vestibular inputs. These symptoms occur rapidly, affecting
20%–50% of users [24], [25], [26], [27]. The condition
generally occurs when visual cues in a virtual environment
do not align with the body’s somatosensory and vestibular
senses. Therefore, posture control based on individual
sensory priorities is crucial during VR experience [28].
Recent research has demonstrated that VR-based language
education systems can enhance immersion and engagement,
thereby supporting the effectiveness of VR in educational
training. However, the study did not address specific
strategies for mitigating cybersickness [29]. Additionally,
Anwar et al. introduced a comprehensive framework for
evaluating Quality of Experience (QoE) in immersive media
consumption within autonomous vehicles, termed the ‘‘Mov-
ing Metaverse.’’ The study emphasizes key QoE factors such
as presence, immersion, and cybersickness, and discusses the
challenges of motion-induced sickness in VR environments.
To address these issues, the authors advocate for adaptive
content design, network optimization, and the integration of
standardized assessment methodologies to minimize motion
sickness and enhance user immersion [30].
Sensory conflicts in VR environments are a critical
topic of research. Finding effective and intuitive locomo-
tion techniques is a challenge for minimizing fatigue and
cybersickness. Moreover, maintaining immersion remains
challenging. Studies have analyzed scenarios in which users
physically move in one direction in a real environment.
However, the study reported that motion sickness still occurs
when moving in a different direction because of the sensory
mismatch between visual and vestibular stimuli.
Studies on cue conflict theory have investigated the
individual factors that induce cybersickness to diagnose and
alleviate symptoms in VR environments. A study found a
potential method for assessing visual cue sensitivity to vari-
ability [31]. The results demonstrated that a higher sensitivity
to motion parallax in VR was a significant predictor of motion
sickness severity. Studies have investigated object motion in
a VR headset to induce moderate levels of motion sickness.
After completing the motion task, the participants viewed
stereoscopic videos inducing discomfort in the VR headset.
The variables of sensitivity to motion sickness induced by
VR varied among individuals. Furthermore, observers tended
to self-regulate motion sickness through head movements.
The results also revealed that sensitivity considerably affected
rotational head movement. Although the suppression of
head movements was observed during translational motion,
the effect was significant during rotational motion. This
phenomenon suggests that rotational motion is a major factor
that induces motion sickness in VR.
Another study investigated the potential of synchronizing
physical movements with visual stimuli to alleviate VR-
related discomfort. For instance, a system that uses a motion
platform to simulate various levels of visual–vestibular syn-
chronization conditions can reduce cybersickness [32]. The
results indicated that users experienced lower levels of cyber-
sickness when their overall comfort improved. This improve-
ment occurred under the synchronized visual–vestibular con-
dition in multiple trials. The provided conditions simulated
visual–vestibular synchronization between visual rotation
and synchronized visual–vestibular motion to generate basic
movements. Furthermore, the study included vestibular
motion in a fixed self-referenced visual environment.
Minimal sensory adjustment was required when visual
and vestibular sensations were aligned with one another.
Thus, based on the sensory conflict theory, the severity
of cybersickness is likely to be regulated according to
individual sensitivity characteristics. The techniques used
to synchronize motion platforms could be used to design
interactive VR systems. However, developing strategies to
minimize cybersickness is crucial in motion platforms driven
by voluntary movements.
In early studies, cybersickness was considered less sen-
sitive to the effect of translational motion than to that of
rotational motion. However, limited studies have been con-
ducted on generalized reference values for the measurement
of cybersickness in VR. A consensus is yet to be reached
regarding the specific optical flow and intensity that cause
cybersickness. This can be attributed to various reasons;
however, limited robust empirical evidence regarding the
recommended strategies for VR developers to minimize
cybersickness could be a major factor. For this reason,
one study generalized the measurement of cybersickness
among VR users by distinguishing between their sensitivities
to translational and rotational motion [33]. Participants
were exposed to various optic flow types in VR to detect
rotational and translational velocities and accelerations.
The findings indicated that the velocity and acceleration
thresholds for rotational optic flow were notably lower than
those for translational optic flow. This study demonstrated

Studies have revealed that cybersickness can be reduced
through behavioral strategies related to an individual’s
posture depending on the choice of equipment. For example,
a study [34] revealed the effects of various motion stimuli
combinations and the user’s range of neck motion through
two experiments. Several navigation tasks were conducted
to measure the simulator sickness using four types of VR
locomotive controllers. A strategy to reduce cybersickness
can be adopted depending on the equipment selection. This
proves that spontaneous posture considerably affects cyber-
sickness reduction. In some cases, the predictive abilities
of conventional and recurrence quantification analysis-based
measures of postural activity were compared by investigating
spontaneous fluctuations in foot pressure in a stationary
position [35]. When exposed to an optical flow, participants
who displayed lower foot pressure were more likely to
report stronger vection and VIMS. These results indicate
that individual differences in spontaneous postural stability
enable the prediction of the likelihood of experiencing VIMS
in the future. This study demonstrated that individuals with
greater postural instability while standing still in stationary
visual environments are more likely to experience cyber-
sickness. The study reported that this effect occurred after
exposure to visual motion stimulation. However, addressing
cybersickness when considering subtle individual differences
and their impact could be less feasible. Establishing a
validation method that represents reliable cybersickness
reduction is necessary.
V arious studies have utilized external peripheral devices to
address this issue by devising different methods to reduce
sensory conflict and enhance the user experience [36],
[37]. These methods involve tracking the user’s physical
movements and translating them into virtual locomotion.
Exploring newer approaches that incorporate external devices
or simulators capable of reflecting a user’s physical
movements offers a promising solution for mitigating
cybersickness [36]. Moreover, effectively synchronizing an
individual’s active movements in strategies designed to
minimize the induction of cybersickness by incorporating
natural walking characteristics is essential [37]. Studies have
focused on exploring and designing optimized methods for
natural walking velocity adjustment. A controlled treadmill
enables users to adjust their walking velocity; however,
this experience differs from the experience of walking on
actual ground. Overcoming the sensory mismatch between
visual and vestibular stimuli is crucial for reducing disparities
between treadmill and real-world walking environments
and is essential for ensuring an accurate simulation of
real-world walking conditions during treadmill exercise.
Studies on walking in VR synchronized with a treadmill
have continuously investigated the design and analysis of
systems that update the walking velocity. A study considered
various aspects of walking motion characteristics to create
a natural walking environment [38]. In the study, two
locomotion techniques, an omnidirectional treadmill and arm
swinging, were compared in the context of VR environments.
According to this study, although the treadmill was designed
to simulate walking, users did not feel as if they were
walking, particularly during directional changes. Several
problems regarding methods that consider walking motion
characteristics remain unresolved.

III. DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATIONS
A. UUT TRAINING SIMULATOR
The proposed UUT training simulator was composed of a
control PC, a 4-DOF motion platform, a vertical fall device,
a motion recognizer, and an HMD (Fig. 1). The virtual reality
content of the UUT for experiential training is modeled
using high-precision LiDAR [49], which uses Simultaneous
Localization and Mapping (SLAM) technology to automat-
ically perform registration on the move, and is designed to
be similar to the actual tunnel so that users can experience
various facilities in the UUT, such as communication lines,
power lines, and water supply (general water and industrial
water), to satisfy the requirements of experiential training.
When the system initiates, the virtual reality content installed
on the PC is visualized on the HMD, and the motion
recognizer recognizes the user’s three-dimensional posture
and position in real time.
The motion recognizer used in the system incorporated an
Azure Kinect DK [50] and a Time of Flight (TOF)-based
motion recognition sensor to acquire the joint position and
motion data of the human participants. Using its built-in
body tracking SDK, the Azure Kinect DK estimates a 3D
skeletal model comprising 25 joints mapped to the human
body structure in real time, based on depth data and machine
learning algorithms.
The motion during walking, centered on the base of the
spine of the tracked joint skeleton, was estimated in real time
and applied to the virtual training content of the UUT. The
forward direction and speed of the participant relative to the
base of the spine determined the surge direction and speed of
the motion base. It constrained the user to stay centered and
not fall off the motion platform.
The motion base and PC were wired through UDP and
USB communication with the vertical falling device. The
motion base was precisely synchronized and simulated
according to the terrain in the virtual tunnel (inclination angle
on the x- and y-axes, fall, etc.) so that the user can experience
realistic UUT training within a limited space.
B. MOTION SIMULATOR
The proposed motion simulator consisted of a motion
platform and a vertical falling device for controlling a
4-DOF (roll, pitch, surge, and heave) motion. The nature
and purpose of the motion feedback of these two devices
in the simulator differ considerably; therefore, they are
operated independently using separate controllers for ease of
implementation.
The motion platform has 4-DoF and is categorized into
upper and lower driving parts. The bottom driving part is
called the motion base and is powered by three motorized
units (A, B, and C), as depicted in Fig. 2, providing 3-DoF
motion (roll, pitch, and heave).
FIGURE 2. Proposed 3-DoF motion base.
Each driving unit consists of a servomotor, a shaft, a lifting
link, and devices for fixing the parts. The links installed in
driving units A and B were in the same direction. The link
installed in driving unit C was in the opposite direction, and
these three links were connected to the top driving unit. In this
case, the axial coupling direction between the lifting link of
driving unit C and the top driving unit was perpendicular to
the axial coupling direction between the lifting link of driving
units A and B and the top driving unit. That is, the axis
coupled between the lifting link of driving unit C and the top
driving unit is parallel to the shaft. With this configuration
of driving units, the bottom drive unit performed pitch, roll,
and heave. The top driving part, also known as the treadmill
simulator, consisted of a servomotor, rubber belts, shafts, and
a walking pad to perform the surge of the motion base (Fig. 3).
These structures created a smooth motion, providing users
with a realistic and smooth walking experience in the VR
space.
FIGURE 3. Proposed top driving unit (treadmill).
The vertical falling device uses four pneumatic cylinders
to make the motion platform perform other heave motions
independent of the motion base, as depicted in Fig. 4,
to provide the user with a realistic falling experience. Each
cylinder was connected to an individual 5/2 solenoid valve,
which received an electrical signal from the controller and air
from the compressor to control the flow direction and speed
of the air, causing the plate to rise or fall. The air cylinder

By administering the SSQ before and after VR sessions,

headache, and motion sickness after experiencing one sim-

We employed the SSQ, originally introduced by [51],
to collect participants’ validated self-reported responses
regarding cybersickness. Through the MANOV A analysis,
we identified the significant effects of the the proposed cyber-
sickness reduction method and age groups on cybersickness
symptoms, including eye strain, headaches, and dizziness.
First, the overall model demonstrated a strong fit to the
data, with significant explanatory power for the dependent
variables. Specifically, Wilks’ Lambda was 0.0608, with an
associated F-statistic of 138.97 (p <0.001), indicating that the
combination of the independent variables (Age Group and
Condition) significantly influenced the cybersickness scores.
Regarding the effect of Age Group, the analysis showed a
statistically significant impact on cybersickness scores, with
Wilks’ Lambda = 0.2355, F(9, 65.86) = 5.94, p <0.001. This
result suggests that experiences of cybersickness symptoms
vary significantly among different age groups. Additional
metrics, including Pillai’s Trace and Hotelling-Lawley Trace,
corroborated this finding, reinforcing the robustness of the
age-related differences.
Participants in their 30s experienced the least eye
strain, headaches, and motion sickness, whereas those in
their 50s experienced the most strain. The participants
experienced the same eye strain with and without the
cybersickness reduction techniques; however, headaches and
motion sickness were less severe with the cybersickness
reduction techniques. In particular, participants in their 50s
experienced a considerable reduction in the intensity of
their headaches and motion sickness. Older adults have
a higher acceleration threshold at which they begin to
detect actual physical surge movements [52] and are more
comfortable walking on a treadmill that accelerates and
decelerates than younger participants. Standard deviations
ranged up to 1.4, indicating moderate variability across
subgroups. The normality tests using Mardia Skewness (p
= 0.6717) and Mardia Kurtosis (p = 0.9244) indicated no
significant deviation from a multivariate normal distribution.
The condition variable, which compared the presence or
absence of the cybersickness reduction technology, also had a
significant effect on the dependent variables. Wilks’ Lambda
for this factor was 0.6780, with F(3, 27) = 4.27, p = 0.0136.

V. CONCLUSION
This study provided a realistic UUT training system with
minimal motion sickness to participants by precisely con-
trolling visual and physical object movements in a virtual
environment using a 4-DoF motion platform. The proposed
system provides realistic visual and various kinesthetic
motion feedback, such as sudden fall while walking and
micro-vibration caused by trucks passing by on the ground,
by closely examining the real UUT environment and
modeling it using a LiDAR sensor. Furthermore, visual and
kinesthetic acceleration control methods were applied to
minimize motion sickness in participants experiencing VR
on a motion platform to provide a comfortable experience
training for participants. To evaluate the proposed simulator,
we analyzed the cybersickness induced by experiencing
the UUT training program with 17 participants of various
ages. The results revealed that compared with existing VR
solutions, the training not only reduced dizziness and motion
sickness but also demonstrated the effectiveness of facility
management training in a UUT.
The proposed motion platform should be improved con-
siderably. Because most of the interior of the UUT is a long
straight passageway, the 4-DoF motion platform proposed
in this study is sufficient for simulating such a tunnel.
However, to satisfy the various types of VR applications,
supporting yaw or sway motions is difficult. As the system
has 4 DOFs, we are currently working toward adding yaw to
create a 5-DOF motion platform to realize an unlimited yaw
movement capability of 360◦ through a rotary drive on top of
the motion base [53].
