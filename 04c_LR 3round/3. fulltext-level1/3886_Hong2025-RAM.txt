ABSTRACT
Poor construction worksite conditions cause many Serious Injuries and Fatalities (SIF). This paper introduces the 
Right-time Analysis and Mitigation (RAM) module as part of the Digital Twin for Construction Safety (DTCS) 
concept. RAM (a) leverages safety rule-based compliance checking of construction layout plans in Building In -
formation Modeling (called safeBIM) and (b) records and analyzes resource trajectory data involved in potential 
struck-by incidents using wearable and mobile Real-Time Kinematic Global Navigation Satellite Systems (RTK- 
GNSS) technology. RAM further (c) provides new access to unprecedented information and gives detailed in -
sights into human-machine interactions that (d) permit rapid implementation of change by targeted control. 
Results from real-life validations create an in-depth understanding of the potential root causes that often lead to 
harmful struck-by incidents. While the proposed DTCS technology contributes data input and information 
output, the change in safety management processes leads to safer work practices. Both prevent SIF that plague 
the construction sector.
1. Introduction
Construction sites are widely recognized as some of the most haz -
ardous workplaces, with higher rates of fatalities and injuries compared 
to any other industrial sector. In the European Union, for example, 
construction is responsible for over one-fifth of all workplace fatalities, 
with up to 15 % resulting from incidents where workers are struck by 
equipment or other objects [ 1 ]. Similarly, in the United States, the close 
proximity of pedestrian workers — those who are on foot at off-road 
worksites working alongside vehicles and equipment — to equipment 
or its components increases the risk of struck-by injuries, accounting for 
48 % fatal and 20 % nonfatal injuries in construction sites [ 2 , 3 ]. The 
inherent safety risks on construction sites arise from the simultaneous 
execution of multiple tasks and the presence of essential resources 
within the constrained workspaces, such as partially completed struc -
tures, temporary installations of supportive equipment, and cluttered 
areas filled with building materials. In tasks involving heavy equipment, 
workers are particularly at risk of being run over, crushed, or struck by 
moving equipment or falling objects. Unless controlled, many of these 
risks can evolve into serious injuries and fatalities (SIF).
The potential root causes and control methods of many SIF are well 
studied. Consequentially, as one of many examples around the world, 
the German Workplace Ordinance [ 4 ] sets (minimum) requirements for 
employee safety and health protection. Such a law primarily intends to 
establish and operate safe workplaces in the construction sector. It re -
quires employers to identify and assess the risks associated with work 
tasks and design hazards out before work starts. While various measures 
exist to mitigate risks upon detection, such as installing barricades that 
clearly separate pedestrian workers from onsite vehicle traffic, safety 
plans are still predominantly generated manually and are rarely updated 
along dynamic construction schedules. Likewise, safety responsibilities 
are often outsourced to consultants managing multiple projects, making 
it difficult to frequently update safety plans. Often, construction site 
safety supervision is left to site managers and superintendents. Unless a 
construction site reaches a decent size to warrant dedicated safety 
personnel on site, safety tasks stay within broad field monitoring. 
Furthermore, unplanned work or changes in activities often lead to non- 
compliant safety practices, necessitating continuous monitoring.
Traditional safety management tools rely heavily on manual 
methods, such as identifying hazard zones on printed layouts and con -
ducting manual site observations [ 5 ]. Nevertheless, labor-intensive 
methods often lack sufficient frequency and accuracy to keep up with 
the actual site condition. Relying yet on safety managers ’ experience and 
subjective judgment can result in overlooking hazards that can result in

The emergence of advanced technologies as part of digitalizing 
construction safety processes has significantly transformed safety man -
agement tools. For instance, Building Information Modeling (BIM) 
method has enhanced the geometric and semantic representation of 
construction schedules, enabling more precise planning. BIM has facil -
itated the adoption of the Prevention through Design and Planning (PtD/ 
P) concept by enabling the proactive identification and elimination of 
hazards during the early stages of construction planning. Recent ad -
vancements include preemptive hazard zone identification algorithms 
that automate the creation of a digital construction safety plan [ 6 – 9 ]. 
Sensing technology has also advanced, enabling continuous and accu -
rate monitoring of safety status. Despite varying location errors, Real- 
Time Location Tracking Systems (RTLS), such as Ultra-Wideband 
(UWB) and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), allow resource 
trajectory monitoring in outdoor construction environments in runtime 
[ 10 , 11 ]. The integration of magnetic-field technology on construction 
equipment and wearable radio-frequency tags on pedestrian workers 
enhances situational awareness around construction equipment [ 12 ]. 
Such advancement in technologies can enable the detection of incidents 
where workers enter hazard zones, providing the involved operator and 
worker/s with critical real-time alerts. These alerts help prevent injuries 
or equipment damage. Additionally, incident data logging and analysis 
have demonstrated value for safety managers and trainers, supporting 
the development of virtual training environments as part of serious 
gaming [ 13 – 18 ].
However, a research gap remains in the method of holistically inte -
grating the output from these technologies, resulting in incomplete 
hazard identification and incident detection. (Semi-)Static hazard 
zones — temporary zones with hazards that are not mitigated effectively 
or immediately and are often pre-identified on the site layout plan — and 
dynamic hazard zones - areas surrounding moving and operating 
equipment that constantly change in location and size along with con -
struction activities - cannot be effectively merged for incident detection. 
This limitation significantly impedes the ability of safety personnel to 
accurately assess the on-site safety situation. Even with the availability 
of digital safe site layout plans, merely visualizing RTLS data on a 
simplified or outdated site layout model in BIM would still require sig -
nificant human effort to detect or monitor deviations over time before a 
safety responsible person could react upon worthwhile findings [ 5 , 19 ].
Recent studies towards this direction have proposed a Digital Twin 
for Construction Safety (DFCS) as a method to enhance safety engi -
neering and management processes throughout the planning and 
execution phases [ 7 ]. DTCS not only automates data flows between 
physical construction sites and their digital counterparts but also en -
ables the analysis, prediction, and prevention of next or future hazard -
ous incidents. Additionally, DTCS serves as a comprehensive platform 
and user interface for exchanging data, information, and knowledge 
related to safe site layout planning, monitoring, and control [ 7 ]. As such, 
a digital twin that integrates models and resource trajectories has the 
great potential to make construction safety more efficient, proactive, 
and adaptive.
This paper focuses on the process of the automation and integration 
of incident detection, assessment, and proactive mitigation within the 
DTCS framework. The process is achieved by connecting the modules of 
safety layout planning and sensor-based tracking, utilizing statistical 
methods to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of hazards and in -
cidents, and providing site layout adaption recommendations, which are 
eventually integrated into the site model. Additionally, this study eval -
uates the applicability and performance of Real-Time Kinematic-Global 
Navigation Satellite System (RTK-GNSS), a GNSS-based tracking solu -
tion with improved accuracy and reliability [ 20 , 21 ], for construction 
resource location tracking in live outdoor construction work 
environments. In summary, this study aims to address the following 
research questions (RQ): 
• RQ1: Is RTK-GNSS a viable and accurate location tracking technol -
ogy for resource tracking in live outdoor construction work 
environments?
• RQ2: How can digital worksite layout plans and location tracking 
technologies be integrated to enhance safety planning, monitoring, 
and incident prevention?
• RQ3: What level of detail is required to identify and understand the 
root causes contributing to too-close proximity or struck-by equip -
ment incidents?
• RQ4: Accordingly, what are suitable mitigative measures for the 
different types of incidents, and how can these measures be effec -
tively communicated and implemented in the DTCS?
This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related work 
in BIM-based safety planning, sensing technologies for resource location 
tracking, and the most recent works on digital twins with respect to 
construction safety. Section 3 presents the proposed framework and the 
developed computational methods. Section 4 details the field experi -
mental setups and presents results from two realistic case study imple -
mentations. Lastly, the method ’ s strengths and limitations are reviewed 
and discussed, followed by future research directions.
2. Related work
This section presents an overview of existing researches that focus on 
digital site layout planning, real-time location sensing, and digital twin 
applications for construction safety.
2.1. From manual to digital safety site layout planning
BIM has made significant contributions to the digitalization of con -
struction management processes, incorporating multiple domains crit -
ical to construction projects, such as design, cost, and scheduling. 
However, the integration of safety into BIM in the project design and 
planning phases, which would enable Health, Safety, and Environment 
(HSE) professionals to eliminate or reduce hazards at the earliest 
possible moment or more frequently, has only recently begun to gain 
recognition [ 9 ].
So far, digital safety planning using BIM methods aligns with the 
existing PtD/P strategy, which proactively identifies and eliminates 
hazards during the early design stages of a project, continuing up until 
the start of execution [ 8 ]. While PtD/P is still applied iteratively in the 
operations phase, it primarily involves the identification and mitigation 
of known yet uncontrolled hazards before they escalate active risks [ 22 ]. 
In the context of safe construction equipment operations, Teizer and 
Cheng [ 23 ] classified the hazard types, while Johansen et al. [ 9 ] further 
expanded this classification, distinguishing between semi-static and 
dynamic hazard zones.
Traditionally, PtD/P requires substantial manual effort from the 
architectural and engineering disciplines [ 24 ]. For instance, in con -
ventional risk assessment, the safety responsible must manually identify 
unprotected leading edges and floor openings on printed construction 
drawings to determine where fall protection equipment is required. As 
project sizes grow, this task becomes increasingly labor-intensive and 
prone to errors [ 25 ]. Even with the adoption of BIM models, a safety 
responsible knowledgeable in BIM must examine three-dimensional 
(3D) building information models and schedules to identify hazards 
embedded in the spatial-temporal context of the design intent. To assist 
this process, automated hazard identification has been proposed, uti -
lizing BIM object information (e.g., geometry, spatial-temporal re -
lationships, and associated work tasks) to reduce manual efforts and 
detect hazard zones that might otherwise be overlooked [ 6 , 26 ].
Zhang et al. [ 6 ] first came up with a rule-checking algorithm to

2.2. Real-time resource location tracking
Real-time location tracking using sensor-based systems is an essen -
tial method for monitoring the locations of workers, equipment, and 
other resources on construction sites.
The research community has extensively tested various location 
tracking systems to evaluate their effectiveness on construction sites. 
Cheng et al. [ 30 ] assessed UWB systems for real-time tracking of mobile 
resources in challenging construction environments and performed an -
alyses of worksite operations through case studies. Their findings 
demonstrated UWB’s applicability for the design of construction man -
agement tools. Costin and Teizer [ 31 ] used Radio Frequency Identifi -
cation (RFID) to monitor worker locations in indoor environments, 
visualizing these locations within BIM models. Park et al. [ 5 ] presented a 
safety monitoring framework that integrates Bluetooth Low Energy 
(BLE)-based location tracking technology with BIM.
For outdoor tracking in construction sites, Global Positioning Sys -
tems (GPS) have been widely applied due to their simplicity and wide 
coverage [ 32 ]. Zhang et al. [ 33 ] utilized GPS data loggers attached to 
workers’ hardhats to monitor their movements during the construction 
of cast-in-place concrete columns. RTK-GNSS, an enhanced GPS solu -
tion, improves accuracy by receiving signals from more satellites and 
incorporating corrections from static sensors, thereby delivering 
centimeter-level accuracy [ 34 ]. Compared with standard GNSS, RTK- 
GNSS provides significantly higher accuracy and reliability, making it 
especially valuable for safety monitoring applications. Beyond sensor- 
based tracking, computer vision has become a prominent alternative 
for detecting and monitoring construction resource detection and 
monitoring on construction sites. For instance, Kulinan et al. [ 19 ] 
highlighted the application of vision-based tracking, while Pfitzner et al. 
[ 35 ] used vision data to track workers’ locations and map them onto 
floor plans for productivity analysis.
Collected location data often requires further filtering to remove 
offsets. Various filters, such as the speed filter, Kalman filter, and 
Savitzky-Golay filter, have been implemented to preprocess collected 
location data for productivity monitoring and analysis [ 31 , 36 ]. How -
ever, these low-accuracy techniques are not suitable for immediate 
incident detection, as they may trigger false alarms or, more critically, 
fail to detect critical incidents. To sum up, Table 1 summarizes the 
features, benefits, and application scenarios of various location tracking 
technologies referenced for construction safety monitoring.
2.3. Digital twin for construction safety
A digital twin is a data-driven model that mirrors real-time condi -
tions of physical systems, enabling autonomous control and manage -
ment based on accumulated insights [ 37 ]. Originally adopted from the 
manufacturing sector, this concept has been adapted in construction 
research to enhance real-time monitoring, decision-making, and process 
optimization. Sacks et al. [ 38 ] introduced the term Digital Twin Con -
struction (DTC) as a data-driven management and control model, 
leveraging various site monitoring technologies and data sources to 
provide accurate real-time status updates and enable proactive process 
optimization. Similarly, other studies [ 39 , 40 ] suggest digital twins as a 
comprehensive approach for real-time management and control over 
construction processes, equipment, materials, workforce, quality, and 
safety.
Teizer et al. [ 7 ] first introduced the concept of Digital Twin for Con -
struction Safety (DTCS), integrating safety assessment and control within 
the DTC framework. DTCS comprises four key steps: (a) hazard pre -
vention, (b) conformance checking, (c) right-time alerts and analysis, 
and (d) continuous performance improvement via proactive learning 
and feedback. DTCS enhances the utility of real-time site data (e.g., 
building progress, schedules) by integrating on-site sensor data, 
enabling continuous safety monitoring, incident detection, proactive 
avoidance, and learning.
In digital twins, the integration of Internet of Things (IoT) devices 
enables the collection and transmission of real-time data for processing 
in the digital twin environment. Zhao et al. [ 41 ] introduced a BLE 
sensor-based digital twin framework for safety management, demon -
strating its effectiveness in tracking motion and identifying stationary 
behavior through accelerometer data in residential building construc -
tion projects. Similarly, Ye et al. [ 42 ] developed a digital twin-enabled 
platform for safety management and early warning systems in dy -
namic tunnel construction, analyzing sensor data to provide real-time 
alerts to potential incidents.
Digital twin platforms enable the implementation of sensor-based 
spatiotemporal analysis, allowing for immediate responses to monitor 
safety conditions, detect incidents, and issue timely warnings. This 
analysis primarily evaluates the proximity of workers to hazard zones, 
facilitating targeted safety interventions. For instance, Li et al. [ 43 ] 
explored the use of crowd-sourced density maps to infer safety condi -
tions across multiple site areas, demonstrating how collective data can 
enhance situational awareness. Golovina et al. [ 12 ] proposed a frame -
work for assessing the severity of close-call incidents based on worker

3. Framework and methods
This section expands upon and elaborates on the DTCS framework, 
providing additional details on the methods employed in this study, 
which are subsequently applied in a case study for validation.
3.1. Framework
Building upon the DTCS framework [ 44 ], an enhanced framework 
was developed to integrate RTK-GNSS with BIM for hazard detection, 
incident management, and layout modification recommendations. As 
shown in Fig. 1 , this framework focuses on the Right-time Analysis and 
Mitigation (RAM) module, which interfaces with other modules to enable 
timely and effective safety interventions.
As defined in prior research, hazard zones are classified as semi-static 
and dynamic [ 9 ]. Semi-static hazard zones arise from operational con -
straints or planned activities and maintain consistent geometry and 
location over a time period. In contrast, dynamic hazard zones are asso -
ciated with moving and operating equipment and are characterized by 
variable geometry and location.
To identify hazard zones, the PtD/P module accesses the site layout 
and equipment models from the Project Status Knowledge within a DTC 
[ 38 ]. Semi-static hazard zones are determined through a risk assessment 
process based on established safety standards and regulations. Dynamic 
hazard zones are defined initially by delineating a 1-m boundary (ac -
cording to [ 45 ]) and identifying equipment operator blind spaces from 
the equipment models (like previous research [ 46 – 50 ]).
Since the location of dynamic hazard zones shifts as equipment 
moves, location and orientation data are continuously collected from 
sensors mounted on the equipment to accurately track these changes in 
real time. The Construction Site Interface module gathers real-time site 
data, including the locations of equipment and pedestrian workers. The 
identified hazard zones, along with the location data, are then trans -
mitted to the RAM module for incident detection. A hazard zone be -
comes active when an incident is detected within it. If the pedestrian 
worker within this active hazard zone lacks the capacity to control the 
incident, it escalates to a close call (def. in [ 51 ]), triggering an imme -
diate alert. This alert is sent to the Construction Site to enable proactive 
measures to prevent events that otherwise could result in an injury, fa -
tality, and/or collateral damage.
Each close call initiates a root cause analysis, with the results 
communicated to the safety responsible (incl. HSE manager) assigned to 
the worksite. Regardless of whether an incident escalates to a close call, 
all events are systematically documented to facilitate active, personal -
ized learning in the Personalized Learning module. This process involves 
analyzing incident density maps — areas of concentrated activity that 
warrant further attention — alongside movement patterns, hazardous 
activities, and active work zones. These insights are then used to update 
safety protocols and recommend mitigation strategies, such as gener -
ating designated zones or temporary controlled walkways, by revising 
the safety plan.

Before construction starts, a safety plan is generated from as-planned 
building models, and a first baseline safety plan is selected. As con -
struction progresses, the plan is continuously updated to reflect the 
current construction status and accommodates the results of confor -
mance checks (as shown by [ 52 ]).

Designated zones and protective equipment play a critical role in 
preventing workers from being exposed to hazards. A safety plan defines 
work and support areas — including work zones, pedestrian walkways, 
and equipment movement zones — and specifies the locations of pro -
tective equipment. It also identifies built-in hazard zones arising from 
unavoidable risks inherent in the design and planning phases, such as 
traversable intersections.
SafeBIM, a BIM-based safety layout, integrates safety details into as- 
built and as-performed BIM models. It is created manually or through 
automated methods, such as the SafeCon framework [ 30 ], to incorpo -
rate safety equipment and highlight hazard zones. This study focuses on 
integrating location-based hazard analysis into SafeBIM. The use of In -
dustry Foundation Class (IFC)-formatted BIM ensures interoperability 
across different BIM tools [ 53 ].
The PtD/P module identifies hazard zones within the construction 
models and utilizes Ifcopenshell, an open-source Python library, to 
extract their locations and geometries [ 54 ]. For example, unprotected 
crossings are classified as semi-static struck-by hazard zones, while 
unprotected leading edges in active elevated workspaces are categorized 
as semi-static fall hazard zones. Fig. 2 presents an example of how a 
struck-by traffic hazard zone is identified and extracted.
Commercially available components were configured to an RTK- 
GNSS system that provides high-precision, real-time tracking of pedes -
trian workers and construction equipment. The developed system con -
sists of one base station and multiple rovers. The base station broadcasts 
correction signals to nearby rovers, enabling them to correct measure -
ment errors. Although public or subscription-based base station services 
are available, using a ‘private ’ base station enables centimeter-level 
precision within the coverage area without requiring extensive infra -
structure or incurring high implementation costs. For this study, a base 
station was installed on the construction site, placed statically in an open 
space with an unobstructed view of the sky. The rovers were attached to 
construction resources — embedded as wearables in safety vests (on the 
back or shoulder) for pedestrian workers and mounted on top of 
equipment cabins for construction equipment.
Hazard monitoring and incident detection require the integration of 
hazard zone data with real-time location data. Hazard zones are 
extracted from SafeBIM, which must first be georeferenced to ensure 
alignment with the location data. The integration begins by unifying the 
coordinate systems, which differ in dimensions, scales, and datums. 
SafeBIM typically operates in a local Cartesian coordinate system opti -
mized for site-specific precision and efficient data handling. In contrast, 
RTK-GNSS uses the World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS84), represented 
by latitude, longitude, and altitude.
To align the two, WGS84 coordinates are first converted to the 
Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) system, a Cartesian coordinate 
system compatible with BIM. The final step involves applying a trans -
formation matrix to translate and rotate the UTM system so it matches 
the local BIM coordinate system. This procedure is detailed in the 
pseudo-code in Algorithm 1.
Location data is streamed to the DTCS in real time and stored locally. 
This way complies with the required General Data Protection Regula -
tions (GDPR). A data preprocessing module filters the incoming data to 
remove noise, converts it to the BIM coordinate system, and reformats it 
as (id, x, y, z, t) before passing it to the run-time incident detector. The 
RAM module detects incidents during real-time site monitoring and 
creates valuable output information for the safety responsible in the 
Digital Twin user interface. If necessary, it sends immediate alerts to the 
affected pedestrian workers and equipment operators.

3.3. Assessing safety performance and site conditions
Construction sites present unique challenges due to the presence of 
both semi-static and dynamic hazard zones, each requiring distinct ap -
proaches for effective incident detection and assessment.
3.3.1. Detecting unauthorized entry and too-close proximity
Every construction resource is surrounded by a (virtual) protective 
envelope (aka. Safety envelope) designed to restrict intrusion by other 
resources (e.g., vehicles or parts). For dynamic hazards, the safety en -
velope aligns (at minimum) with the associated hazard zones. Three 
levels of detail in safety envelope models are considered: 
• Simplified model : Represents the boundary of construction re -
sources as polygons or circles with a 2-m boundary as a safety en -
velope for general safety requirements, adjusted per standard. The 
safety envelope for a pedestrian worker is modeled as a circle with a 
1.5-m radius, adjusted according to standard guidelines.
• Detailed model : Incorporates detailed measurement of risky areas 
surrounding the construction resources. For example, construction 
equipment involves operation range, blind spaces, and a 1-m 
boundary. The blind spaces are based on operational visibility 
guidelines defined in ISO 5006:2017 [ 45 ]. These parameters are 
further derived from on-site observations or manufacturer specifi -
cations when available.
• Statistical model : Utilizes spatial data distributions and movement 
patterns around construction equipment, necessitating substantial 
sampling to map the areas where each construction resource can 
operate safely and without interruption.
Incidents are primarily categorized into two types: unauthorized 
workspace entries and too-close proximity to moving equipment. 
• Unauthorized entry: Pedestrian workers or mobile equipment 
deviate from designated work zones and enter semi-static hazard 
zones, such as equipment driveways.

3.3.2. Creating density maps and identifying hazardous activities
An incident or risk density map visualizes work zones based on 
observed incident frequencies, using heatmaps to present the data in an 
intuitive and easily interpretable format. Areas with higher incident 
frequencies can inform predictive analysis and support the proactive 
elimination of future risks. In addition to incident counts, equipment 
behavior data, such as frequent speeding or reverse motion, also 
contribute to classifying hazardous activities in work zones. For 
example, backward driving presents an elevated hazard level due to 
limited rear visibility. Although construction equipment typically drives 
at slower speeds than road vehicles, any increase in speed can signifi -
cantly increase the hazard level. Therefore, construction site speed 
limits are typically set by a traffic engineer and range anywhere from 5 
to 15 km/h [ 55 ]; in this study, a 10 km/h speed limit was applied.
To visualize the spatial distribution of incidents and hazardous ac -
tivities, density maps are generated using 2D histograms of location data 
points. Each histogram maps the number of events within a grid of 1 × 1 
meter cells over a specified time period. Since data are collected at a 
fixed frequency, the number of location data points in each cell directly 
reflects the duration of events within that cell. The 1-m grid resolution 
was chosen to align with the average worker ’ s body dimensions 
(approximately 0.5 m in width) and movement characteristics (1.3 – 1.5 
m/s), ensuring a practical and human-scale level of detail. Once the 
heatmap is created, dense areas are identified through a thresholding 
process. In this study, the median value was used as the threshold to 
filter out outliers, such as areas where equipment may have been idling 
for extended periods.
The identified cells are then combined into polygons, representing 
areas where event occurrences exceed the median value. To facilitate 
spatial data extraction and integration into the updated safety plan, 
polygons are in the further simplified using the Ramer-Douglas-Peucker 
algorithm [ 56 ]. It reduces the complexity of the polygons by removing 
vertices while preserving the overall shape and key features. By mini -
mizing the number of points in the polygons, computational load is 
reduced, enabling faster processing for real-time hazard detection or 
spatial mapping. The process for identifying dense areas is summarized 
in Algorithm 2.

3.4. Revising and updating the safety plan
To enhance safety plans, a quantitative analysis is conducted to 
identify the dense areas of incidents and hazardous activities, focusing 
on incident frequency and their spatial distribution. Based on the anal -
ysis results, revisions to the safety plan are proposed. The primary 
strategy is to improve the spatial separation between workers and 
equipment, thereby significantly reducing the risk of future conflicts and 
safety incidents.
3.4.1. Separating worker and equipment areas
As described previously, a dense area of incidents is defined as a 
cluster of cells with an above-median incident occurrence. These areas 
require additional localized attention and targeted safety interventions.

between pedestrian workers and mobile equipment in the highly fre -

calculate the overlap ratio, as calculated using the formula:

3.4.2. Generating temporary pedestrian walkway
Temporary controlled walkways serve as an effective mitigation 
strategy for resolving movement zone conflicts between workers and 
equipment, particularly when predefined pedestrian paths are insuffi -
cient. The temporary walkways are designed to follow optimal routes 
that minimize pedestrian workers ’ exposure to equipment movement 
while maintaining travel efficiency.
A graph-based route optimization approach is utilized, combining 
Dijkstra ’ s algorithm with a visibility graph [ 59 ]. Unlike cell-based 
methods, which divide walkable space into uniform grids and often 
produce inefficient or indirect paths, visibility line analysis connects the 
vertices of the polygonal obstacles and walkable areas via direct lines, 
allowing for a smoother and more realistic path [ 60 ].
Dijkstra ’ s algorithm, a well-established method for finding the 
shortest path in a weighted graph, iteratively selects the node with the 
lowest cumulative cost and explores its adjacent nodes until the target is 
reached [ 61 ]. In this approach, polygon vertices are recognized as nodes 
in the Dijkstra ’ s algorithm, and edges are evaluated for line-of-sight 
visibility. Any edge intersecting with non-walkable areas is discarded, 
ensuring that only feasible paths are considered.
To incorporate safety factors, this cost function accounts for both 
travel distance and hazard level. As illustrated in Fig. 3 , the process 
begins by identifying start and end points based on workers ’ frequently 
visited areas and active work areas. Walkable zones are then defined, 
and paths intersecting hazardous regions are penalized with higher 
travel costs to prioritize safer alternatives. The cost of intersections be -
tween pedestrian walkways and hazardous areas is factored into the 
analysis, increasing the travel cost of routes by traversing hazardous 
zones to prioritize safer paths. Notably, this approach simplified the 
dimensions and trajectories of workers into an idealized point and 
straight line, ensuring computational simplicity and compatibility with 
Dijkstra ’ s algorithm.

4.1. RTK-GNSS implementation
An RTK-GNSS system [ 62 ], based on a Long Range (LoRa) network 
[ 63 ], was deployed to monitor the movement of workers and equipment 
throughout a three-day-long field experimental period. The system, 
sourced from simpleRTK2B, consisted of a base station and multiple 
rovers. As shown in Fig. 4 , the base station was positioned on top of a 
two-story office container, while rovers were embedded into existing 
pockets on pedestrian workers ’ safety vests. For mobile equipment, 
rover antennas were mounted through existing strong magnets outside 
and on top of the equipment cabin, centered close to the equipment 
operator ’ s seat. All relevant dimensions and equipment specifications 
were recorded.
Location data collection and streaming began once the base station 
was fully operational and the rovers started receiving correction signals. 
Workers ’ locations were recorded at a frequency of 1 Hz, the system ’ s 
default setting, while equipment locations were recorded at 5 Hz to 
better capture motion. All data were stored locally on smartphones 
connected to the rovers.
During data collection, a precision test was conducted for the RTK- 
GNSS system at the implementation site. According to the RTK board 
and device manufacturer ’ s specifications, the system supports three 
tracking modes: RTK Fix, RTK Float, and Single [ 64 ]. RTK Fix offers 
centimeter-level precision, RTK Float provides accuracy within 0.5 to 1 
m, and Single mode has the lowest precision, with accuracy ranging 
from 1 to 5 m.
To assess the system ’ s precision, a rover was placed at a clear, un -
obstructed location in the staging area, and its position was recorded 
continuously for three hours. The system operated in RTK Fix mode, and 
the deviation from the mean position was used to evaluate precision. 
Approximately 10,800 location points were collected. The 50th 
percentile precision was 1.0 cm, and the precision at the 95th percentile 
reached 2.3 cm. Fig. 5 presents a visualization of the precision results, 
demonstrating the RTK-GNSS system ’ s suitability for high-accuracy 
tracking in field applications.

4.2. Hazard zone identification
A site layout model of the staging area was created using commer -
cially available BIM software for manual modeling objects and geome -
tries. Self-taken photographs and satellite images provided valuable 
input to designate zones for the identified work area, such as existing 
building structures, rail tracks, temporary material storage areas, and 
office containers. Based on this site layout model, the researchers, 
guided by responsible safety engineers, developed a safe baseline model 
to delineate distinct movement zones for the pedestrian workers and 
equipment. All of this work was done independently from the RTK-GNSS 
data collection and analysis to avoid potential bias.
As shown in Fig. 6 , pedestrian worker walkways, mobile equipment 
driveways, and rail track zones were color-coded within the model. 
Given that the experimental site was confined to a single ground level, 
the coordinates were simplified to two dimensions (2D) for clarity and 
ease of use.
In the baseline model, concrete barriers were recommended for 
installation along the boundaries of the pedestrian walkways to protect 
them from nearby moving equipment. These barriers were intended to 
create a physical separation between workers and vehicles, ensuring 
that pedestrian workers remained within designated safe zones and 
reducing the likelihood of inadvertent entry into equipment driveways. 
However, due to operational requirements, full separation of pedestrian 
walkways and equipment driveways could not be achieved. Specifically, 
vehicles moving across these (traversable) zones were necessary to allow 
equipment, such as telehandlers and wheeled loaders, to access key 
areas for material transportation to and from the railcars. These had to 
be loaded and unloaded with new or recyclable material, respectively. 
This inability to fully separate worker and equipment zones resulted in 
the declaration of one of the research objectives: the identification of 
these intersections as semi-static hazard zones.
Dynamic hazards were observed during the experiments, particularly 
due to the movement and operation of a telehandler tasked with 
transporting materials between storage zones and railcars. The tele -
handler ’ s continuous operation created a hazardous environment for 
nearby pedestrian workers. Yet, they often needed to guide the tele -
handler during material handling tasks, such as positioning the forks 
into the handles of the ballast bags. This close coordination required 
workers to stand in very close proximity to the telehandler, often within 
its 1-m boundary and several blind spaces. The combination of workers 
standing near the equipment and the operator ’ s limited visibility in 
these critical zones significantly increased the risk of struck-by incidents 
or close calls.

its frequent traversal of pedestrian walkways, creating overlaps between

worker presence in specific areas. Brighter regions represent areas 
where the workers spent more time. The results reveal that Worker 01 
had a concentrated presence on the left and right front of the tele -

pattern.
The concentrated presence in the right front area, which overlaps 
with the telehandler ’ s larger blind space area to the right than to the left 
(as identified in the visibility analysis, Fig. 13 ), raises the question of 
whether Worker 01 should operate from the left than right front side 
(more visibility). This observation underscores the importance of 
focusing on such high detail. The result is encouraging workers to 
approach the equipment from safer angles, such as from the left front 
where visibility is clearer (no lifting arm blocks the operator ’ s field of 
view). This could further reduce the risks associated with the tele -
handler ’ s blind spots.

5. Discussion and limitations
The work performed demonstrates that RTK-GNSS offers enhanced 
accuracy and precision for outdoor workplace tracking, thereby 
reducing the need for extensive post-processing and enabling effective 
real-time alerts and analysis (RQ1). The combination of digital worksite 
layout baseline planning and construction resource location tracking 
results in safer work alternatives (RQ2) and leads to a more detailed 
understanding of the root causes that contribute to fragmented on-site 
safety behavior (RQ3). As such, three experiments specific to too-close 
proximity or struck-by equipment incidents were showcased to 
generate previously unavailable knowledge. To name a few examples: 
workers ’ presence inside equipment blind spaces, limited operator vis -
ibility, entry of resources into other than designated zones, various 
dangers involved in forward and reverse motions, some occasional 
speeding, and design of internal traffic plans, incl. Providing protected 
pedestrian walkways and separated driveways. All these play important 
roles in analyzing the current, predicting the future, and eliminating 
struck-by hazards in daily operations. Once modifications were sug -
gested, these led to positive changes (RQ4). Applying standard Pre -
vention through Design and Planning (PtD/P) approaches using the 
proposed digital support provided access to detailed proactive feedback 
(here: safer logistics in material handling operations involving 
machinery).
From a research point of view, this work shed light on the underlying 
semi-static and dynamic hazard zones. These were associated with 
pedestrian workforce and heavy equipment interacting in complex and 
highly dynamic construction settings. Several key parameters of engi -
neering out safety risks were identified that otherwise would compro -
mise pedestrian worker safety. Particularly, these are brought into a 
quantifiable context of limited equipment operator visibility, equipment 
operating at higher speeds, and, among other recurring hazards related 
to on-site logistics, when moving material in overlapping work zones 
between the loading and unloading stations.
A broader finding of this study is how real-time data can help create 
safer work site layouts. Traditionally, safety planning is linear, design- 
based on pre-defined conditions, and seldom updated. However, by 
incorporating real-time data and analysis, this work emphasizes the 
need for a transformation towards a more circular approach, where 
safety plans are continuously refined based on actual site conditions. 
This then ensures that hazard zones, for example, once detected and 
updated in runtime, are protected as construction progresses.
However, this approach does have some limitations. It demands the 
use of advanced technologies, such as the proposed safeBIM and RTK- 
GNSS. These must work reliably together and within the proposed 
DTCS [ 44 ]. To reinforce the complexity of this thought, this study found 
that many incidents occurred when workers entered semi-static hazard 
zones , for example, where pedestrian and equipment pathways over -
lapped and when they were insufficiently separated from each other (e.

While this work made some positive findings, there are a few limi -
tations to note. The presented technical contributions show that 
advanced real-time location sensing and topographic survey technolo -
gies make it possible to quickly and accurately capture live spatial- 
temporal data of static and dynamic construction resources in multiple 
simulated and as-built construction environments. However, not all the 
observed proximity cases can be counted as close calls simply because 
the term safe distance has yet to be defined. Perhaps a standard is needed.
Future site recordings may want to consider human behavioral pat -
terns as well. Examples are safety literacy levels (e.g., how frequently 
workers receive safety hazard education and risk awareness training) 
and perhaps also consider workers ’ emotional states (e.g., daily 
feelings).
Despite the contributions of this study, several limitations must be 
acknowledged. First, the experiments were conducted over a limited 
time period in a realistic and yet controlled setting. This may restrict the 
generalizability of the findings to other construction work environ -
ments. Future research should expand the data collection period and 
investigate a larger variety of construction scenarios to account for more 
diverse operational conditions. Extending the testing scenarios to 
include other equipment, such as excavators or cranes, could provide a 
more comprehensive understanding.
Second, although the RTK-GNSS system provided high-precision 
location data, factors such as signal loss due to obstructions and in -
terferences in certain areas may affect data reliability and the DTCS ’ s 
runtime approach. In the case observed (the commercial RTK-GNSS

Fourth, as earlier research already pointed out [ 68 ], further studies

collection technology was simple to use, installed, and removed within a 
few minutes. Recharging the accompanying smartphone and operating 
software application to start and end the location tracking required no 
special expertise either. However, further advancements are necessary 
in the analysis of large data sets (ref. big data). The help of Artificial

needed in the fields of standards and benchmarking of historical data, 
such as defining terms and how erroneous data and uncertainty of the

Fifth and yet another very important aspect is related to research and 
work practice ethics and privacy rights. These need to follow guidelines 
according to work councils, Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), research 
ethics committees, and government mandated General Data Protection 
Regulations (GDPR), respectively, to protect human research partici -
pants. Participants in this study volunteered. No personal data was 
recorded and shared or otherwise anonymized. This is a limitation, as 
the experience and background of individuals contribute further insights 
into data analysis and reasoning.
6. Conclusions
Construction throughout the world and over the past decades ranks 
among the workplaces having the highest accident numbers. Of which, 
workers being struck by equipment often lead to Serious Injuries and Fa -
talities (SIF). The dynamic nature and high energy of equipment oper -
ations make it difficult to design out such hazards which makes 
continuous monitoring in the complex and dynamic, and especially 
shared, workplaces very much necessary. Empirical data show that 
control theory applied in construction safety management establishes a 
positive impact over time. However, the zero-accident goal has by far 
not been reached yet, partially due to the inefficient and insufficient 
responses that could be possible once hazards and incidents are identi -
fied and detected, respectfully.
From a high-level point of view, this paper showed how important 
data recordings of live operations on construction sites are, especially 
when combined with automated analysis and mitigative recommenda -
tions. This work provides effortless integration, much needed input 
values that – once analyzed – generate objective information with no or 
little human bias attached to the output values. The incorporation of the 
automated process and the gained information in the proposed Digital 
Twin for Construction Safety (DTCS) concept, and part thereof Right- 
time Mitigation and Alerting (RAM) module, creates knowledge based 
on performance checks once the change is implemented in the field. 
Applying the sequence of data-information-knowledge ultimately creates 
quicker and better alternatives for establishing safer work conditions.
On the practical implementation side, this paper highlighted the 
need for data sensing and communication technology (aka. IoT) in the 
construction environment. As shown in this work, simple-to-use IoT can 
assist in real-time safety monitoring of live construction site operations 
and provide rapidly adequate implementation of control measures, as 
needed. In particular, RTK-GNSS, as a high-accuracy RTLS, enables ac -
curate hazard identification and incident detection, together with the 
layout model contextualizing location data. On the basis, the developed 
automated computational algorithms analyzed relevant construction 
project site geometry and spatial, temporal, and kinematic characteris -
tics of various static or dynamic construction resources. These related to 
the available site safety and layout plans, volunteering pedestrian 
workers, and selected pieces of heavy construction equipment. The 
experimental work has also been qualitatively validated by comparing 
the results to manual observations and video recordings. The results 
demonstrate that the developed automation of previous manual efforts 
can accurately, consistently, and reliably detect and measure pedestrian 
workers ’ safety performance under the proximity hazards in the 
research scope. Furthermore, the algorithms automatically analyzed the 
overall site safety condition, making aware of hazard and incident 
hotspots, and accordingly recommends mitigative modifications to the 
site layout plan.
This work demonstrated that construction safety can be significantly 
enhanced by addressing two complementary levels: influencing indi -
vidual worker behavior and adapting construction site layout through 
automated analysis. Mitigation strategies should target both levels to 
effectively reduce risks and foster a more worker-oriented construction 
work environment.
