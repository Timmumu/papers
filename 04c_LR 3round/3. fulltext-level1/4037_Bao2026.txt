ABSTRACT
With the rapid integration of the Internet of Things (IoT), Big Data, and Artificial Intelligence (AI), tunnel en -
gineering is undergoing an accelerated shift toward intelligent construction and operation & maintenance 
(O & M). This paper systematically reviews the technological evolution of intelligent tunnel systems across design, 
construction, and operation & maintenance (O & M) phases. In the design stage, emphasis is placed on digital 
design tools and geological digital twins to enhance constructability. Intelligent perception, real-time risk 
analysis, and autonomous control jointly improve construction safety and efficiency. In the O & M phase, real-time 
adaptive monitoring combined with predictive maintenance ensures sustained tunnel performance and resil -
ience. Parallelly, the development of tunnel information platforms has transitioned from standalone systems to 
integrated, modular, and interoperable platforms that enable multi-source data fusion, collaborative manage -
ment, and real-time decision-making. This review highlights key enabling technologies, practical applications, 
and persistent bottlenecks, including data heterogeneity, cost-efficiency, and model generalization. Looking 
forward, intelligent platforms are expected to evolve into closed-loop ecosystems that combine real-time sensing, 
AI-based optimization, and digital twin co-simulation. These systems will also embed sustainability indicators 
such as carbon tracking, material recycling, and lifecycle assessment to support green decision-making. 
Collectively, the study provides a comprehensive roadmap for developing next-generation intelligent, resilient, 
and sustainable tunnel systems.
1. Introduction
With the rapid advancement of Internet technologies, the Internet of 
Things (IoT), and digital transformation, urban rail transit infrastructure 
is undergoing a new wave of intelligent upgrading. In the face of 
increasingly complex tunnel construction environments and rising de -
mands for efficient, safe, and sustainable engineering, the intelligenti -
zation of tunnel construction and O & M processes has become an 
inevitable trend. Rather than implying that tunnels themselves are 
‘intelligent ’ , this trend refers to the integration of intelligent sensing, big 
data analytics, and automated control technologies into LCA, from 
design and construction to O & M. Chen et al. ( Chen et al., 2022 ) and Zhu 
et al. ( Zhu et al., 2024 ) have jointly pointed out that the development of 
intelligent tunnel construction lies in the deep integration of informa -
tion technology with engineering systems, enabling full-process digital 
empowerment, intelligent sensing, and automated control to foster 
resilient and high-quality tunneling.
Recent studies have increasingly attempted to define the scope and 
technical foundation of ‘intelligent tunnel construction. ’ Qin ( Qin et al., 
2024b ) systematically summarized the key technological components, 
ranging from rock-machine perception to digital platform integration 
and proposed a framework that supports the evolution from traditional 
automation to full intelligentization, bridging tunnel construction and 
O & M phases. However, the O & M stage continues to face critical chal -
lenges, including heterogeneous data integration, real-time structural 
monitoring, equipment degradation, and the absence of robust decision-

Scholars have explored various approaches to advancing intelligent 
tunnel construction and O&M. Padgett ( Padgett et al., 2024 ) highlighted 
the need for dynamic, adaptive systems that address uncertainty while 
incorporating social equity considerations. Bai ( Bai et al., 2020 ) 
designed a three-tier architecture for intelligent O&M platforms in 
urban rail systems, integrating big data, BIM, and machine learning to 
enhance lifecycle management. In parallel, several review articles have 
analyzed intelligent technologies from specialized perspectives ( Chen 
et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Liu et al., 2024a; Xie et al., 2025a ). For 
instance, Chen et al. ( Chen et al., 2023b ) provided a comprehensive 
review of seismic resilience assessment frameworks for shield tunnels, 
outlining current research progress, challenges, and future directions in 
tunnel resilience modeling and performance-based design. Zhao et al. 
( Zhao et al., 2024c ) emphasized lifecycle-based digital twin frameworks 
for subway tunnels. Song et al. ( Song et al., 2024b ) examined digital 
early-warning platforms in mining, offering transferable insights for 
tunnel risk management. Liu et al. ( Liu et al., 2024a ) focused on AI 
integration and conducted a bibliometric analysis evaluating machine 
learning methods combined with multi-index evaluation frameworks, 
and Song et al. ( Song et al., 2025 ) systematically examine the applica -
tion progress of deep learning in tunnels and underground engineering 
across seven key areas. Rather than restricting attention to a single 
tunneling method, this review adopts a broad perspective that spans 
shield tunnels, drill-and-blast tunnels, mining tunnels, immersed tun -
nels, and urban utility tunnels serving transportation and infrastructure 
needs. Drawing on publications indexed in the Web of Science (WOS) 
over the past five years, this paper provides a comprehensive synthesis 
of recent progress.
This review systematically examines technological advances in 
intelligent tunnel engineering, focusing on sensing, data-driven 
modeling, automated control, real-time risk warning, and integrated 
platform development. By clarifying the role of intelligent technologies 
across the full tunnel lifecycle, covering design, construction, and O&M, 
it provides a holistic perspective that links technological evolution, 
platform integration, and sustainable development. Through this 
lifecycle-oriented framework, the review not only assesses current 
progress but also highlights key challenges and outlines future di -
rections. The overall technical framework is illustrated in Fig. 1 .
2. Key technologies for intelligent tunnel construction and O&M
Tunnel structures are subjected to long-term influences from 
geological evolution, traffic loads, and environmental factors, which 
may lead to deterioration, cracks ( Xu et al., 2025 ), settlement ( Song 
et al., 2024a ), and other structural problems. Scientific and technical 
inspections and assessments are essential throughout the tunnel lifecycle 
to ensure safety. During the design phase, tunnel intelligence platform 
offers accurate data support and optimization solutions through 
advanced geological prediction technologies and structural visualiza -
tion tools. In the construction phase, it ensures safety and quality control 
( Ma et al., 2024 ) through real-time data acquisition, risk identification, 
and intelligent decision-making technologies ( Zhang et al., 2025a ). In 
the O&M phase, the platform acts as a unified center for integrating 
data, facilitating visualization, and supporting intelligent analytics. 
Drawing on digital twin frameworks and AI-driven analytics, intelligent 
platforms support long-term structural performance evaluation, surface 
defect detection, and predictive maintenance decision-making, and also 
predicts potential risks with high accuracy and provides targeted 
maintenance strategies ( Zhang et al., 2024c; Zhou et al., 2024a ). By 
applying these technologies, intelligent O&M through the lifecycle of 
tunnels can be achieved. This approach significantly enhances opera -
tional efficiency and safety, reduces the potential risks, extends tunnel 
utilization, and provides vital technical support for the construction of 
smart and resilient cities.
In this section, the classification of intelligent technologies into 
design, construction, and O&M phases is based on their primary func -
tional objectives and typical temporal application within the tunnel 
lifecycle.
2.1. Application of key technologies in the tunnel design phase
Traditional tunnel design has largely depended on two-dimensional 
drawings and static documentation, which limits integration with dy -
namic, data-driven workflows and constrains efficiency, precision, and 
adaptability in modern tunnel engineering. To overcome these short -
comings, intelligent design and analysis systems have emerged, enabling 
the integration of geological surveys, structural performance re -
quirements, and environmental constraints within advanced computa -
tional frameworks. Coupled with 3D visualization interfaces, these 
systems support performance-based and inverse design approaches and 
promote seamless transitions from digital models to construction plan -
ning and operation and maintenance. This section reviews the devel -
opment of intelligent technologies in the tunnel design phase, with 
emphasis on structural visualization and advanced geological predic -
tion. Representative studies were synthesized based on a systematic 
search of Web of Science using keywords such as ‘intelligent design’, 
‘structural visualization, and ‘geological prediction’.
2.1.1. Structural visualization
Structural visualization during the tunnel design phase supports 
designers, engineers, and other stakeholders in intuitively understand -
ing and effectively communicating the spatial configuration, functional 
characteristics, and potential challenges associated with tunnel struc -
tures. With the advancement of intelligent construction technologies, 
visualization methods have evolved beyond static 3D representations, 
becoming integrated platforms for multi-source data fusion, predictive 
analysis, and proactive risk management.
Building Information Modeling (BIM) has become a central tool in 
the digital transformation of tunnel infrastructure, offering a unified 
platform to represent both physical structures and functional systems. 
Widely adopted in the design phases of large-scale metro and highway 
tunnel projects in China and abroad, BIM enables the development of

their deployment remains largely limited to pilot or research projects. 
Key challenges include the reliance on high-quality monitoring data and 
difficulties in integrating heterogeneous sources into BIM systems. 
Moreover, the scalability of such predictive applications to large tunnel 
networks and real-time O & M scenarios remains limited.
In addition to AI-based predictive modeling and risk visualization, 
BIM platforms have also been increasingly integrated with physics- 
based numerical simulations to support dynamic decision-making dur -
ing tunnel construction. These simulation approaches have been 
increasingly integrated into BIM platforms, enabling synchronized 
model updates and real-time data visualization ( Cheng et al., 2025 ). 
Current practices have also incorporated 3D geological models, 
geotechnical parameters, and tunnel lining design details into BIM sys -
tems. Through advanced parametric modeling and geometric recon -

2.1.2. Advanced geological prediction
Advanced geological prediction during the design phase leverages 
modern exploration techniques such as ground-penetrating radar ( Xin, 
2017 ), acoustic wave surveys ( Liu et al., 2023a ), seismic reflection im -
aging ( Aoki et al., 2007 ) and borehole drilling ( Fang et al., 2021 ). These 
methods yield critical subsurface data, including stratigraphy, geo -
mechanically properties, hydrogeological conditions, and geological 
discontinuities. Such information supports the identification of weak 
zones, prediction of adverse geological features ahead of the excavation 
face, assessment of ground deformation trends, and estimation of 
seismic response behaviors. By integrating multi-source datasets, accu -
rate 3D geological models can be constructed, forming the basis for 
selecting appropriate tunneling methods, designing support systems, 
and mitigating geological risks, as illustrated in Fig. 4 . To enhance 
geological section prediction in scenarios with sparse borehole data, Qiu 
( Qiu et al., 2024 ) proposed a multi-spatial receptive field (MSRF) 
XGBoost model in Fig. 5 . This model combines classification and 
recognition modules with multi-scale spatial feature extraction and 
automated model selection mechanisms, thereby improving robustness 
in complex geological settings.
In addition to data-driven prediction, a range of 3D geological 
modeling approaches have been explored to address limited and het -
erogeneous subsurface data. Cooperative kriging methods fuse multi- 
source inputs to improve the representation of complex formations 
and enable dynamic model updates ( Zhang and Zhu, 2018 ). Data-driven 
frameworks such as IC-XGBoost3D combine sparse borehole records 
with 2D geological images to construct 3D models while quantifying 
uncertainty ( Shi and Wang, 2022 ). Multiscale modeling techniques 
further integrate field reconnaissance, drilling, and geological fore -
casting for construction support ( Xiong et al., 2018 ). Improved coupled 
Markov chain (CMC) methods, as shown in Fig. 6 , enhance lateral 
stratigraphic simulation by integrating Walther ’ s Law with vertical and 
intermediate transition matrices. This approach reduces horizontal 
transition probability errors to under 3 % and remains robust across 
varying borehole layouts. ( Zhang et al., 2022a ).
Collectively, these approaches address major challenges in 3D 
geological modeling by improving prediction accuracy, enabling adap -
tive construction-oriented modeling, and enhancing the fidelity of 
complex stratigraphic simulations. Nevertheless, advanced prediction 
methods still face critical limitations, including restricted generaliz -
ability in heterogeneous geology, high computational demands, and 
strong reliance on data quality and coverage. The intrinsic complexity of 
subsurface conditions continues to hinder accurate site reconstruction. 
Future research should prioritize improving model scalability and 
robustness, coupling with real-time sensing, and advancing physics- 
informed machine learning to achieve reliable and practical pre -
dictions under complex and dynamic underground conditions.

2.2.1. Data acquisition
Real-time data acquisition provides the foundation for intelligent 
decision-making and automated control in tunnel construction. Key data 
streams include: (i) environmental parameters such as temperature, 
humidity, gas concentration, and particulate matter, which ensure safe 
working conditions in enclosed underground settings;( Jha and Tukkar -
aja, 2020; Mu ˜niz et al., 2023; Weng et al., 2022 ) (ii) structural response 
data (displacement, stress, strain) that reflect the stability of surround -
ing rock and tunnel linings during excavation and initial support ( Ling 
et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2020 ); and (iii) progress-related indicators such 
as construction stage, equipment runtime, and material consumption, 
which enable schedule tracking and resource management.( Xu et al., 
2023a ) Multi-source sensing systems integrating these datasets have 
already been validated in metro and highway tunnel projects, demon -
strating effectiveness in early warning, real-time risk assessment, and 
adaptive control. As shown in Fig. 7 , such systems form the backbone of 
intelligent construction monitoring platforms and provide transferable 
methodologies adaptable to other large-scale underground engineering 
projects.
A critical element of data acquisition in tunnel construction is the 
real-time monitoring of shield tunnelling parameters such as propulsion 
force, cutterhead speed, torque, and chamber pressure. These parame -
ters, captured by embedded sensor arrays, are vital for detecting 
abnormal conditions, enabling adaptive control, and mitigating risks 
including ground settlement and segmental deformation. Recent ad -
vances highlight three development trends: (i) enhanced sensor preci -
sion, durability, and fault tolerance for reliable monitoring in harsh 
underground environments; (ii) the adoption of edge computing and 5G 
communication to accelerate data transmission and support real-time 
early warning and closed-loop control; and (iii) the integration of AI, 
machine learning, and big data analytics to optimize tunnelling pa -
rameters and guide operational decisions. These technologies have been 
successfully applied in metro and cross-river shield tunnel projects, 
demonstrating their practical value and scalability across different 
geological settings. A summary of representative developments is pro -
vided in Table 1 .
Building on the multi-source data acquisition discussed above, 
effective data utilization requires further processing and optimization 
through integrated algorithmic frameworks. These algorithms encom -
pass data cleaning, noise filtering, and data fusion technologies, which 
collectively enhance data accuracy, consistency, and reliability. Addi -
tionally, by combining advanced technologies such as machine learning 
and artificial intelligence with both historical and temporal data, it be -
comes possible to dynamically predict shield machine behavior and 
ground responses, optimize tunnelling parameters such as propulsion 
force and cutterhead torque, and support adaptive control to ensure 
construction efficiency and safety. Representative application cases of 
these integrated technologies are summarized in Table 2 .

encounter significant challenges under complex and dynamic under -

2.2.2. Risk assessment
Building intelligent monitoring and risk assessment is a core 
component of shield tunnel construction, systematically evaluating the 
probability and consequences of potential risks to support hazard 
control and risk-informed decision-making. Given the dynamic and 
uncertain nature of underground conditions, robust frameworks 
improve the reliability of construction strategies across varying sce -
narios. In practice, tunnel projects commonly classify risks into four 
categories: project-inherent, geological, environmental, as shown in 
Fig. 8 , which have proven valuable for identifying critical risk sources 
and prioritizing mitigation measures.
To support effective risk assessment in shield tunnel construction, 
diverse approaches have been applied to identify, evaluate, and manage 
construction-related risks. Common methods include expert surveys, 
fault tree analysis (FTA), event tree analysis (ETA), and machine 
learning models, which together enable both qualitative and quantita -
tive evaluation of hazards and guide mitigation strategies. According to 
the ‘Code for risk management of underground works in urban rail 
transit ’ ( Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the 
People ’ s Republic of China, 2011 ), assessment methodologies in rail 
transit construction are generally categorized into qualitative, quanti -
tative, and semi-quantitative types, as summarized in Table 3 .
The tunnel construction phase is exposed to diverse and dynamic 
risks, including differential settlement, water and mud inrush, tunnel 
face instability, and disturbances from adjacent activities. These hazards 
are marked by high uncertainty, hidden evolution, and sudden onset, 
which challenge traditional empirical judgment and static monitoring.

Recent advances in AI, IoT, big data analytics, and digital twin systems 
have transformed risk identification, assessment, and management 
practices in tunneling. The following section reviews the development 
and application of these technologies in addressing four critical cate -
gories of construction risks.
(1) Uneven settlement of soil.
Uneven soil settlement during tunnel construction threatens both 
construction safety and the stability of overlying infrastructure. Recent 
advances show that machine learning (ML) techniques significantly 
outperform empirical and statistical models in predicting settlement 
patterns. Supervised algorithms such as RF, XGBoost, SVM, and LSTM 
have been applied to shield tunnelling projects, effectively combining 
operational parameters, geotechnical data, and monitoring records to 
improve prediction accuracy and adaptability ( Wang et al., 2024d; Ye 
et al., 2022 ). Hybrid soft computing approaches that integrate fuzzy 
logic with neural networks further capture the nonlinear and uncertain 
nature of soil-structure interactions in EPB tunnelling ( Zhang et al., 
2024b; Zhang et al., 2021c ). In addition, probabilistic risk evaluation 
frameworks have been introduced to incorporate data uncertainty and 
construction variability into settlement risk metrics, supporting more 
reliable decision-making under uncertainty ( Chen et al., 2024b ).
Complementing predictive models, remote sensing and multi-source 
monitoring techniques enhance spatial and temporal coverage. InSAR 
using Sentinel-1 imagery has been successfully employed in dense urban 
areas to detect tunnelling-induced deformation ( Liu et al., 2023b ). At 
the local scale, distributed optical fiber sensors such as BOTDA, inte -
grated into IoT frameworks, enable continuous high-frequency moni -
toring and early warning of anomalous settlement. Furthermore, BIM- 
GIS integration provides intuitive spatial visualization of risk zones 
and improves information sharing across project stakeholders. Collec -
tively, these developments illustrate a shift from reactive monitoring to 
predictive and proactive settlement management, with demonstrated 
value in metro and urban tunnel projects.
(2) Tunnel water and mud inrush.
Water and mud inrush represent one of the most severe hazards in 
shield tunnelling, typically triggered by face instability or sudden 
groundwater intrusion. Intelligent technologies have greatly advanced 
risk identification and early warning. Machine learning models, 
including ensemble methods and SVM, have shown high predictive ac -
curacy under complex geological conditions ( Mahmoodzadeh et al., 
2021 ). Multi-criteria decision-making frameworks such as FAHP-I- 
TOPSIS and uncertainty theory provide structured evaluation of key 
indicators including permeability, water pressure, and geological 
anomalies ( Dou et al., 2023; Kong and Zhang, 2024; Zhao et al., 2024b ), 
supporting both qualitative and quantitative decision-making, as shown 
in Fig. 9 .
To better capture uncertainty, multi-source geophysical data com -
bined with improved Dempster-Shafer evidence theory enables proba -
bilistic identification of potential inrush zones ( Ding et al., 2022 ).

(3) Collapse of the tunnel.
Tunnel face collapse is a persistent challenge in underground con -
struction, especially in karst zones, faulted strata, and weak rock masses. 
Recent studies demonstrate the value of intelligent modeling and 
probabilistic evaluation for risk identification and control. For example, 
Yang et al. ( Yang et al., 2023b ) developed a hybrid random forest-based 
model incorporating metaheuristic optimization to forecast shield- 
induced ground settlement in Singapore, achieving high accuracy 
across three real EPB projects. Similarly, Wang et al. ( Wang et al., 
2024d ) employed a PSO – BP neural network calibrated with monitoring 
data from a metro project, demonstrating strong agreement with field- 
observed settlement profiles. These studies affirm the practical poten -
tial of data-driven approaches in early warning and control of tunnel 
face instability.
Advanced algorithms further support early warning and probabilistic 
assessment. PSO-optimized LSTM models capture nonlinear settlement 
trends near tunnel portals ( Sun et al., 2025b ), and SVR combined with 
Monte Carlo simulation quantifies collapse probability under uncer -
tainty ( Meng et al., 2023 ). These data-driven approaches improve risk 
quantification based on construction monitoring data and geological 
features, facilitating targeted mitigation. Complementary methods such 
as cloud models, Bayesian networks with self-learning capacity, and 
entropy-weighted clustering ( Ju et al., 2024 ; Sun et al., 2024 ) allow 
dynamic refinement of risk classification, while hybrid frameworks 
incorporating extension theory have been applied in karst terrains to 
address spatial variability ( Zhang et al., 2022b ), as shown in Fig. 10 .
Expert knowledge systems, including analytical hierarchy process 
(AHP) and Delphi methods, have been increasingly integrated with 
quantitative models to enhance interpretability and practical adoption, 
and information fusion techniques such as enhanced Dempster-Shafer 
theory improve robustness under ambiguous or sparse data ( Huang 
et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2022 ). Collectively, these developments signify a 
critical shift from traditional experience-based evaluation toward data- 
integrated, adaptive, and interpretable frameworks for tunnel collapse 
risk prediction, ultimately enabling more resilient and responsive con -
struction practices.
(4) Disturbance of adjacent buildings.
Shield tunnelling in dense urban areas can induce deformation and 
damage in adjacent buildings and underground structures, making 
proximity disturbance a critical external risk. To address the limitations 
of traditional assessment methods, which often overlook dynamic 
weight variation and uncertainty, recent studies have introduced 
advanced probabilistic and fuzzy frameworks. For example, risk 
assessment models combining variable-weight theory with cloud 
modeling flexibly capture fuzziness and randomness in disturbance 
scenarios ( Guo et al., 2024 ), while improved FAHP approaches inte -
grating game-theoretic weighting and nonlinear cloud models ( Guo 
et al., 2025 ), enhance the identification of dominant risk factors, as 
shown in Fig. 11 . The developed model was applied to assess shield 
tunneling risks near buildings along Changsha Metro Line 4, identifying 
‘medium ’ to ‘high ’ risk levels. The results aligned well with actual site 
conditions, validating the model ’ s reliability.
In addition, hybrid models have also been developed to support 
dynamic and probabilistic evaluations. A representative example is the 
integration of the Trapezium Cloud Model (TCM) with Bayesian Net -

2.2.3. Parametric prediction
Accurate forecasting of key operational parameters is central to 
intelligent shield tunneling, ensuring construction continuity and 
structural safety. The tunneling process is affected by geological con -
ditions, equipment performance, this section focuses on three high- 
impact tasks: (i) prediction of cutter wear, supporting predictive main -

prediction of shield attitude control, enabling feed corrections through 
sequence models that map ground conditions and operational inputs to 
deviation risk; and (iii) prediction of shield muck identification, classi -

required data, engineering maturity, and scalability considerations.
(1) Prediction of shield cutter plate wearing.
Cutter wear is a critical parameter in shield tunnelling, as excessive 
degradation leads to downtime, higher maintenance costs, and reduced 
excavation efficiency. Accurate prediction supports optimized tool- 
change planning and continuous progress. With advances in machine 
learning, models such as LSTM ( Wang et al., 2024b ), gene expression 
programming (GEP) ( Tao et al., 2022 ), ensemble learning, and 
attention-based architectures ( Zhang et al., 2023b ) have been applied 
using torque, thrust, penetration rate, and geological indices, demon -
strating strong predictive performance across diverse strata ( Li et al., 
2023b ; Mo et al., 2024; Shin et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a ), as shown 
in Fig. 12 . Hybrid approaches that integrate physical indicators such as 
the Cerchar Abrasivity Index ( Sun et al., 2023 ) and energy-based fea -
tures ( Wang et al., 2012 ) enhance generalization, while frameworks 
combining sensor data with mechanical models improve robustness 
under complex geological conditions ( Elbaz et al., 2021 ). Kwon et al. 
( Kwon et al., 2025 ) have developed an interpretable XGBoost model 
with data augmentation to predict abnormal cutter wear under complex 
mixed ground conditions. Trained on 65 CHI reports, the model ach -
ieved a recall of 0.80 and an F1-score of 0.808. Through model inter -
pretation, key influencing factors, such as chamber pressure, cutter 
installation radius, and torque were identified. The incorporation of 
equivalent elastic modulus and class-balancing strategies effectively 
addressed geological variability and data imbalance, providing valuable 
insights into the mechanisms of abnormal wear.
However, most models remain project-specific, with limited trans -
ferability across different strata, and face challenges from sensor noise, 
data heterogeneity, and computational demands. Moreover, the black- 
box nature of deep learning restricts acceptance in field operations. 
Future research should emphasize cross-project benchmark datasets, 
multi-source fusion, and hybrid data-mechanics frameworks to enable 
robust, interpretable, and scalable cutter wear prediction, as already 
piloted in metro and cross-river tunnel projects.
(2) Prediction of shield attitude control.
Shield attitude control refers to predicting and adjusting the ma -

alignment accuracy and tunnel geometry. Traditional deterministic 
methods lack adaptability under complex conditions, prompting the

records with real-time sensor data. Hybrid deep learning models with 
attention mechanisms have proven effective in capturing nonlinear re -
lationships between geology, operational parameters, and posture ( Dai 
et al., 2023; Kang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025b ), while Bayesian-based 
models such as Bayesian-LGBM improve robustness through explicit 
uncertainty quantification ( Chen et al., 2023a ; Wang et al., 2024c ). 
Dimensionality reduction techniques like PCA further stabilize training 
in high-dimensional tunneling datasets ( Zhang et al., 2025b ).

tunneling parameters with muck physical features for real-time classi -

project benchmark datasets covering diverse geological, structural, 
and operational conditions to enhance robustness and support general -

monitoring techniques such as remote sensing, ground-penetrating 
radar, and fiber Bragg grating-based systems, to provide comprehen -
sive excavation feedback and improve prediction reliability.

2.3. Application of key technologies in the O & M phase of tunnels
Ensuring the long-term safety and reliability of tunnels during 
operation and maintenance (O & M) is essential for the overall resilience 
of underground infrastructure. Potential defects and degradation high -
light the need for early fault detection and proactive maintenance 
strategies. Recent advances in inspection and monitoring technologies, 
particularly real-time sensing, dynamic condition assessment, and early 
warning systems play a pivotal role in identifying risks and enabling 
timely intervention. This section reviews representative studies on 
tunnel O & M, synthesizing progress in defect detection, continuous 
monitoring, and intelligent maintenance technologies, based on 
literature retrieved with keywords such as ‘tunnel operation ’ , ‘defects 
detection ’ , ‘real-time monitoring ’ , and ‘maintenance technologies ’ 
across Web of Science.
2.3.1. Defects detection
During the O & M phase, tunnel structures are increasingly exposed to 
long-term environmental and mechanical effects, making them prone to 
defects such as cracks, water leakage, segment dislocation, and cross- 
section convergence. These anomalies not only degrade structural per -
formance but also threaten operational safety, especially in deep-buried 
tunnels subject to high stress and hydrostatic pressure. Traditional 
manual inspections often suffer from poor visibility, subjective judg -
ment, and incomplete coverage, highlighting the need for intelligent and 
automated inspection technologies. Recent advances in this domain can 
be summarized in three major directions: (i) crack detection, focusing on 
surface and structural crack recognition; (ii) leakage detection, targeting

(1) Crack detection
Cracks in tunnel linings are key indicators of structural deterioration 
and durability loss, often reflecting stress concentrations, fatigue, or 
construction defects. Their timely detection is critical for operational 
safety and effective maintenance. Recent years have witnessed a rapid 
shift from manual inspection to deep learning – based computer vision, 
which offers higher accuracy, automation, and scalability. Object 
detection models built on YOLO variants (YOLOv5, Mini-YOLOv5s, 
YOLOvX) have shown strong performance in real-time crack localiza -
tion, with refinements for small-object detection and computational 
efficiency ( Duan et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2024a; Zhou et al., 2023b; Xu

enhanced DeepLabV3 + , Retinex-based networks, and GAN-augmented

Wu and Zhang, 2023; Zhou et al., 2023c ). In addition to surface-level 
crack detection, Bao et al. (2025) proposed a transfer learning – based 
YOLOv8 framework for identifying internal voids within reinforced 
concrete segments. By addressing the challenge of steel bar interference, 
their method offers an effective solution for concealed defect recogni -
tion in shield tunnel linings.
To enhance robustness, ensemble learning and multi-sensor fusion 
approaches integrate outputs from multiple models or combine imagery 
with auxiliary spatial information. Examples include mobile imaging 
platforms coupled with ensemble networks for automated inspections 
and SLAM-assisted 3D reconstruction of crack distributions, as shown in 
Fig. 16 ( Xu et al., 2024a ). System-level implementations such as robotic 
platforms equipped with onboard detection algorithms have further 
enabled routine, autonomous tunnel inspections ( Xu et al., 2025 ). 
System-level implementations of intelligent inspection tools have been 
developed to support autonomous tunnel maintenance operations. Ro -
botic platforms equipped with temporal crack detection algorithms and 
onboard crack detection algorithms were deployed for routine in -
spections ( Qin et al., 2023 ). Pilot applications in metro tunnels have 
demonstrated the feasibility and scalability of these intelligent inspec -
tion solutions under real-world operating conditions ( Brien et al., 2023; 
Lei et al., 2021 ).
(2) Leakage detection
Water leakage threatens tunnel durability and service safety, 
particularly in aging or heavily loaded structures. Recent work has 
moved from manual inspection to automated pipelines that couple vi -
sual sensing, 3D reconstruction, and deep learning. Feature-enhanced 
learning improves the perception of weak, thin, and reflective leakage 
patterns under adverse lighting ( Wang et al., 2024a ). Semantic seg -
mentation models, such as Mask R-CNN and improved variants have 
shown robust performance on lining images and point-cloud data ( Liu 
et al., 2024b; Wang et al., 2024e ). To achieve spatially consistent 
localization, 3D voxel modeling and Structure-from-Motion (SfM) are 
integrated with CNNs to recover leakage geometry and map defect 
extent along the tunnel axis ( Xue et al., 2022 ), as shown in Fig. 17 .
Beyond single-model detectors, hybrid frameworks that combine 
classical image morphology (edge extraction, dilation) with CNNs 
improve boundary fidelity at joints and segment interfaces ( Ma et al., 
2025 ). To enhance engineering credibility, visual explanation tools 
(CAM/Grad-CAM) are embedded to expose attention regions and sup -
port result auditing ( Feng et al., 2023 ). Field deployments in metro O & M 
have verified feasibility for routine inspection; remaining challenges 
include illumination variability, water-film glare, and domain shift 
across lines. Priorities include standardized image acquisition, cross- 
project benchmarks, and tighter coupling with geometric reconstruc -
tion to strengthen generalization and maintenance decision-making.
(3) Multi-disease detection and identification
Tunnel structures often exhibit multiple coexisting defects such as 
cracks, seepage, and lining deterioration, whose combined effects pose 
greater risks than isolated anomalies. Recent studies employ image- 
based deep learning to simultaneously detect and segment diverse sur -
face defects in complex subway environments ( Huang et al., 2018a; Xu 
et al., 2023b ), as shown in Fig. 18 . Vision Transformer (ViT)-based ar -
chitectures further improve segmentation accuracy by capturing long- 
range spatial dependencies, while the integration of ground- 
penetrating radar (GPR) with neural networks enables the identifica -
tion of hidden subsurface defects beyond the reach of conventional vi -
sual inspection ( Qin et al., 2024a ) ( Zhao et al., 2024a ).
Multi-source data fusion has emerged as a key strategy to enhance 
robustness, combining imagery, GPR signals, and construction logs into 
unified pipelines ( Gan et al., 2024 ), as shown in Fig. 19 . Two-stage 
frameworks that first perform coarse classification followed by fine- 
grained segmentation further improve localization and characteriza -
tion accuracy ( Feng et al., 2024a ). Collectively, these approaches extend 
tunnel disease assessment from single-defect detection toward compre -
hensive, multi-modal, and system-level evaluation, supporting more 
reliable O & M decision-making.
This section reviewed recent advances in defect detection technolo -
gies for tunnel O & M, covering cracks, leakage, and multi-disease iden -
tification. These methods have greatly enhanced the accuracy and 
efficiency of tunnel condition assessment, and integrated systems now 
enable simultaneous detection of multiple defect types. Nevertheless, 
challenges persist, including label ambiguity, inter-class feature overlap, 
reliance on large-scale annotated datasets, and limited generalizability 
across different tunnel types. These issues continue to constrain the 
robustness and scalability of current models in real-world monitoring 
applications.

3.1. Single function architecture
The earliest digital platforms in tunnel engineering were character -
ized by a single-function architecture, designed primarily to support 
isolated tasks within specific phases of a project, such as data acquisi -
tion, equipment status monitoring, or progress visualization. These 
systems typically operated in silos and lacked interoperability and data 
integration, resulting in fragmented workflows and inefficiencies across 
the tunnel lifecycle. This stage aligns with the initial phase of BIM 
evolution, described by Brki ´c as the ‘lonely BIM ’ era, where digital tools 
served individual disciplines without cross-domain collaboration or in -
tegrated workflows ( Borkowski, 2023 ). Essentially, these platforms 
functioned as digitized replicas of manual processes, limited to static 
modeling or documentation-oriented tasks.
In tunnel engineering, typical examples include standalone platforms 
for monitoring ground settlement, logging TBM data, or documenting 
inspections. For instance, Bossi ( Bossi et al., 2017 ) developed a 
specialized monitoring system for structural health assessment of a road 
tunnel intersecting an active landslide, focusing exclusively on localized 
deformation and strain data. Similarly, Qin ( Qin, 2019 ) proposed a 
dedicated monitoring platform for submarine cable tunnels, which 
applied data mining techniques to support operational decision-making. 
However, these platforms remained confined to single-domain 
applications and lacked integration with broader tunnel life-cycle 
management frameworks. While effective within their specific scopes, 
they were not designed for interoperability, data sharing, or system- 
level coordination, thereby limiting their value in holistic tunnel man -
agement. Despite their limitations, single-function platforms served as a 
critical role for subsequent digital development. They facilitated the 
initial introduction of digital tools into engineering workflows, high -
lighted the inefficiencies of fragmented and non-interoperable systems, 
and ultimately catalyzed the shift toward integrated-function and 
intelligent platform architectures.
3.2. Integrated function architecture
Driven by the growing demand for efficient and holistic tunnel 
management, the architecture of tunnel platforms has progressively 
evolved from single-function systems to integrated-function frame -
works. This transformation has been facilitated by the convergence of 
enabling technologies, such as cloud computing, big data analytics, and 
IoT, which collectively support cross-domain integration and intelligent 
decision-making. While these technologies address different functional 
needs, their integration substantially enhances the overall performance 
of tunnel management platforms by breaking down data silos and 
enabling interoperability across construction and O & M stages. Through 
the adoption of unified data standards and advanced integration in -
terfaces, platforms can now seamlessly link shield tunnelling parame -
ters, geological data, monitoring information, and structural health 
data, creating a unified and comprehensive data ecosystem that supports 
collaborative operations and informed decision-making.

4. Challenges and prospects
4.1. Challenges
Intelligent tunnel construction technologies and O & M platforms 
have achieved significant advancements, particularly in multi-source 
data acquisition, real-time monitoring, risk forecasting, and intelligent 
decision support. However, with the rapid expansion of tunnel engi -
neering, especially the development of deep and complex underground 
spaces in urban areas, the growing need for life-cycle maintenance of 
aging infrastructure, and the increased emphasis on low-carbon and 
sustainable construction, the limitations of conventional technologies 
and management approaches have become increasingly evident. These 
traditional often fall short in addressing the challenges posed by high- 
risk geological conditions, intricate engineering scenarios, and 
evolving safety and efficiency requirements. This gap highlights the 
urgent need for integrated, adaptive, and intelligent solutions capable of 
supporting resilient tunnel construction and operation under dynamic 
and uncertain conditions. 
(1) The lack of effective data integration and interoperability re -
mains a major barrier to intelligent tunnel management. The 
tunnel construction and O & M process generates large volumes of 
heterogeneous data, including geological surveys, construction 
monitoring, and equipment operation records, which often exist 
in incompatible formats and lack standardized interfaces. As a 
result, integrating and sharing these data across systems becomes 
difficult, reducing the effectiveness of intelligent tools, particu -
larly for real-time decision-making and cross-phase data conti -
nuity. A case study ( Feng et al., 2024b ) from the Changsha Metro 
Line 4 demonstrated how an AIoT-based system effectively inte -
grated machine parameters and settlement data to support pre -
dictive control. However, the system ’ s performance was highly 
dependent on localized datasets, and the machine learning 
models required retraining before use in other tunnel projects, 
exposing the broader limitations in data standardization and 
transferability.
(2) The current level of intelligence embedded in tunnel construction 
and O & M platforms is still insufficient to meet real-world de -
mands. Despite the integration of automation, AI, and IoT tech -
nologies, there remains a clear gap between research progress 
and field application. AI-based prediction models are constrained 
by limited training data and often struggle to maintain accuracy 
in highly variable geological conditions. For example, deforma -
tion prediction models ( Sauer and Fischer, 2013 ) trained on 
small-scale datasets require frequent retraining to remain effec -
tive in different tunneling scenarios. Similarly, although digital 
twin systems hold promise, they are hampered by slow model 
updates and latency, which limit their utility for real-time con -
trol. This becomes especially critical under extreme or emergency 
conditions, where delayed system responses may compromise

prerequisites for the broader adoption of intelligent construction

limiting their ability to support safety – critical decision-making 
and reliable tunnel operation.

generalization capability of AI models hinder the practical scal -

and deep learning have made notable strides in tunnel engi -

(5) In addition to technical challenges, sustainable and low-carbon 
construction practices in tunneling require further quantifica -

resource reutilization and green soil conditioning. For instance, 
Wu et al. ( Wu et al., 2024a ) reported in-situ trials on the Suzhou

partially replace synchronous grouting materials. The modified 
grout achieved comparable mechanical strength while reducing

another case, Wang et al. ( Wang et al., 2024f ) investigated 
bentonite-silty clay modified slurry using field samples from the 
Shenyang Metro Line 6, deriving quantitative rheological 
thresholds (yield stress ≈ 120 Pa, slump ≈ 18 cm) under different 
injection ratios. These studies provide measurable indicators such 
as spoil reuse percentage, rheological performance ranges, and 
optimized injection ratios, offering engineering guidelines for 
sustainable tunneling practices. Nevertheless, wider imple -
mentation is constrained by the absence of standardized carbon 
accounting frameworks and limited demonstration projects. 
Future work should therefore establish lifecycle-based carbon 
footprint models and develop benchmark systems supported by 
policy incentives to promote large-scale adoption.
4.2. Prospects
In future, the intelligent platforms will evolve into integrated, closed- 
loop ecosystems that directly support tunnel engineering management 
strategies. By combining real-time sensing, adaptive control, and pre -
dictive analytics with digital twin co-simulation, these platforms will not 
only enhance technical performance but also enable data-driven O & M 
strategies, resource optimization, and emergency coordination. 
Furthermore, the embedding of low-carbon indicators, such as carbon 
footprint tracking, material recycling, and lifecycle sustainability 
assessment will allow managers to incorporate environmental objectives 
into routine decision-making. From an industrial perspective, the 
establishment of standardized data protocols, interoperable modular 
platforms, and open-source toolchains will lower the barriers to adop -
tion and promote cross-project management integration. Collectively, 
these developments illustrate how intelligent platforms will facilitate 
smarter, greener, and more effective engineering management in tunnel 
construction and O & M.
5. Conclusion
This review systematically examined the evolution of intelligent 
technologies and platforms across the full life cycle of shield tunnel 
engineering, encompassing design, construction, and operation & 
maintenance stages. The study not only highlights the progress in 
automation and digitalization but also identifies current limitations and 
outlines future research opportunities to promote resilient and sustain -
able tunnel systems. Key conclusions are as follows: 
(1) Intelligent technologies have been increasingly integrated across 
the full lifecycle of shield tunneling. They are now widely applied 
in the design, construction, and operation phases, forming a 
closed-loop system that encompasses parametric modeling, 
automated construction, intelligent sensing, and preemptive 
response. These technologies demonstrate strong coordination 
between stages and provide a solid foundation for achieving 
digital and refined lifecycle management of tunnel infrastructure.
(2) Tunnel management platforms are evolving from simple data 
integration functions, such as visualization and archiving, to 
intelligent systems capable of real-time analysis, decision- 
making, and coordinated control. Supported by cloud-edge 
collaboration, BIM, and IoT technologies, these platforms 
enable dynamic data fusion and provide unified management 
across all phases of tunnel projects.
(3) Lifecycle-intelligent tunnel systems still face a series of key en -
gineering challenges related to data integration, algorithm 
generalization, real-time responsiveness, platform security, and 
sustainable implementation. Fragmented and non-standardized 
data constrain cross-phase interoperability, limiting the effec -
tiveness of digital tools in supporting continuous lifecycle man -
agement. Although AI models have shown strong potential, most 
remain project-specific with limited transferability across varying 
geological strata, and they often suffer from sensor noise, data 
heterogeneity, and high computational demands. These factors 
undermine the robustness and scalability of intelligent systems in

carbon, and synergistic subsystems that underpin smart and 
sustainable cities.
