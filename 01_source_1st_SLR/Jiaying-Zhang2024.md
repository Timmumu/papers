## Abstract ^sec-abstract

> Heritage building facades play a fundamental testimonial role in cities and society by contributing to the townscape, character of
the built environment, and community identity [1]. It also provides attractive and unique environments for commercial activities [2]. The typical parameters of air quality (AQ) for heritage buildings include temperature, relative humidity (RH) and air pollutants [3,4]. Most heritage buildings that have been renovated for commercial use are located in the prosperous parts of the city where they are exposed to air pollutants emitted from vehicles. Corrosive gases in polluted air, especially sulfide gas (SO2), cause damage to historic buildings [5]. The color patterns resulting from air pollutants on facades have an adverse impact on the aesthetics of heritage buildings to the detriment of their identity and potential economic value [6]. In the absence of preservation efforts, the deterioration may spread to the structural components and may lead to an increased risk of building collapse [5,7]. Therefore, proper heritage building preservation is important for building safety and for avoiding costly renovations. However, research on the preservation of heritage building facades is limited. Traditional cleaning methods for facade is inefficient. For heritage building facades, the best solution is to adopt intervention measures, such as appropriate adaptation of mechanical ventilation equipment to assist or expand natural airflow [8]. There is a lack of natural airflow intervention strategy to preserve facade in a more continuous and dynamic operation way. Additionally, traditional methods are difficult to monitor and optimize the air quality of heritage building facades in a stable and non-destructive manner in the long term [9].
