Tunnel maintenance Machine learning ABSTRACT Tunnel facility management (FM) is crucial for ensuring safety,
efficiency, and resilience of tunnel infrastructure. Current FM practices, such as reactive and preventive
maintenance, have limitations - reactive maintenance can't prevent failures, and preventive maintenance can't
predict asset maintenance needs, leading to costly and in - efficient processes. In addition, existing
computerized tunnel FM systems face various challenges, including the lack of integrated real-time monitoring
information, visualization of assets in a three-dimensional (3D) en - vironment, supporting predictive
maintenance, and scaling into long infrastructure with complicated spatio - temporal relationships. This study
addresses these limitations by proposing a data-driven digital twin (DT)-based framework that supports
predictive maintenance to improve tunnel FM processes and enhance resilience. The proposed framework consists
of six layers, allowing the integration of data from monitoring system, FM system, and building information
modeling (BIM) models. The framework proposes a flexible tunnel data model and classification system that
hierarchically divides the tunnel models, ensuring an efficient data connection from the physical twin to the
DT. The system was implemented in a tunnel case study that generates maintenance plans and work orders using
historical and current condition monitoring data, and the 3D visualization tech - nology suggests maintenance
and repair processes, making the FM decision process more effective. The proposed system detected and
predicted the twin state based on a data-driven analysis, and the prediction accuracy of the machine learning
models was sufficiently high for use in real scenarios to make FM plans in advance and prevent asset failures.
The proposed framework is contributing to the infrastructure resilience by enhancing the tunnel system ability
to predict the maintenance tasks and prevent failures using data-driven DT technology. 1. Introduction The
increasing population and urbanization of cities, have driven attention to underground infrastructures such as
tunnels. An under - ground tunnel system consists of a variety of structural elements, in - cluding shotcrete,
concrete linings, drainage, and other functional fa - cilities, such as lighting, fire protection, plumbing,
and ventilation. These elements are surrounded by a nonhomogeneous complex geolo - gical environment (
Sharafat et al., 2021 ). During operations, tunnel components face various defects like deformation, cracking,
leakage, service disruptions, and equipment failures, necessitating inspections and maintenance for optimal
service. Developing a resilient system for tunnel maintenance tasks can significantly reduce the risk of
expected failures ( Dui et al., 2025 ). Currently, reactive and preventive main - tenance approaches are used
for the facility management (FM) of tun - nels. Reactive maintenance is adopted after the failure of an asset,
whereas preventive maintenance is a schedule-based method that enables an FM team to perform maintenance tasks
at predefined in - tervals. However, these maintenance methods have limitations. Re - active maintenance
doesn't prevent facility failures, and preventive maintenance can't forecast facility conditions for proactive
scheduling of repairs or extending its lifespan. ( Hosamo et al., 2023 ). Hence, these maintenance approaches
should be changed to smart maintenance systems to address their shortcomings. A predictive maintenance ap -
proach can be adopted to enhance the resilience that uses historical data from the systems to predict the
condition of the asset so that maintenance actions can be planned in advance ( Gbadamosi et al., 2021 ). In
addition to these maintenance approaches, computerized systems are used to manage the FM data during FM
activities. One such system is the computerized maintenance management system (CMMS), which stores historical,
daily operational and maintenance (O&M), work or - ders, and other FM-related data. However, CMMS has
limitations, in - cluding difficulties in locating the facility, extracting the required FM https

al., 2021; Lee et al., 2018 ). However, these approaches si - mulate the tunnel lifecycle data in a virtual
environment that provides static information. Moreover, these studies focused on improving the visualization
aspects and did not integrate real-time condition data or support predictive maintenance. Relying on FM
decisions using such systems can result in low efficiency, low accuracy, high latency, and one-sided
decisions. The digital twins (DTs) approach can be adopted as a comprehen - sive smart solution ( Vivi et al.,
2019 ). Due to the emergence and ben - efits, numerous researchers have provided definitions and interpreta -
tions ( Khan et al., 2025; Lu and Brilakis, 2019 ). Several review papers summarizes, DT definitions,
challenges, and opportunities across in - dustries ( Babanagar et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023; Opoku et al.,
2021; Qi et al., 2021; Semeraro et al., 2021 ). Most definitions share three fun - damental components of
building DT: (1) physical components, (2) digital components, and (3) connections. Physical components re -
presents the actual objects or process, digital components replicates the objects in 3D environment, and
connections facilitates to connect the physical and digital components using hardware and software tools ( Tao
et al., 2019 ). The digital representations of physical components has been cate - gorized into three
categories ( Babanagar et al., 2025 ): (1) digital model, (2) digital shadow, and (3) digital twin. The
digital model depicts only the digital replica of the physical components, which is achieved through BIM
techniques. The digital shadow is the next stage, where the information from the physical twin have a uni-
directional flow with the digital model. Digital twin, on the other hand, have a bi-directional flow of
information between physical and digital components. For op - erational stage, a DT simulates physical
infrastructure/assets in a vir - tual environment by integrating and processing dynamic real-time FM
information in an integrated environment ( Lo et al., 2021 ). To max - imize the benefits of the collected
data, DTs develop machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) models to predict the future of the
infrastructure ( Hallaji et al., 2022 ). Within this context, Li et al. (2024) proposed a four-level maturity
framework for DT: descriptive twin, reflective twin, predictive twin, and prescriptive twin. Cotoarbă et al.
