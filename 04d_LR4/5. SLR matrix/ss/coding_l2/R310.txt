This study presents an exploratory, decision-support analysis of evacuation dynamics in the walled Old Town of
Nicosia, Cyprus, using a high-fidelity digital twin and a hybrid agent-based simulation framework integrating
pedestrian and vehicular movement. Rather than modeling a specific hazard, the study evaluates how structural
stressors commonly produced by disasters, including exit closures, road blockages, and shelter saturation,
influence evacuation clearance, congestion formation, and overall system robustness. Controlled simulation
experiments examine the sensitivity of evacuation performance to network disruptions, the effectiveness of
existing and expanded shelter configurations, and the role of population familiarity and guidance in
constrained historic environments. Results show that evacuation outcomes are highly sensitive to a small
number of critical exits and that uncoordinated increases in shelter capacity, without adaptive guidance, may
increase congestion and clearance times. Findings also reveal nonlinear congestion dynamics and long-tailed
evacuation delays that persist under idealized conditions. The study demonstrates the value of high-resolution
digital twins as exploratory tools for identifying structural vulnerabilities, evaluating mitigation
strategies, and supporting risk-informed evacuation planning in historic districts exposed to multiple
hazards. 1. Introduction Historic urban centers embody a persistent tension between cultural heritage
preservation and urban vulnerability. Dense morphology, lim- ited network redundancy, and regulatory
constraints on structural mod- ification often restrict the range of feasible risk-reduction interventions. As
a result, evacuation planning in such environments must operate within spatial and institutional limitations
that differ fundamentally from those of modern cities. This tension is particularly evident in the walled Old
Town of Nicosia, Cyprus. Enclosed by 16th-century Venetian fortifications and partially bordered by a
militarized buffer zone, the historic core is accessible through only six operational gates. Its irregular
medieval street network, narrow corridors, pedestrian-only segments, and one- way constraints create
asymmetric flow patterns and limited alternative routing options. While these features contribute to the
city’s heritage value, they also introduce structural bottlenecks that can severely constrain evacuation under

historic cities. 1.1. Multi-hazard evacuation challenges in historic cities Historic districts are rarely
exposed to a single hazard type, and risk often emerges from interacting or cascading events [1,4]. Earth-
quakes may generate debris-induced road blockages and façade col- lapses, while floods introduce depth-
dependent walkability constraints and progressive route loss [5]. In morphologically constrained his- toric
environments, such hazard-specific effects interact with limited network redundancy, potentially producing
nonlinear congestion and long-tailed evacuation delays. Although the present study does not simulate a
specific hazard process, it focuses on structural stressors that commonly arise across hazard types: gate
closures, road blockages, and shelter saturation. By abstracting from hazard triggers while retaining their
systemic conse- quences, the analysis provides hazard-agnostic insights into evacuation vulnerability in
historic environments. The framework is intentionally extensible, enabling the integration of debris fields,
flood extents, or fire spread models in future work. Different hazards can create different evacuation
conditions in dense historic environments. Earthquakes can cause sudden infrastruc- ture damage and debris
that block narrow streets. Floods gradually reduce road accessibility as water levels rise. Urban fires, in
contrast, require rapid evacuation under strong time pressure and may lead to more chaotic and panic-driven
movement patterns. Previous studies in historic city centers such as Florence (Italy) [1], Dubrovnik (Croatia)
under seismic risk [6], and Beirut (Lebanon) in earthquake evacu- ation simulations [7], as well as other
Mediterranean heritage con- texts [5], highlight how warning time, infrastructure disruption, and population
behavioral responses vary, shaping evacuation dynamics and congestion formation. 1.2. Research questions This
study addresses the following research questions: RQ1: How do limited exit availability and constrained street
mor- phology influence evacuation clearance times and congestion patterns in a historic walled city? RQ2: How
do shelter capacity and spatial distribution affect evacua- tion performance under conditions of network
stress? RQ3: To what extent does population familiarity and guidance modify evacuation outcomes in dense
heritage environments? Rather than predicting evacuation outcomes for a specific disas- ter scenario, the
objective is to identify structural vulnerabilities and decision-relevant sensitivities that can inform risk-
informed planning. 1.3. Study approach and contributions To investigate these questions, we employ a high-
fidelity, geo- referenced 3D digital twin of Nicosia’s Old Town.1 The simulation framework integrates detailed
spatial data with a hybrid agent-based model capable of representing both pedestrian and vehicular move- ment
within the same constrained environment. Pedestrian and vehicle agents operate on layered navigation meshes
that encode one-way restrictions, pedestrian-only segments, and exit constraints. Behavioral parameters are
calibrated using available traffic counts and density observations to approximate realistic movement dynamics.

selective gate closures, road blockages, and alternative shelter configurations. These scenarios isolate the
