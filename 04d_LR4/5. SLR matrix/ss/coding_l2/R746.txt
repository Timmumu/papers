Introduction 39 Tower cranes are essential in modern construction projects, facilitating the lifting and 40
moving of hefty modules, equipment, and tools from lifting points to target locations on 41 construction sites
[1]. Over prolonged periods of use, however, aging tower cranes may 42 experience structural fatigue,
corrosion, and deterioration due to exposure to high -cycle and 43 variable-amplitude loads, harsh working
environments, and improper operation and 44 maintenance, leading to degraded lifting capacity (LC) [2–8]. Such
degradation can increase 45 the risk of accidents and jeopardize the safety of workers and nearby properties,
resulting in 46 severe injuries or fatalities [9]. For instance, in 2022, the tower crane at the "S au Mau
Ping" 47 construction site in Hong Kong collapsed and killed three workers, where the operator set the 48
manufacturer-specified load without considering the deteriorated load and /or capacity [10]. 49 Similar
incidents have occurred worldwide, including a crane collapse that killed 18 people in 50 2016 in Dongguan,
China [11], and another crane collapse that killed seven people and injured 51 dozens more in 2008 in New York
City in the United States [12]. Thus, there is still a practical 52 need for real-time safety monitoring
systems to detect and prevent accidents caused by aging 53 tower cranes with degraded LC. 54 Various safety
monitoring systems have been developed and adopted in the construction 55 industry to address crane safety,
including load moment indicators (LMIs) [13–16], anti-two-56 blocking (ATB) systems [17, 18], and crane
cameras [19, 20]. LMIs measure the load on the 57 crane and provide a warning when the load is near or exceeds
the crane's max imum LC [13]. 58 Anti-two-block systems prevent the crane from collapsing by limiting its
operation when the 59 hook block is too close to the boom tip [18]. Crane cameras monitor the crane's movement
and 60 detect unsafe behavior by recording a visual perspective [20]. However, while these systems 61 can
improve safety on construction sites, they are generally not designed to address the specific 62 issue of
aging tower cranes with degraded LC. For instance, LMIs, ATB systems , and crane 63 cameras do not consider
the effects of wear and tear on crane components or the health level 64 of the crane and, thus, fail to
predict the internal degradation in LC, which can result in 65 unexpected accidents due to unintentional over-
loading. Furthermore, due to the unavailability 66 of historical data on crane operations, the complexity of
the deterioration mechanism itself, and 67 the unpredictability of external deterioration-related factors, it
remains an open question of how 68 to estimate and update the aging-induced LC degradation. 69 Many studies
have been conducted to tackle the issues of data availability and deterioration 70 mechanism in understanding
the degraded LC of tower cranes, which can be categorized into 71 two groups: (1) data-driven methods (DDM)
and (2) model-based methods (MBM). DDM uses 72 real-time, historical, and maintenance data [21] to predict
deterioration patterns [22]. Several 73 studies have used DDM to predict performance degradation in tower
cranes [23]. However, 74 DDM is only effective when data are abundant, and without proper domain knowledge of
the 75 deterioration mechanisms, it may be less reliable when applied practically. On the other hand, 76 MBM
requires an understanding of failure mechanisms such as cracks, wear, deflection, 77 friction, and stress. One
can estimate performance degradation by developing a mathematical 78 model that quantitatively describes the
crane ’s degradation. However, extensive experiments 79 are required for parameter identification, varying
from case to case. Due to the complex nature 80 3 of tower cranes, MBM may be less efficient. To overcome the
limitations of DDM and MBM, 81 Liu et al. [24] proposed a hybrid approach that combines both methods (DDM
-MBM). This 82 approach collects data from mu ltiple sensors since a single sensor cannot capture all the 83
relevant parameters that affect degradation. The use of multiple sensors may increase 84 complexity, but it
results in more accurate predictions. Additionally, the mechanical model 85 used in MBM can simulate the
structural performance of the tower crane, which increases the 86 interpretability of DDM. Therefore, a hybrid
approach that integrates multi -sensor data with 87 an interpretable model could be a possible solution to
enable real -time monitoring and 88 prediction of tower cranes’ safety conditions considering degraded LC. 89
As an instance of hybrid DDM -MBM methods, the digital twin-driven (DTD) approach 90 creates a replica of a
physical system , aiming to enable real-time data acquisition, response 91 prediction, and human-machine
interaction (HMI) [25]. The DTD approach has been widely 92 utilized across various studies f or various
applications, including product design, 93 manufacturing, planning, maintenance, management, control,
traceability, anomaly detection, 94 operational errors, and safety analysis [26–36]. For instance, in a study
by Yang et al. [37], a 95 hybrid DTD approach was used to predict the performance degradation in the
transmission unit 96 of CNC machines by incorporating damage indices with wear data from Achard wear theory.
97 Similarly, Tran et al. [23] predicted machine degradatio n by considering both the present 98 conditions of
the machine and degradation indices based on a year of data. However, these 99 methods are time-consuming and
primarily applicable only to those tools. Despite the extensive 100 use of DT in the degradation field, there
is limited research exploring the potential of the DTD 101 approach in predicting the deterioration of complex
