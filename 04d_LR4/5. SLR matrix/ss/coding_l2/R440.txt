multifunctional building skins that merge structural performance, daylighting, and human−architecture
interaction. However, the lack of integrated design frameworks coupling durability, real-time aesthetics, and
long-term service-life prognosis limits their adoption. Here, we present a glass- fiber-reinforced polymer
(GFRP) mesh-enhanced translucent polymer-concrete (GFRP- TRPC) panel that simultaneously serves as load-
bearing façade, AI-driven light canvas, and self-sensing structural element. Epoxy-vinyl ester polymer
concrete with 30 vol % recycled glass aggregate and embedded PMMA optical fibers (ϕ 1 mm) was fabricated by a
one-step vacuum- assisted resin infusion process, yielding 30-mm-thin elements with 65 MPa compressive
strength and 22% optical transmittance. Bidirectional E-glass GFRP grids (44% fiber volume) restrained
microcracking and enabled distributed fiber Bragg-grating (FBG) sensing at 5 cm pitch. A vision
transformer−generative adversarial network (ViT-GAN) converted real-time skeleton streams from Azure Kinect
into 30 fps light patterns on a 12 m curved light-emitting diode (LED) back wall, producing an interactive
“living fresco” that increased visitor dwell time by 340%. Edge-deployed TinyML CNNs processed FBG signals to
detect 0.1 mm cracks with 96.7% accuracy and autonomously dimmed the artwork as a safety cue. Synchrotron
X-ray CT revealed interfacial void fractions below 1.2%, validated by digital image correlation strain fields.
A calibrated reduced-order digital twin predicted only 7.8% stiffness loss and <5% optical attenuation after
30 years of Shanghai climate loads, with maintenance alerts triggered six months in advance. This study
demonstrates a scalable pathway for polymer-composite building skins to merge artistic expression, structural
integrity, and intelligent longevity management. 1. INTRODUCTION The building envelope is rapidly evolving
from a passive separator to an active, multifunctional skin that simultaneously bears structural loads,
modulates daylight, harvests informa- tion, and communicates with occupants. 1−3 This trans- formation is
driven by increasing demands for energy efficiency, occupant well-being, and adaptive building perform- ance
in response to climate change and urbanization pressures. Among emerging technologies, translucent concrete
(TRC) has attracted significant attention for its ability to transmit natural light while maintaining the
compressive strength of conventional concrete. 4,5 Traditional TRC systems incorporate optical fibers or
light-transmitting aggregates within Portland cement matrices to create architectural elements that blur the
boundary between structure and fenestration. 6 However, state-of-the-art TRC systems face several fundamental
limitations that restrict their widespread adoption. First, the reliance on brittle cementitious matrices and
steel reinforcement leads to high self-weight (typically 2.4−2.8 g/ cm 3 ), limiting their application in
lightweight façade systems. 7 Second, the alkaline environment of cement causes degrada- tion of embedded
optical fibers over time, reducing light transmission efficiency. 8 Third, conventional TRC lacks integration
with digital building systems, missing opportunities for real-time interaction and adaptive performance. 9
Finally, the absence of validated long-term durability models makes it difficult for architects and engineers
to predict service life and plan maintenance schedules. 10 Polymer-matrix composites offer a promising
alternative route to address these limitations. Epoxy- or vinyl-ester-based polymer concretes reinforced with
glass-fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) grids can achieve high specific stiffness, excellent corrosion
resistance, and superior design flexibility compared to cementitious systems. 11,12 The neutral pH environment
of polymer matrices is compatible with optical fibers, enabling stable light transmission over extended
periods. 13 Moreover, the electrical properties of polymer composites can be tailored to support embedded
sensors and actuators, creating opportunities for intelligent building skins. 14 Recent advances in fiber-
optic sensing, particularly fiber Bragg gratings (FBGs), have enabled distributed structural health monitoring
(SHM) in composite structures. 15,16 When integrated with machine learning algorithms, FBG sensor Received:

functional building skins that simultaneously provide structural support, environmental control, and human

systems. By creating real-time digital replicas of physical assets, digital twins enable predictive
maintenance, performance optimization, and lifecycle manage- ment. 20,21 In the context of building envelopes,
digital twins can integrate material degradation models, environmental loading data, and sensor feedback to
predict long-term performance and optimize maintenance schedules. 22 However, the application of digital twins
to polymer-composite building skins remains largely unexplored, particularly for systems that integrate
structural, optical, and interactive functions. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning techniques
are increasingly being applied to building systems for energy optimization, occupant comfort, and predictive
mainte- nance. 23,24 Computer vision algorithms can analyze human behavior and environmental conditions to
adapt building performance in real time. 25 Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have shown particular
promise for creating responsive architectural elements that can adapt their appearance based on contextual
inputs. 26 The integration of AI with building- integrated photonics could enable new forms of human- building
interaction that enhance both functional performance and aesthetic experience. Despite these technological
advances, three critical gaps remain in the development of next-generation translucent building skins. First,
there is an absence of scalable manufacturing routes for large-area translucent polymer- concrete panels that
can achieve both high mechanical performance and optical functionality. 27 Second, the integra- tion between
real-time aesthetic interaction and structural health monitoring has not been demonstrated, missing
opportunities for expressive safety communication. 28 Third, validated long-term durability models that couple
material degradation with environmental and human-use variables are lacking, hindering lifecycle assessment
and maintenance planning. 29 This study addresses these gaps by developing a comprehensive system that
integrates materials science, manufacturing technology, artificial intelligence, and digital twin modeling.
The primary objective is to create a 30-mm- thin GFRP-reinforced translucent polymer-concrete (GFRP- TRPC)
façade that functions simultaneously as a load-bearing wall, an AI-driven interactive light canvas, and a
self-sensing structural element. The system is designed to demonstrate how advanced polymer composites can
serve as platforms for intelligent building skins that merge structural integrity, energy-efficient
daylighting, and human-centric digital inter- action. Figure 1 positions the GFRP-TRPC façade as a
