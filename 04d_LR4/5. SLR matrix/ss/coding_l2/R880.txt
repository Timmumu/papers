Disaster prevention and mitigation a b s t r a c t Construction of disaster-resilient cities has attracted
considerable attention. However, traditional methods of studying urban disaster resilience through
experimental approaches are often constrained by various limitations, such as testing sites, costs and ethical
considerations. To address these constraints, this paper proposes incor- porating digital twin concepts into
urban disaster resilience research. By establishing a connection between the physical realm of the city and
its virtual counterpart, this approach utilizes digital simulations to overcome the limitations of
experimental methods and enables dynamic deduction and control of the disaster process. This pa- per delves
into three key aspects encompassing the acquisition of data from reality to the virtual space, disaster
simulation within the virtual space, and translation of virtual insights into eﬀective disaster prevention
strate- gies in reality. It provides a comprehensive summary of relevant research endeavors from the authors’
research group and showcases the eﬀectiveness and potential of the proposed techniques. These ﬁndings serve as
refer- ences for pre-disaster planning, real-time emergency assessments, post-disaster rescue operations, and
accident investigations for buildings and cities. 1. Introduction “Sustainable Cities and Communities ”(also
known as SDG 11) is at the heart of achieving all 17 of the United Nations Sustainable Develop- ment Goals
(SDGs) [ 1 , 2 ], aiming to ensure inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable cities and communities by
providing aﬀordable transporta- tion solutions, curbing urban sprawl, promoting active participation in urban
governance, safeguarding cultural assets, and addressing climate change issues. Earthquake, ﬁre, and wind
disasters are three of the most devastating disasters, resulting in immediate loss of life and long-term
economic damage [ 3–5 ]. Taking China as an example, according to data released by the National Bureau of
Statistics, the direct economic losses caused by earthquake, ﬁre, and wind disasters in 2021 were 10.6, 6.7,
and 13.2 billion, respectively. Therefore, enhancing the resilience of cities and communities against these
disasters is of paramount impor- tance for advancing SDG 11. In previous studies, physical-object-based
experimental methods have been widely used to test the performance and resilience levels of infrastructures,
such as buildings and bridges, at an individual object scale [ 6–8 ]. Owing to limitations resulting from
testing sites and costs, this method faces signiﬁcant diﬃculties when applied at an urban scale. ∗

Digital twins have become popular research topics in recent years [ 9–11 ]. The earliest concept of digital
twins was proposed by Grieves [ 12 ] in 2003. In 2011, the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Admin-
istration (NASA) ﬁrst introduced the concept of “twins ”i n the Apollo program [ 13 ] and provided a detailed
deﬁnition of digital twins [ 14 ]. It refers to mapping real-world entities into the virtual space, synchro-
nizing the virtual model with the real state of the entities, and achiev- ing interaction between the virtual
and real worlds [ 15 ]. Digital twin technology can be applied to guide decision-making in the real world
through simulations in the virtual space. Hence, in the last 10 years, re- searchers from diﬀerent ﬁelds have
explored the applications of digital twins in various areas, such as product design [ 16–19 ], manufacturing [
19–21 ], and operation condition monitoring [ 22–24 ]. In civil engineering, digital twin technology can not
only be used for three-dimensional(3D) reconstruction and visualization of individ- ual infrastructure
objects[25–28] or cities[29–30], but also can be ap- plied to disaster risk assessment and other
ﬁelds[31–38].Regarding in- dividual infrastructure objects, Ariyachandra and Brilakis [ 25 ] used a car-
