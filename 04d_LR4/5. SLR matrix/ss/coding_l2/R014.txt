zvonko.sigmund@grad.unizg.hr Abstract While Digital Twins offer transformative potential, their efficacy for
real-time control is constrained by the slow data acquisition and the high computational intensity re- quired
to process raw datasets like point clouds. This paper identifies these critical bottlenecks—specifically the
latency between data capture and actionable insight—and proposes a refined theoretical framework for near-
real-time automated progress monitoring and quality reporting. Building on the findings of the NORMENG project
and informing the subsequent AutoGreenTraC project, this research synthesizes state-of-the-art advance- ments
in reality capture, including LIDAR, SfM-MVS, and 360-degree vision. The study highlights a fundamental
divergence in stakeholder requirements: the need for millimeter- level precision in quality control versus the
demand for high-velocity documentation for progress monitoring. A key innovation presented is the shift toward
neural rendering techniques to bypass the computational delays of traditional photogrammetry and enable
immediate on-site visualization. By structuring a tiered processing hierarchy that combines lightweight edge
analysis for immediate safety and progress monitoring with asynchronous high-fidelity Digital Twin updates,
the framework aims to establish a single source of truth. Keywords: Digital Twin; automated progress
monitoring; reality capture; computer vision; AI; robotics; UAS; point cloud; edge AI 1. Introduction—The
Shift from Manual to Digital and Automated The construction industry, a cornerstone of global infrastructure
development, fre- quently encounters challenges in project management, often stemming from inefficient
progress monitoring combined with lacking and reactive quality reporting measures and rather descriptive
productivity assessment techniques [1]. This inefficiency contributes to significant cost overruns, schedule
delays, and a staggering $570 billion in direct rework costs annually within the trillion-dollar construction
industry, alongside an additional $440 billion in indirect costs [ 2,3]. While the theoretical foundations of
Digital Twins (DT) and Building Information Modeling (BIM)—pioneered decades ago [ 4,5]—promise transformative
potential, their practical efficacy for near-real-time construction control has been severely constrained by
two primary bottlenecks: the slow velocity of physical data acquisition, reliant on labor-intensive manual or
scanning methods prone to delays and errors [1,6,7], and the high computational intensity required for
processing vast, unstruc- tured datasets like point clouds [ 2,8]. These substantial financial implications

a critical need for advanced technological integration to enhance project oversight and operational
efficiency. This shift from manual to automated oversight is driven by the need for objective performance
benchmarks, as seen in the NORMENG project. While the project utilized high-tech modalities like LIDAR and UAS
photogrammetry to move away from the subjec- tive descriptive productivity assessments, it encountered a
critical reality: simply capturing data is insufficient for real-time control. The “norming”
process—calculating constructed volumes and labor efficiency—was hindered by the sheer latency of processing
these vast datasets, effectively turning a high-tech solution back into a slow, reactive one. Conse- quently,
the rationale for this paper’s focus on processing speed stems directly from these field experiences, where
the goal of “instantaneous” norming was unmet due to current computational constraints. It is expected that
real-time monitoring technologies will deliver comprehensive oper- ational visibility of the construction
processes through an integrated digital infrastructure. Progress monitoring integrated with DT, IoT sensors,
visual-based data collection processes, and technologies is expected to enable data-driven decision-making
that directly impacts schedule and cost control [9]. The development of advanced, automated systems for real-
time progress monitoring and quality reporting remains a significant challenge within the construction
industry [6]. Traditional methods, largely reliant on manual inspections and reporting, are time-consuming and
susceptible to human error, but with the lack of educated and reliable expert knowledge, the construction
industry is in dire need of a switch to digital technologies [6,10]. Within the scope of this research, “near-
real-time” is operationally defined as a spec- trum of processing latency dictated by a hybrid edge-cloud
architecture, ranging from milliseconds for critical on-site safety interventions to minutes and hours for
daily progress verification. This stands in contrast to the traditional asynchronous processing and manual
reporting, where the identification of issues depends on the supervisor’s capability to detect issues and
assess the level of completion without complex assessments or, on the other hand, analysis of vast
unstructured datasets with currently available data analy- sis processes, which frequently require hours to
days. Consequently, “reporting” in this framework encompasses a multi-modal delivery of insights tailored to
these timeframes, including instantaneous automated safety alarms, rapid on-site 3D visual deviations, and
continuous cloud-based DT dashboards. Although digital processes are anticipated to provide seamless access to
project data and facilitate expedited decision-making, their practical implementation frequently encoun- ters
substantial obstacles in achieving real-time data integration and generating actionable insights. This
consequently impedes timely and automated progress monitoring, effective discrepancy and quality reporting,
and accurate productivity assessments. Essentially, the current state of digitalization often falls short of
its anticipated benefits, particularly in delivering the promised swift and precise progress monitoring
capable of mitigating project schedule delays and cost overruns [7]. Currently, in the construction industry,
there is a paradigm shift occurring. While construction monitoring and control are still relying on labor-
intensive manual methods, they are slowly transitioning to automated, vision-based sensing technologies to
improve accuracy and timeliness in project management [1], aiming to provide a single source of truth. Here,
in the paper, the single source of truth is used as a presentable, reviewable, and documented result of the
construction process, which can be an information provider immediately during the construction process but
also further on down the line, where the construction process is not visible anymore. Still, digitalization
technologies are lagging behind the expectations of their users. https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
