Abstract Post-blast operations in tunnel construction represent a critical bottleneck due to manda- tory
downtime and hazardous environmental conditions. This study addresses these challenges by developing and
validating an integrated cyber–physical architecture that coordinates an autonomous Unmanned Aerial Vehicle
(UAV) and an Autonomous Wheel Loader (AWL) under the supervision of a Digital Twin acting as central
operational dig- ital interface. Specifically, this technology was designed to access the tunnel, evaluate
post-blasting conditions, and initiate operations during mandatory exclusion periods for personnel. The system
was validated in a realistic, Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-denied tunnel environment emulating
post-detonation visibility constraints. The results demonstrate that the aerial agent successfully navigated
and mapped the excavation front in less than 8 min, establishing a shared coordinate system for the ground
machinery. Through this collaborative workflow, the autonomous deployment enabled operations to commence 50%
to 80% earlier than conventional manual procedures. Furthermore, the system reduced daily operational time by
approximately 8%, with an estimated return on financial investment between one and seven months. Overall, the

point cloud processing; robotics in construction 1. Introduction Underground construction plays a pivotal role
in the development of modern infras- tructure, facilitating essential urban transportation networks and
enabling the efficient utilisation of subsurface space. This sector is currently undergoing a phase of rapid
expan- sion, driven by accelerating global urbanisation and the transition towards more resilient
infrastructure systems. Recent market analyses reflect this momentum, forecasting a com- pound annual growth
rate of approximately 6.8% for the global tunnel construction market between 2023 and 2028 [1], with certain
regional estimates projecting growth as high as 9% [1]. However, the execution of these subterranean works
presents significant technical and operational challenges. The environment is inherently hostile,
characterised by the absence of GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) signals, poor visibility, the
presence of dust, and the potential accumulation of harmful gases. These factors, combined with severe
communication constraints, critically affect navigation, sensing capabilities, and mission autonomy [2,3].
Consequently, these conditions complicate planning and operations while significantly elevating safety risks
for the workforce. Comparative analyses between underground and surface construction activities indicate that
accident rates in subterranean works can be considerably higher, in some instances more than double, due to
restricted spatial configu- rations, complex geological conditions, and limited evacuation options [4,5].
Within this context, the post-blast phase, which encompasses cavity inspection, geological character- isation,
debris removal, and material transport, represents a persistent bottleneck in the tunnelling cycle.
Traditionally, this phase relies on a series of sequential manual operations that must be executed by
specialised personnel. However, these activities cannot commence immediately, as they are contingent upon the
completion of mandatory ventilation periods to ensure atmospheric safety, often depending directly on on-site
observations [6,7]. This dependency on human intervention introduces significant limitations, includ- ing
exposure to potential ground instability, oxygen deficiency, and airborne dust [ 4,8]. Furthermore, the
requisite downtime for ventilation and the subsequent sequential exe- cution of inspection and cleaning tasks
results in substantial periods of inactivity. This directly impacts the project’s efficiency by lengthening
excavation cycles and reducing overall productivity [9,10]. Compounding these operational inefficiencies is
the difficulty of obtaining reliable real-time data. Measurements in such environments are often qualitative
rather than quantitative, hindering accurate decision-making and delaying the planning of subsequent project
phases [ 11]. Moreover, the lack of integrated digital information constrains interoperability with management
platforms, impeding effective coordination between on-site operations and control systems [12–14]. In response
to these challenges, the automation of operations through advanced robotic technologies and autonomous
navigation is increasingly recognised as a decisive step towards a safer and more resilient construction
paradigm [15–17]. Over the last decade, significant advances have been made in specific domains. Un- manned
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have been successfully deployed for tunnel inspection and
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18081133 Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 1133 3 of 25 mapping, capable of performing close-
range visual assessments and generating detailed three-dimensional reconstructions using sensor fusion
approaches like light detection and ranging (LiDAR) and vision-based Simultaneous Localization and Mapping
(SLAM). Nev- ertheless, UAV performance remains strongly challenged by environmental conditions such as low
illumination and airborne particulates [2,18,19]. In these post-blast scenarios, relying solely on standard
Mobile Laser Scanning (MLS) is insufficient; high concentrations of suspended matter cause significant laser
backscattering and attenuation, introducing noise and ‘phantom obstacles’ that compromise navigation safety
[3]. Therefore, to ensure robust data acquisition where single-modality perception fails [ 15], the proposed
architecture moves beyond varying geometric lidar-inertial odometry by integrating Thermal Imaging, which is
unaffected by visible obscurants, and specific intensity-based filtering algorithms. In parallel, the
deployment of autonomous or semi-autonomous ground vehicles for debris removal has shown promising results.
