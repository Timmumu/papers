Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (AEC) industry is in the midst of a profound digital
transformation. Historically charac- terized as a late adopter of digital technology, the sector has
tradition- ally relied heavily on the experiential knowledge of its professionals. However, growing pressures
from economic uncertainties due to factors such as low productivity, persistent labor shortages, and the
increasing complexity of modern projects are compelling companies to rapidly adopt digital construction
management strategies to optimize resources and workflows. Market projections indicate that adoption of
technolo- gies such as the Digital Twins (DTs) in the construction market will reach $155.01 billion by 2030,
with safety applications and worker monitoring driving significant growth [1]. For example, Cyber–Physical
Systems (CPS) integrate computation, networking, and physical pro- cesses, creating a feedback loop where
physical systems are monitored and controlled by computer-based algorithms [2]. Technological ad- vances such
as Building Information Modeling (BIM), DT, robotics, and Artificial Intelligence (AI) form the basis of a
next-generation approach to construction management that moves beyond manual processes and reactive decision-
making [3]. I This article is part of a Special issue entitled: ‘Next-Gen Construction’ published in

Accordingly, BIM2RDT implements an LLM-assisted orchestration layer as bounded semantic reasoning com- ponents
that close the loop between sensing and model maintenance, representing an initial step towards agentic
behavior, where LLMs are used iteratively via tool calls to transform observations into state updates and
bounded actions, rather than only producing responses. As such, we use the term ‘‘agentic’’ in a narrow,
operational sense where the AI module performs semantic reasoning that conditions per- ception (e.g.,
assigning gravity priors for ICP), triggers IFC-consistent twin updates, and proposes navigation constraints,
without claiming autonomous long-horizon planning or full decision authority. This study investigates three
research questions: RQ1: How can semantic information from static BIMs be leveraged to constrain and improve
point cloud registration accuracy in construction environments with ambiguous geometry and limited features?
RQ2: To what ex- tent can an LLM-based reasoning provide physically-informed, object- specific priors that
reduce local-minimum failures in scan-to-BIM align- ment? RQ3: How can sensor-detected safety activities be
semanti- cally mapped into interoperable digital twin representations to enable proactive intervention and
context-aware robot planning? To answer these questions, BIM2RDT implements a three-stage perception-to-update
loop. BIM semantics are translated into context- specific prompts for open-vocabulary detection using YOLOE
[4], en- abling recognition of both permanent BIM families and temporary site objects without retraining. We
introduce Semantic-Gravity ICP (SG-ICP), a point-cloud registration method where an LLM assigns object-
adaptive upright priors per BIM family (e.g., heavy crates vs. easily tipped cones), producing physically
plausible orientation reg- ularization. Finally, sensor-detected activities are mapped into IFC constructs
(IfcEvent, IfcTask), demonstrated via HAV monitoring against ISO 5349-1 thresholds [5] as a proof-of-concept.
The resulting activity layers generate contextual cost maps for robot path planning. The specific
contributions of this work are: • A BIM-conditioned perception pipeline using BIM object labels to prompt
open-vocabulary detection (YOLOE) and define ROIs for scan-to-BIM alignment. • SG-ICP: a registration method
using LLM-inferred, BIM-family- specific upright priors to regularize ICP and reduce local-minimum failures. •
An IFC-compatible mechanism to write sensor-recognized activi- ties into the digital twin (via IfcEvent
/IfcTask), demonstrated with HAV monitoring. • Experimental validation showing RMSE reductions up to 97% in
challenging scenarios and its consequences in context-aware path planning. Fig. 1 shows an overview of the
