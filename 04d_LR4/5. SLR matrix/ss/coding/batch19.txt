ABSTRACT As digital twins are gaining importance in various industries, research on their impact, use cases, and implementation in the field of construction is accelerating. In this study, we examine the impact of digital twins on building safety and security throughout their lifecycle, utilizing two state-of-the-art use cases for fire and anomaly detection. The solution employs advanced sensors, 3D visualizations for detecting safety and security threats, and data analysis to predict potential future threats. Using the data that was collected over a seventeen-month period, we present an implementation architecture for safety and security digital twins in buildings, explore the benefits and shortcomings of such a system, and discuss the learnings applicable to different lifecycle stages of a building. This paper contributes to the creation and study of a digital twin for fire safety, analyzing its performance using two real-world use cases and reporting the implementation results. Additionally, we extrapolate the learnings from the use phase of a facility to other lifecycle phases of buildings and facilities. INTRODUCTION The building safety and security is a crucial topic in today’s digitalized world, which encompasses elements such as fire safety, environmental (heating, ventilation, and air condition- ing) control, and surveillance and access monitoring. Safety refers to the intention of safeguarding from the impacts of accidents on health of people [1], and security means protect- ing assets such as information and resources from intentional attacks [2]. Safety and security requirements are essential throughout the building lifecycle. For instance, according to US Fire Administration, in 2020, 3500 people lost their lives to fire related accidents and there were 15200 injuries caused by fire, only in the United States [3]. Moreover, losses caused by fire incidents, in 2020, in the US stood at $21.9 billion [3]. THE BUILDING LIFECYCLE A lifecycle, generally, refers to the series of stages or phases that an object, system, or process undergoes from its inception to its conclusion or eventual end. The lifecycle of a building can be divided into four main stages which are design, con- struction, exploitation, and maintenance stages [22]. Accord- ing to CEN/TC 350 which is the European Committee for Standardization’s Technical Committee 350 for construction works, a building’s lifecycle is divided into four stages [23]: 1) Product phase which refers to producing and supplying the raw materials to the site. 2) Construction phase that refers to the on-site operations and processes such as installing materials on-site. 3) Use phase which means maintenance and repairs, oper- ational use such as heating, cooling, ventilation, light- ing as well as water use. 4) End-of-life phase, which is deconstruction, transport, recycling and reuse as well as discarding By integrating the two building lifecycle breakdowns [22], [23] and to offer a more comprehensive analysis in this research, we propose a building’s lifecycle consisting of pre- design, design and construction planning, site preparation, construction, use during the occupancy, and maintenance, renovation, and demolition phases, as illustrated in Fig 1. For instance, the digital design models are often not kept up to date through- out the building lifecycle [30]. Implementing BIM requires a multi- faceted approach involving multiple stages and is influenced by project networks and supply chain interdependencies [38]. Furthermore, over 80% of buildings in Europe do not have BIM models [25]. B. ADDITIONAL FINDINGS FROM THE CASE STUDIES The digital twin systems were consistently tested with fire events, demonstrating high accuracy and reliability in detect- ing small flames. Beyond fire detection, the system also proved effective at identifying other events and anomalies. In this section, we present examples of the anomaly detection capabilities of the digital twin system. It was observed that the digital twin can accurately monitor the uptime and downtime of the grain drying facility. For example, the operation of the hot air blower was clearly visible in the IR spectrum view. In one instance, we were able to detect the operation time span of the grain drying facility by monitoring the operational temperature of the hot air blower using data collected by the digital twin. As a result, the heat signature serves as a highly reliable indicator of machine operation, which in turn allows for conducting indirect facility/machinery utilization analysis. In an instance demonstrating the sensitivity of the digital twin, an area of fire hazard created by significant heat from a leaf blower used to clean the area after the grain drying season was detected and registered on the cloud-based dashboard (Fig 12 (a), (b)). Such incidents, in addition to providing an opportunity to confirm the alertness of the system, can be used to improve the anomaly detection capabilities of the digital twin. This can be achieved by marking the event with computer vision and adding the machinery (in this case, the leaf blower) to the items with NOS. In the future, the alarm will only be sent to the owner/operator when the leaf blower or other machinery is operating outside its NOS temperature range. During our second case study, we encountered an inter- esting event where the Senpack 2 detected the movement of birds as areas of temperature variations (Fig. 12 (c)). This is because birds and other mammals present a temperature FIGURE 12. (a) A leaf blower’s hot engine is detected and marked as an area of fire hazard. Such events can be similar to intrusion detection and demonstrate the potential for the digital twin for safety to provide security features that are independent of motion detectors. This presents an opportu- nity for added features that are not possible with conven- tional fire detection sensors, such as smoke detectors and IR detectors. For instance, the sensor package detecting motion in the environment based on this principle can incorporate a space utilization algorithm that can help with rescue missions during and after an incident. One of the challenges faced by every fire detection system is false positive alarms. Conventional approaches to address this issue while using a thermal camera include the use of two dimensional (2D) exclusion zones in the thermal image. Exclusion zones refer to areas considered as low fire hazard areas but can still create a significant number of false alarms due to processes or events involving high-temperature equip- ment and systems. However, the use of 2D exclusion zones can create inaccuracies that result in the inclusion of hazard areas in the fire detection exclusion zones. A feature of a digital twin-enabled fire detection system is the possibility of establishing 3D exclusion zones to mitigate false alarms. Access to pixel distance data allows the system to evaluate whether the collected data belongs to the 3D fire detection exclusion zone or not. For example, in Fig 13 from the first case study, the cube on the table near the laptop is a 3D exclusion zone assigned to a low-risk area of the environment. During the monitoring of the environment, the excluded spaces are exempted from monitoring and therefore cannot trigger false fire alarms. Similarly, the integration of AI with OMA has been explored to improve feature extraction under varying operational conditions (Zhang et al., 2022). However, the combination of these approaches into a unified, real-time monitoring framework for construction-phase steel structures remains underexplored. This study aims to bridge this gap by integrating multi-sensor data, AI- driven feature extraction, and probabilistic decision-making within a digital twin environment. Conventional SHM methods primarily depend on frequency-domain feature extraction and threshold-based discrimination for maintenance strategy formulation or damage localization (Honarjoo et al., 2024). However, these methods are unsuitable for capturing non-smooth and heterogeneous features in construction-phase signals and may experience sudden accuracy degradation under real-world interference. Machine learning and deep learning have demonstrated significant advantages in damage recognition, including improved accuracy, enhanced efficiency, and superior generalization performance; nevertheless, these methods are largely confined to offline analysis and post-processing, with limited research on online incremental learning and practical deployment. Furthermore, research integrating digital twin frameworks with probabilistic graphical models (PGMs) for operational decision-making remains primarily theoretical, lacking seamless integration with construction-phase monitoring systems. The widespread implementation of end-to-end real-time early warning systems remains limited, which constrains the effectiveness of maintenance decision-making (Smith et al., 2024). To address these challenges, this study develops an end-to-end dynamic monitoring framework encompassing sensor data acquisition, intelligent analysis, and decision mapping. A hybrid denoising scheme, based on wavelet transform and Kalman filtering, enhances the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) by approximately 30%, ensuring robust feature extraction. A convolutional neural network (CNN)–long short-term memory network (LSTM) hybrid model, equipped with an online incremental learning mechanism, adaptively fine-tunes spatiotemporal feature representations, achieving a classification accuracy of 93.6%. Finally, a digital twin decision platform employing a Bayesian network maps health indicators to maintenance strategies, effecting dynamic warning triggers and prescriptive maintenance recommendations. By integrating the sensing layer, edge computing layer, and cloud-based algorithm layer through digital twin technology, the system achieves accurate health identification of steel structures during construction. This integration provides technical support for ensuring structural stability throughout the construction phase (Brenner et al., 2023). The molding equipment is the key to the construction of super high-rise buildings and structures. Among the various types of molding equipment, the overall steel platform (jacking molding system) and hydraulic climbing mold technology are the most widely employed [1–3]. In the construction of buildings with a height of more than 300 m, there are problems such as large lateral sloshing amplitude, large lateral load, and high safety risk of high-altitude operation. To overcome the difficulties of high-altitude operation, the fourth-generation intelligent integrated platform “air building machine” independently developed by China Construction Third Engineering Bureau Group Co., Ltd. realizes the integration technology of formwork and machinery, which greatly improves work efficiency and construction safety [4–7]. In the process of using the whole construc- tion platform, the jacking operation of the BM represents the pinnacle of construction industrialization, digitalization, and intelligence. This places high demands on the attitude control of the jacking system [ 8]. Introduction The traditional construction industry has the disadvantages of extensive construc- tion mode, waste of resources, frequent safety accidents, and low intelligence level [ 1]. Integrating modern information technology into the current practice of the construction in- dustry has promoted the rapid development of new building industrialization represented by prefabricated buildings. Prefabricated building is a new construction method, which involves many new technologies in the construction stage [ 2]. Such a new construction method has the advantages of resource-saving, environmental protection, and a short construction period, which is an important way to transform the traditional construction mode to the modern industrialized construction mode [3]. Unfortunately, due to the low level of professional skills of construction workers, the construction stage of prefabricated buildings has also become a stage of frequent safety accidents. Prefabricated construction processes contain the transportation stage, hoisting stage, and installation stage. The hoisting stage is the most critical part of various prefabricated construction processes. Hoisting operations have the following characteristics [ 4]: (1) prefabricated components with large weight and high-altitude operation; (2) the hoisting operation is a combined movement, with many and scattered dangerous points; (3) large range of movement; (4) cooperation of multiple working types; (5) the inﬂuence relation- ship between various hoisting safety risk factors is complex. In the hoisting stage, safety accidents such as the overturning of tower cranes, rope breaking, collision, decoupling, and the falling of components occur easily [5]. The reasons for safety accidents are mostly Sensors 2022, 22, 2522. https://doi.org/10.3390/s22072522 https://www.mdpi.com/journal/sensors Sensors 2022, 22, 2522 2 of 24 inadequate on-site safety management, workers’ illegal operation, and inadequate equip- ment maintenance. Therefore, the safety control problem in the hoisting stage is worthy of in-depth study. Safety has always been the focus of the construction industry. According to ofﬁcial statistics, there were 773 accidents and 904 deaths in the production of housing and munici- pal engineering in 2019. The object strike accidents accounted for 15.91% of the total, and the hoisting machinery injury accidents accounted for 5.43% of the total [6]. The occurrence of the two accidents is closely related to the hoisting operation. Many scholars have done a lot of research in the ﬁeld of safety risk management, but there are still few research results in the ﬁeld of prefabricated building safety risk management. A considerable num- ber of scholars focus on the optimal arrangement of tower cranes [ 7,8], transportation scheduling [9], and motion control [10,11]. With the development of safety risk research theory and technology, the research concept of safety risk issues has also been transformed from post-analysis to pre-prevention. The research on safety issues is roughly divided into three stages [12]: the ﬁrst stage focuses on the statistical analysis of safety accidents and safety measures; the second stage enters the active safety management stage, using more advanced statistical techniques to identify and evaluate the safety risk; the third stage is the real-time monitoring stage of security risks. The use of virtual reality technology for security training and the real-time collection of security risk information can prevent and control security risks in a timely fashion. However, the dynamics of construction site elements are still difﬁcult to track, and lack effective information integration and interaction mechanisms. At present, relying on the supervision of on-site safety management personnel to control the occurrence of safety accidents is very time-consuming. Visualization technol- ogy has been gradually applied to the safety management of construction sites. With the help of location information, imaging information, and alarm technology, workers’ behaviors and site environments (including equipment) can be monitored, analyzed, and warned [13]. Although visualization technology has developed, it is often only managed for individual safety risk factors. The hoisting operation process of prefabricated buildings is a dynamic system that contains multiple factors of the human-machine-component- environment. Therefore, the elements contained in the system cannot be separated and controlled separately. Digital twin provides a solution to complex system problems and has been widely used in workshop scheduling [14], fault detection, and diagnosis [15] in the ﬁeld of intelligent manufacturing. Digital twin can integrate various technologies. Based on establishing a virtual model, the data collected from the site are fused with the model, and the analysis results are fed back to the construction site in real-time to realize the visual control of safety risks. However, the application of digital twin in the construction industry is not mature enough. The introduction of a digital twin to the safety management of prefabricated building hoisting mainly faces the following problems: based on considering the correlation between the safety risk factors of prefabricated building hoisting, it is very important to integrate the safety risk mechanism model with the data collected in real-time and achieve the purpose of the scientiﬁc decision-making of safety control. This paper presents a framework for the safety risk control of prefabricated building hoisting based on digital twin. To ensure the better integration of the mechanism model and data to express the relationship between safety risk factors, the hierarchical model of hoisting safety risk is deﬁned in the process of establishing the digital twin model. A multi- level hierarchical model is established by using the Decision-making Trial and Evaluation Laboratory (DEMATEL) and Interpretative Structural Modeling Method (ISM) to show the inﬂuential relationship between the factors of hoisting safety risk. Bayesian network is introduced in the process of driving the digital twin mechanism model and monitoring data integration analysis. After determining the direct and indirect inducement variables of security risk, the prior probability of each node is obtained by training Bayesian network parameters with the expectation–maximization (EM) algorithm. If the direct inducement variable is in a dangerous state, the occurrence probability is set to 100% and input into the Sensors 2022, 22, 2522 3 of 24 Bayesian network structure as evidence to obtain the posterior probability. By calculating the criticality of each indirect inducement variable to the direct inducement variable in a dangerous state and comparing the criticality, the key factors leading to the occurrence of danger are screened to achieve the purpose of the visual analysis of safety risk control decision. Finally, the feasibility of the proposed hoisting safety risk control framework is further veriﬁed by taking the risk of the cable inclination at a certain time in a speciﬁc case as an example. Construction Safety Risk Management Many scholars have studied the issue of safety risk management and have formed many mature methods and technologies [ 16]. Safety risk management includes safety risk identiﬁcation and assessment, security risk prediction, and security risk control. At present, work breakdown structure-risk breakdown structure (WBS-RBS) [17], analytic hi- erarchy process (AHP) [18], fault tree analysis (FTA) [19], Fuzzy comprehensive evaluation (FCE) [20], Cloud model [21], D-S evidence theory [16], and other methods have been used in construction safety risk management. The safety risk management scheme integrated by various methods has also been applied. For example, Yang et al. introduced data-driven Bayesian networks into the TOPSIS method. A new ship detention risk assessment method and reasonable risk control strategy are proposed [22]. With the deepening of theoretical research on safety risk management, the research focus has gradually turned to the essence of safety risk. Based on the extraction of the accident chain, the construction of the risk network, and the formulation of safety measures, Chen et al. proposed a data-driven research framework of construction engineering safety promotion strategy based on the risk network [23]. In recent years, BIM, artiﬁcial intelligence, and Internet of Things are also applied to construction safety risk management. Choe et al. proposed a four-dimensional building safety planning process, which solved the problem of spatio-temporal safety information integration at speciﬁc locations, and provided an active safety planning tool for safety managers [24]. Lin et al. used the Fuzzy set theory and machine learning method in the risk assessment of mining process and integrated real-time collected data into the BIM management platform to monitor, control, and manage dynamic safety risks [25]. Li et al. proposed the safety risk identiﬁcation system (SRIS) and early warning system (SREWS) for subway construction in China. The contents of the risk identiﬁcation knowledge base, retrieval matching algorithm, data fusion, and BIM technology monitoring safety state are mainly studied [26]. Some scholars also apply ontology knowledge [27], computer vision, and semantic reasoning [28] to construction safety management, to more accurately assess the level of safety risks. Sensors 2022, 22, 2522 4 of 24 In the methods mentioned above, the objects of construction safety risk assessment and control are mostly subway, tunnel, pipe gallery, and other underground projects. Although BIM, Internet of Things, artiﬁcial intelligence, and other technologies are integrated, the technologies are not very well integrated, the information interaction mechanism is still imperfect, and most studies ignore the correlation between safety risks. At present, the research on the safety risk management of prefabricated building hoisting operation is relatively minor. Safety risk assessment methods are mostly static and subjective. Xun et al. proposed the DEMATEL method to calculate the mutual inﬂuence between the index weights and the method of applying game theory to calculate the combined weights. The safety risk of the prefabricated building hoisting operation is evaluated by the cloud model [29]. Zhao et al., based on the method of determining the weight of the conventional safety risk index, introduced the system dynamics theory and established the SD model of the safety risk measurement of prefabricated building hoisting operation combined with qualitative and quantitative, which solved the dynamic and complex problems in the construction safety risk system [30]. Shen et al. constructed a safety risk assessment model based on Cloud-BN to dynamically evaluate the safety risk of prefabricated hoisting [31]. Liu et al. established a safety risk early warning model of hoisting operation based on RVM (relevance vector machine) to accurately judge the safety status of hoisting operation [32]. In addition, some scholars analyzed the safety accidents of tower cranes and determined the safety risk factors of tower cranes. From the perspective of complex social and technical systems, the safety of tower cranes was evaluated by qualitative and quantitative meth- ods [4], the safety factor of tower cranes was developed [33], and the safety standards were improved [34]. 2.2. Introduction In the construction industry, the lack of integration between the dig- ital world and physical world, especially the interaction between data analysis and the corresponding action, has led to information fragments as well as data duplication and stagnation between differ- ent stages in the construction life cycle. It lowers the efficiency of management and hinders collaboration between staff members, diverse professions, and distinct construction phases. In particular, the situations on a construction site are dynamic with constant changes in facilities, equipment, materials, people, site layout, and various other components at different phases, which increases the requirement for coordination and collaboration ( Kumar and Cheng 2015). Consequently, these increase the complexity of monitoring and management of construction sites. Construction informatization has become the future direction of development, whereby data-driven and dynamic modeling methods are employed to address some concerns in the field of engineering management in recent years, such as worker-power manage- ment ( Kim et al. 2020 ), safety monitoring ( Su et al. 2021 ), and decision-making support ( Abotaleb and El-adaway 2018 )a sw e l l as workflow administration ( Ma et al. 2018 ). The digital twin has the potential capability of providing the effective solutions to ad- dress these problems ( Lu et al. 2020c ). Digital twin technology is defined as creating a visual and digital model of a physical object ( Kaur et al. 2020 ). There is a profound interaction between the physical and digital components through the continual and dynamic data exchange between the physical and digital environments. It provides real-time monitoring, updating, simulating, analyzing, controlling, predicting, and opti- mizing (Kaur et al. 2020; Schleich et al. 2017), as well as validating and seamlessly coordinating throughout the life cycle. As a result, information silos and data duplication can be effectively avoided. By using this approach, t he construction site situation can be monitored and managed alongside the whole process. The digital twin basic architecture contains (1) the digital representa- tion based on building information modeling (BIM) technology, (2) Internet of Things (IoT) containing sensor technology, (3) data storage, integration and analytics, and (4) interaction with the physical environment. Digital representation is the foundation of the digital twin. BIM is the key technology to build the digital representation because BIM establishes corresponding information models for the target components and collects information corresponding to compo- nents. Three-dimensional modeling of BIM for specific construc- tion projects can provide stakeholders with a visualization function to show the overall situation of the project and its components (Davis 2003). Considering the limitations of hardware and software and the purpose of BIM usage in the different stages, it is not necessary to model BIM objects with 100% reality ( CIC 2019d ). As a result, the level of details (LoDs) can be utilized to determine the minimum detail level of BIM objects. The American Institute of Architects (AIA) defines LoDs as the measure, estimate, and schedule progression of a model ( BIMForum 2016). Literature Review Digital twin technology provides a rich virtual representation of the physical environment ( Kaur et al. 2020 ) for the entire life cycle. It can support the planning, tracking, monitoring, operating, risks and problems identifying, improvement, performance optimization, maintenance, and future resources requirement predicting ( Kaur et al. 2020 ; Schleich et al. 2017 ). At present, this technology has been applied in many fields, including construction, industry, aviation, manufacturing, and health care as well as agriculture and others. Digital twins have been developed well in fields such as manufacturing. It guarantees support to manufacturing activities such as monitoring, management, optimization, and maintenance (Cimino et al. 2019 ). Furthermore, it can enhance process effi- ciency to increase the productivity and reduce the energy consump- tion, as well as predict future resource requirements ( Park et al. 2020). The technology provides the capabilities of tracing product fault sources and analyzing production efficient bottlenecks ( Lu et al. 2020c ). In the health care area, the digital representation of an individ- ual, including personal data like health data, activity data, and emo- tional status, can assist in comprehending personal well-being and working conditions in a factory. This can lead to improving the psychological and physical health of employees and achieving upskilling and the best production performance through ultrareal- istic training programs ( Kendzierskyj et al. 2019 ; Lu et al. 2020c ). In the aviation sphere, digital twin technology contributes to mov- ing the aerospace industry from reactive to proactive maintenance based on predicting the structural behavior and asset failure by sim- ulating an aircraft ’s behavior and pinpointing critical weak spots (Glaessgen and Stargel 2012 ; Lu et al. 2020c ). In the agricultural domain, the digital twin can be utilized to predict climate change, monitor the growth of plants, and generate advice to farmers for ensuring harvesting at the most cost-efficient time. It also informs about changes in the microenvironment such as temperature, pressure, pH, and soil nutrients to provide improved feedback (Barnabas and Raj 2020 ). Data/Information Management For a construction site, data management issues mainly involve multiparty data management, project information management, professional technical information, data cooperation and sharing, and security and privacy issues. The construction process has a fragmented and complex struc- ture. It is an information-intensive process because a large volume of information is exchanged and transferred during the project life cycle ( Chen and Kamara 2011 ). [DOI: 10.1115/1.4053606] Keywords: digital twin, safety monitoring, multi ﬁdelity surrogate model, crane boom, design automation 1 Introduction Cranes are one of the most widely used heavy equipment in fac- tories, construction sites, and ports. As a key component, the crane boom deserves great attention in terms of structural safety as acci- dents caused by the structural damage of the crane boom may cause disastrous consequences [ 1,2]. By monitoring, analyzing, and predicting the structural status of the crane boom in real time, the occurrence of such accidents can be decreased to a certain extent. Therefore, an effective way is required to quickly and reli- ably predict the structural performance of any key location on the crane boom and improve its design. As a promising solution, the digital twin is widely used for online monitoring, analysis, and pre- diction of various industrial devices including aircraft, vessels, and automobiles. Therefore, the digital twin was adopted in this study for the safety monitoring and prediction of the crane boom. As initially deﬁned by Grieves [3], the digital twin contains phys- ical products, virtual products, and connections between them. Since its introduction, the concept, its implementation, and applica- tions have been heavily studied [ 4–8]. NASA and the US Air Force have applied digital twin technology to the condition monitoring, fault diagnosis, life prediction, and design optimization of various types of air vehicles by integrating diverse heterogeneous informa- tion sources [ 9–11]. In addition, numerous studies have demon- strated its applications in diverse equipment, including mobile cranes [ 12], wind turbines [ 13], and computer numerical control machine tools [14]. The digital twin has transformed from a descrip- tive concept to an actionable technique. In particular, structural safety monitoring is one of the most promising applications of the digital twin as it can obtain the real- time reﬂection of the physical world and facilitate decision-making based on fast analytical algorithms. By taking the real forces or dis- placements obtained from sensors as inputs, Haag and Andrel developed a digital twin that employed the ﬁnite element method (FEM) to perform the structural analysis of a simple bending beam [ 15]. Guivarch et al. proposed an approach to building a digital twin for a swashplate rotor assembly of a helicopter using multibody simulations [16]. Fotland et al. performed multiple simu- lation methods and ef ﬁciently built a digital twin for a crane pulley and cable [ 17]. Moi et al. implemented a digital twin for the condi- tion monitoring of a knuckle boom crane based on an inverse 1Corresponding author. These studies focused on combining mecha- nistic models, such as structural statics and dynamics with numeri- cal methods to establish digital twins. The essential bene ﬁt of using this way to build the digital twin is that the model parameters have an actual physical meaning, which facilitates the scienti ﬁc interpre- tation of the results. However, numerical methods are typically dif ﬁcult to be used in real-time applications due to their high computational con- sumption. To overcome this limitation, Rasheed et al. suggested using arti ﬁcial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) to build the digital twin [ 19]. Von Rueden et al. described a hybrid modeling approach by combining AI/ML algorithms and simulations, aimed at bridging the gap between data-based and knowledge-based modeling [ 20]. Magargle et al. introduced a simulation-based digital twin to support heat monitoring and pre- dictive maintenance for an automotive braking system [ 21]. Wang et al. presented a digital twin for rotating machinery fault diagno- sis based on rotor dynamics and the FEM [ 22]. Li et al. employed a dynamic Bayesian network to build a digital twin for monitor- ing the crack growth on the leading edge of an aircraft wing [ 23]. Essentially, the currently popular AI and ML methods utilize sta- tistics to predict trends and patterns. To use digital twins in struc- tural safety monitoring, a common way to introduce AI/ML is to use the results of numerical analysis as the training data to build the AI/ML model. In this way, AI/ML model equations are derived by developing a probabilistic model that best describes the relationship between dependent and independent variables. However, since the statistical model is based on correlation rather than causality, it cannot provide an interpretation of the mechanistic process. Both mechanistic and statistical models have their advantages and shortcomings. Therefore, a more reasonable way to combine simulation with AI/ML should be adopted to build the digital twin for the structural analysis. One promising approach was pre- sented by Willcox and her coworkers by combining a library of component-based reduced-order models with Bayesian state esti- mation or optimal tree algorithm to create data-driven physics-based digital twins [ 24,25]. Compared with an optimal tree, the Bayesian state estimation could quantify the uncertainty of the digital twin. Furthermore, they proposed a probabilistic graphical model as a formal mathematical representation of the digital twin and its asso- ciated physical asset, which helped to move the digital twin from a one-off model to an accessible robust model at scale [ 26]. Another way to combine simulations with AI/ML was realized simply by training the simulation results based on several data points of samples, while other data points in the solution space were interpo- lated or ﬁtted [ 27]. However, a reliable model built by this way generally requires substantial samples, which will consume consid- erable computational resources. The two combination-based approaches are limited by high computational costs. Therefore, if the computational effort is reduced and the prediction accuracy is improved, the digital twin composed of mechanistic and statistical models will be more lightweight, ef ﬁcient, and reliable. The multi ﬁdelity surrogate (MFS) model widely used in engi- neering design and optimization is adopted to achieve this goal. A high- ﬁdelity (HF) model is generally accurate but also more time consuming than the corresponding low- ﬁdelity (LF) model [28]. A general trend of the outputs of interest can be provided by a large number of inexpensive LF samples, and the accuracy of outputs can be improved by a few expensive HF samples [ 29]. Thus, the MFS model can not only greatly reduce the computational effort of the numerical methods but also improve the accuracy of the single-ﬁdelity surrogate model. In this study, a lightweight digital twin for structural safety monitoring based on the MFS model was proposed by adopting both statistical and mechanics-based models, and the data obtained from the models and multiple sensors were combined to provide more accurate results and faster solutions. Notably, the simulation of the crane boom was limited to the linear elastic stage of materials. The MFS model- based lightweight digital twin focuses on the key locations of equip- ment, is more accurate, ef ﬁcient, and lightweight, and uses fewer computing resources to achieve higher accuracy compared with the digital twin proposed in our previous work [ 27]. Discussion and conclusions are presented in Sec. 5. 2 Composition of the Lightweight Digital Twin 2.1 Computer Simulation Model. When taking into account the structural performance information, the current digital twin is generally expressed by various mechanistic models and calculated by multiple numerical methods, including the FEM, ﬁnite volume method, ﬁnite difference method, etc. In this study, the FEM was adopted to execute the simulation for the structural performance of the crane boom. However, running HF ﬁnite element analysis might be extremely time consuming, especially for systems with high-dimensional inputs. Therefore, it is signi ﬁcant to conduct the discretization of input variables and the simpli ﬁcation of the system for building a digital twin. The input variables of engineer- ing problems mainly contain shape, posture, velocity, load, and so on. The combination of these input variables is different in various states of the same system, and it is dif ﬁcult to simulate a system that contains all these different states simultaneously. Discretization can signiﬁcantly simplify this problem and break the limitation. When simulating a system, the input variables are divided into several dimensions, and each dimension is further discretized based on which the entire solution space can be represented through sam- pling. In this study, two types of computer simulations are executed: the LF ﬁnite element (LFFE) model and the HF ﬁnite element (HFFE) model. In this study, the LFFE model is built by three- dimensional (3D) beam element, while the HFFE model is built by the tetrahedral element. The key to building the digital twin is to take advantage of both the LFFE and HFFE models to improve computational ef ﬁciency while meeting certain accuracy requirements. 2.2 Multi ﬁdelity Surrogate Model. Surrogate models can be classiﬁed into four types: single-ﬁdelity, hybrid, adaptive sampling- based, and the MFS models. For the construction of a lightweight digital twin, the MFS models with three features, lightweight, high accuracy, and high robustness, are more suitable. Here, “light- weight” means computationally inexpensive, which takes up less storage space. Among the three features, lightweight is the most important one as it can guarantee the real-time calculation of the digital twin. Besides, both accuracy and robustness are considered. To ﬁnd an MFS model appropriate to build the lightweight digital twin, several commonly used MFS models have been studied, including co-Kriging [ 30], MFS-radial basic function (MFS-RBF) [31], MFS-moving least-square (MFS-MLS) [32], and linear regres- sion MFS (LR-MFS) models [ 33]. The lightweight, accuracy, and robustness of these models were compared with each other compre- hensively (see Sec. 3.1). 2.3 Sensor Data. With multiple sensors, the states of physical entities can be dynamically transformed into the digital space, indi- cating that the sensor data are used as the inputs of the MFS model. Taking these sensor data as dynamic inputs in this way, the struc- tural performance of the system can be predicted in real time once the MFS model is built. Different from the previous method, which only uses numerical methods or single- ﬁdelity surrogate models to build digital twins, the MFS model-based lightweight digital twin com- bines their advantages while eliminating their shortcomings. Taking the MFS-RBF model as an example, we proved that the pro- posed digital twin can improve the accuracy of digital twins built by single-ﬁdelity surrogate models and reduce the computational costs of digital twins built by numerical methods. In addition, the uncer- tainty from the physical space (i.e., sensors and other factors) was ana- lyzed to quantify the uncertainty of the lightweight digital twin. The effectiveness of the proposed digital twin was validated by applying it on a lab crane boom. In the MFS model-based digital twin, the single- ﬁdelity LF surrogate model trained by considerable LF data are approximately regarded as the mechanistic model over the entire solu- tion space, while the single- ﬁdelity HF surrogate model built by a small amount of HF data is employed as the statistical model. There- fore, the MFS model can integrate the advantages of the mechanistic model that can provide a causal relationship in structural analysis and the characteristics of statistical models that are easy to build and less costly. Thus, the MFS model-based lightweight digital twin is reliable and computationally affordable, enabling real-time structural safety monitoring. Moreover, the MFS model-based light- weight digital twin is not only suitable for mechanical structures, equipment, and systems but also can be extended to other ﬁelds and industries, such as biomechanics and ﬂuid mechanics. This work was limited to predicting the structural performance of a linear elastic material, and the structural analysis is only restricted to the elastic stage. Future work should, therefore, be designed to explore more complex physical processes, such as nonlinear mate- rials, plastic deformation, and multiphysics coupling because a more complex model can more closely re ﬂect complex physical processes in real life and can provide a more realistic digital twin.
