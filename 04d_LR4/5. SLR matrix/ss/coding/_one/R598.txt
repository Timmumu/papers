F:12740 IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON INTELLIGENT TRANSPORTATION SYSTEMS, VOL. 26, NO. 8, AUGUST 2025 Digital Twin Vision for Freight Railroads: A Case Study in North America Silvia Galván-Núñez , Ryan Alishio, and Christopher Johnson Abstract— In recent years, the concept of “digital twins” (DTs) has gained increasing attention in the railroad industry. This complex concept allows the creation of digital representations of physical assets or processes and can support a railroad’s transportation system throughout its lifecycle. For decades, MxV Rail has used the Facility for Accelerated Service Testing (FAST (Registered trademark.) to enable research and testing designed to improve the safety, reliability, and efficiency of North American railroads. At FAST, data streams generated by 1) operations, 2) maintenance, 3) wayside and onboard detector technologies, and 4) individual testing are employed to better understand the effects of heavy axle loads (HAL) as high tonnage accumulates on track components at various stages of wear. The development and implementation of DTs have strong potential to support decision-making for infrastructure and mechanical systems with regard to monitoring, predictive maintenance, and knowledge transfer to conditions where data is unavailable. This paper seeks to open the conversation by reviewing the concept of DTs in the North American railroad industry and showing the vision of the implementation layers for making a DT for FAST and the current efforts to achieve this vision. Index Terms— Digital engineering, digital twins, railroad freight, FAST. I. I NTRODUCTION W ITH the growing interest and rapid advancements in digital transformation, along with the drive to create more value from both large and small data streams, the concept of digital twins (DTs) has emerged as a focal point across various industries. Before diving into the concept of DTs, it is necessary to introduce the concept of digital engineering (DE), which is defined as a comprehensive approach that consolidates information throughout the lifecycle of a megaproject. This approach leverages digital information management systems for designing, engineering, constructing, and maintaining complex projects [1]. When implemented properly in the aerospace, automotive, energy, and other industries, DE can significantly improve the quality of services and achieve notable cost reductions in these industries [1]. At its optimum, any information that could be obtained from inspect- ing a physical manufactured product can be obtained from its digital twin.” Both DE and DT can be considered the synthesis of established methods and processes, forming a unified framework to improve system management across various sectors. Whether the systems exist or are hypothetical, this framework may integrate techniques such as physics-based modeling, machine learning, and analytics to create cohesive digital system replicas. While some researchers emphasize the real-time visualization capabilities of DTs to distinguish them from traditional modeling and simulation methods, other researchers point to the creation of a virtual duplicate of a physical object as the key innovation for a DT [3]. The concept of DTs has been implemented across a variety of fields and industries, including manufacturing [3], [4], automotive [5], aerospace [6], [7], and energy [8], [9], among others. Based on various applications and definitions found in literature, the conceptualization of DTs has expanded from representing only industrial objects or products to represent- ing almost any physical or intangible object as well. Most published work suggests that the definition of a DT may vary depending on the application area. For instance, in the initial product design phase, a physical object may not be required for the creation of a DT, but it may be essential for asset management. This perspective reinforces the need for a clearly defined DT and invites the establishment of common ground for different industries and application areas. As presented later in this paper, the railroad industry has made multiple efforts to define and apply the concept of DTs in the context of structural health monitoring of track components, asset management, and mechanical systems. However, as in other sectors, there is still no unified concept, indicating the possibility of ongoing opportunities for further exploration and definition within the industry. The Facility for Accelerated Service Testing (FAST ®) provides continuous monitoring, research, and testing of track and mechanical systems for the North American railroads and offers excellent opportunities to explore the DT concept. The objectives of this paper are to 1) continue the conver- sation regarding exploration of DTs in the North American railroad industry with the goal of defining DTs and 2) present an initial attempt to create a DT for the FAST loop. II. L ITERATURE REVIEW Recently, there has been a growing interest in applying DTs within the railroad industry. This section provides a summary of the literature on DTs in railroads. The review compiles peer- reviewed articles, conference papers, and master’s theses that include terms related to DTs, DE, and railroads in their titles, abstracts, and keywords. On a high level, [10] discusses how DTs can improve the railroad industry by optimizing maintenance planning, design, and operations. In addition, to improve risk analyses, real- time asset health visualizations designed to decrease the time needed to complete technical checks display accurate train makeup to improve efficiency in transshipment preparation, etc. Reference [11] presents a definition for DTs along with an execution plan and the applications of the plan based on stakeholders’ questions regarding railcar information. Refer- ence [12] presents an overview of the current state of DT in railroads, highlighting the fact that the emphasis has been placed in the infrastructure side with limited applications in the rolling stock side. Reference [12] also mentions that the status of DT implementation is in the early stages, suggesting a future focus on predictive analytics, lifecycle management, and real-time performance monitoring. Reference [13] provides a detailed literature review and the state of DTs and the railroad industry. On the mechanical side, [14] considers a real-time freight car braking system designed to identify faults. This study uses both physical and model-based information, employing com- plex equations to replicate specific parts and integrating them with specialized sensors to enhance accuracy. On the infras- tructure side, [15] discusses opportunities for and challenges in automatically generating geometric DTs for track struc- tures. In terms of structural health monitoring, [16] provides applications in bridge health, employing machine learning, the Internet of Things, and cloud technology to enhance monitoring, analysis, and decision-making for bridge main- tenance. Another bridge-related study uses machine learning and three-dimensional (3D) simulation to conduct continu- ous online damage detection on bridges using train-induced responses [17]. For rail yard monitoring, a DT was developed to establish 1) optimal train paths, 2) train make-up, and 3) task orders, with the authors using feedback and corrections from controllers to fine-tune their models [18]. A 3D DT to monitor the structure of railroad tunnels was also created [19]. Addi- tionally, DT applications in track maintenance are explored in a case study on the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa by [20], demonstrating the practicality and potential of DTs in the railroad industry. Reference [21] depicts the application of a DT to predict rail surface defects, such as rolling contact fatigue in heavy haul operations, by integrating longitudinal train simulation, multibody simulation, and traction modeling.
