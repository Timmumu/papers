Subsequently, a methodology for extracting one-dimensional geometric models from point cloud data is described. The proposed safety analysis method is then applied to the retractable roof of the Shenzhen Gymnasium, with a comparison made between two approaches: the LiDAR-based method and the consistent imperfection mode method, in terms of their consideration of the impact of initial geometric imperfections on structural mechanical properties. The research findings reveal that the one-dimensional geometric models extracted from airborne LiDAR measurements can accurately reflect the real-world structural imperfections and can be regarded as geometric digital twins of the structures. In comparison with the consistent imperfection mode method, finite element models based on airborne LiDAR measurement technology offer a superior representation of the real performance of the structures. 1. Introduction The use of large-span spatial structures in large-scale public buildings is increasing, due to the advantages of light weight, excellent mechanical performance, a variety of structural forms, innovative shapes and substantial available space. Examples of such structures include the Shenzhen Gymnasium, Shenzhen Financial and Cultural Centre, Shenzhen Natural History Museum and Shenzhen Reform and Opening Exhibition Hall, which are currently under construction and are about to start construction. However, the large-span spatial structure mostly adopts steel structure, which is susceptible to instability and failure of components or structures. The initial defects of the components during the production and transportation processes, the asymmetrical docking of components, and the deviation of joints caused by the on-site installation process can exacerbate the instability and failure of the steel structure. Consequently, the impact of initial geometric imperfections must be considered during the stability analysis of large-span spatial structures. * Corresponding author. Regarding the influence of initial geometric imperfections on the stability performance of large-span spatial structures, Ma et al. [ 1 ] investigated the effect of different defect shapes and amplitudes on the stability bearing capacity of a stadium roof structure by considering the first three buckling modes and their combinations as the defect distribution. Du [ 2 ] investigated the effect of different defect modes on the overall stability of a roof structure by considering the first six buckling modes as the defect distribution. Wang [ 3 ] investigated the overall stability performance of a portal frame structure system by taking the first elastic buckling mode as the defect distribution. Jiang et al. [ 4 ] investigated the influence of different defect sizes on the overall stability performance of a retractable roof structure using the first buckling mode as the defect distribution. Díaz et al. [ 5 ] investigated the overall stability performance of large-span metal roofs by considering the first mode of buckling analysis as the defect distribution. Liu et al. [ 6 ] proposed a random defect mode superposition method for the stability analysis of single layer reticulated shell structures. This method identifies the most unfavourable defect mode for structural instability. Xu et al. [ 7 ] introduced a reliability-based method for calculating the critical stability load-bearing capacity of single-layer reticulated shell structures and validated its effectiveness through two case studies. Borri et al. [ 8 ] used the random defect mode method to study the influence of the distribution pattern and size of initial geometric im - perfections on the structural stability bearing capacity. Chen et al. [ 9 ] proposed a modelling method for initial geometric imperfections in reticulated shell structures based on random field theory and used it to investigate the effect of these imperfections on the structural stability performance. Furthermore, Cui et al. [ 10 ] proposed a simultaneous autoregressive model capable of simulating spatially varying initial geometric imperfections, including those in irregular reticulated shell structures. As can be seen from the above studies, the main methods currently used to account for initial geometric imperfections are the random defect mode method and the consistent defect mode method. However, it should be noted that the defect modes assumed in these methods are random, whereas in constructed structures the defect mode is deterministic. Therefore, when performing a safety analysis on an existing structure, calculations should be based on the actual defect shape. Digital twins, which serve as digital replicas of real-world assets, can effectively reflect the actual state of structures, including stresses, deformations and geometric imperfections. Many scholars have used three-dimensional (3D) laser scanning technology and reverse modelling techniques to capture geometric imperfections of structures and create geometric digital twins. Sangani et al. [ 11 ] used 3D digital image correlation equipment to scan tubular steel columns with initial geometric defects, and evaluated the residual capacity of tubular steel columns according to the scanned model. Chacon et al. [ 12 ] measured the initial geometric defects of planar steel frames using a terrestrial laser scanner. Selvaraj [ 13 ] and Zhang [ 14 ] demonstrated that 3D laser scanning can be used to measure the initial geometric defects of steel members. Xu et al. [ 15 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to detect the full-field geometric defects of circular steel pipes and investigated the influence of geometric defects on the compressive and flexural strength of circular steel pipes. Tzortzinis et al. [ 16 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to measure the corrosion of bridges and combined 3D laser scanning with finite element to evaluate the residual capacity of steel beams. Ban et al. [ 17 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to measure the size and geometric defects of square hollow section short columns, and investigated the local buckling behaviour of square hollow section short columns. Lazzari et al. [ 18 ] proposed a method for measuring geometric defects and dimensional deviations in GFRP profiles using a 3D contact coordinate measuring machine. Feng et al. [ 19 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to obtain and evaluate initial geometric defects such as prestressed CFRP reinforced columns. Zou et al. [ 20 ] attempted to use 3D laser scanning technology to obtain the initial geometric defects of steel structural components, and proposed a method to build a digital model of the components based on 3D laser point cloud data. Xu et al. [ 21 ] proposed a method to determine the initial geometric defects of steel members and establish a geometric model by using 3D scanning, and proved that this method can better predict the initial geometric defects of steel members. Aktepe et al. [ 22 , 23 ] used a 3D laser scanner to measure the geometric defects of cold-formed thin-walled steel in detail to propose a predictive expression for the initial geometric defects. The experimental results show that the finite element results based on the scanning model are in good agreement with the experimental results. Ma et al.
