This paper presents a novel methodology for the virtual representation of infrastructure vulnerabilities and functional impairments during hazard situations. Thereby, focus is on the mapping of interdependencies among critical infrastructure systems and the cascading effects that can arise from failures within these heterogeneous sectors by means of a digital twin representation. An integration of simulation models for urban infrastructure components, particularly in relation to the built environment and emergency response systems is introduced. Leveraging a modular co-simulation architecture, the framework facilitates the analysis of cascading effects across multiple infrastructure systems, such as water, electricity, gas, and telecommunications. As a proof-of-concept example, urban flooding due to heavy rainfall is considered to illustrate the framework’s capabilities in predicting system states and assessing structural impacts on critical infrastructures as well as consequential ramifications for emergency relief units. The findings contribute valuable insights, thereby advertising the utilization of the presented methodology in decision-making and training resources aiding the enhancement of the resilience of urban environments against both natural and intentional threats. Keywords Digital Urban Twin · Socio-technical systems · Distributed co-simulation · Crisis management 1 Introduction Evaluating the susceptibility of urban regions and their essential infrastructure to both natural disasters—like floods, intense rainfall, as well as severe storms—and human- induced events—such as terrorist attacks—has become a vital area of research in recent years. This is largely due to the global rise in the frequency and severity of these inci- dents. Failures or disruptions in critical infrastructure can lead to serious consequences, including ongoing shortages and major threats to public safety. Of particular concern are the hidden interdependencies and unexpected cascading effects among various critical infrastructure systems, which may endanger citizens. Previous research efforts have identified risks and hazards associated with cascading effects in interconnected infra- structure systems. The authors of Koliou et al. (2018) sum- marize the current state of knowledge and challenges related to community resilience and system interdependencies. 1.1 Research gap Although researchers have documented some interdepend - encies among critical infrastructure systems, these findings have rarely been applied to integrate simulations of the components within an urban system. Some projects have created tools like SAT (Severity Assessment Tool) (Hayes et al. (2022)) and CReDo (Climate Resilience Demonstrator) (Wang et al. (2022)) to assess and visualize these interde- pendencies for decision-making purposes. However, specific functionalities of different critical infrastructures and the built environment are typically modeled through data-driven methods rather than engineering approaches, which may fail to recognize the complexity of cascading effects in disas- ter scenarios. Furthermore, coupled simulations primarily focus on essential infrastructures like water, electricity, and gas networks (e.g., Deshmukh et al. ( 2011); Praks et al. (2015), while interdependencies with the built environment and emergency response systems are often neglected. In this context, there is a need for simulation frameworks that facilitate the integration and communication among inter - disciplinary simulation environments. Previous efforts, such as the Interdependent Networked Community Resilience Modeling Environment (IN-CORE) (Lindt et al. (2023)), mandate the integration of various services, e.g., including analyses of buildings with fragility curves and simulations of infrastructure networks under various hazard scenarios. However, the methods used for simulation are not discussed comprehensively, and emergency response systems, which are critical for effective training, are omitted. Regarding the efficient realization of interdisciplinary co-simulations, Restel (2023) introduces the concept of SimulationOps that proposes a collaboration process (from creating and combining simulation components to deploy - ing, configuring, and running simulations). This process uti- lizes the concept of distributed “as-a-service” platforms to achieve its objectives. Numerous co-simulation models and platforms have been proposed. The most widely recognized concept is the High Level Architecture (HLA), publicly available as a set of standards (IEEE (2010)). This concept describes a federated system of multiple simulators, along with a common runtime infrastructure to facilitate com- munication and synchronization. One example of a simula- tion platform that uses the HLA approach in the domain of mobility and communication is Eclipse Mosaic (Schrab et al. (2023)). HLA-based systems synchronize the steps for a simulation in a global fashion but lack something like an event stream approach allowing for each service to execute its next step independently if the necessary input is avail- able, which can boost runtime performance. The Functional Mock-up Interface (FMI) (Modelica Association (2025)) is a standard for exchanging models for tightly coupled co-sim- ulations based on XML and the C programming language. Maestro (Thule et al. (2019)) is one example utilizing FMI for co-simulating cyber-physical systems. However, FMI does not provide the development and deployment flexibil- ity to support the iterative development, deployment, and usage process. The Cybersecurity & Information Systems Information Analysis Center’s approach to “Modeling & Simulation as a Service” (MSaaS) (Siegfried et al. (2018)) emphasizes the use of cloud computing and service-oriented architectures to deliver modeling and simulation capabilities effectively. “As-a-service” refers to a self-service approach, where users can access and use services on-demand. Gütlein and Djanatliev (2020) presents an architecture for MSaaS, which focuses on Apache Kafka for communication between and coordination of components participating in a simula- tion. In summary, the existing “simulation-as-a-service” approaches already address distribution, on-demand usage, or using diverse programming languages, but do not support the event streaming approach envisioned here. 1.2 Innovation To fill these gaps, a new methodology has been established to enable the virtual representation of infrastructure vulner- ability and functional impairments in urban districts during hazard situations. It is built upon the concepts presented in Martini et al. (2024). Its modular architecture and integra- tion with the Fraunhofer Open Source SensorThings API Server (FROST) (Hertweck et al. (2019)) make it adaptable and capable of being expanded to include additional hazard scenarios and infrastructure components. This versatility enables various applications, such as decision-support tools for authorities and infrastructure operators, as well as train- ing resources in public safety. The framework integrates multiple simulation modules for different critical infrastructures and services, including water, electricity, gas, and telecommunications to form a comprehensive digital twin representation of key infrastruc- tures in urban areas. Each module is built on engineering models that are based on physical principles, standards, or (georeferenced) real-world data, allowing for reliable pre- dictions about the modeled system’s state under certain conditions.
