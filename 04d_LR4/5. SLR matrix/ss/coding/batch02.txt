Related works 2.1 Near-miss management: from traditional to technological approaches and the remaining challenges The critical role of near-miss data in proactive construction safety management has been widely recognized, particularly given the persistently high-risk nature of the construction industry. Grounded in Heinrich’s accident pyramid theory (Heinrich, 1941), which was subsequently expanded by Bird, near-miss incidents are considered fundamental to occupational safety, serving as crucial early indicators of potential hazards. Bird’s extensive analysis of over 1.7 million accident reports revealed a striking ratio: for every fatality, there are approximately 600 near-miss incidents, predominantly triggered by unsafe acts (Bird and Germain, 1966). These near-miss events, often regarded as “unrealized accidents,” provide valuable opportunities for intervention. Proactive management of these incidents can substantially mitigate the likelihood of severe accidents, thereby preventing more serious outcomes (Haas et al., 2020). Despite their significance, traditional approaches to near-miss data collection and analysis are often labor-intensi ve and heavily reliant on manual processes, which inherently limit their efficiency and scalability . This limitation has spurred the development of IT-based solutions designed to enhance near-miss detection and management. Recent advancements include the introduction of automated systems and sensor-based tools for real-time data collection and incident tracking. For example, Aria et al. (2014) successfully tested wearable sensor-ba sed technologies for detecting near- miss falls, while Yang et al. (2014, 2015, 2016) demonstrated the potential of automated systems utilizing inertial measurement technology to identify hazardous movements. Zhang et al. (2019) further developed innovative models integrating mobile technologies and artificial neural networks to identify fall-related near misses. Teizer and Cheng (2015) proposed proximity detection models that employ automated location tracking to assess worker proximity to equipment and site layout hazards. More recently, Wu et al. (2010) introduced real-time RFID-based tracking systems, and Chung et al. (2023) explored IoT-based monitoring solutions for real-time near-miss detection. Nasernejad et al. Significant progress has also been made in the prediction of near-miss incidents. For instance, Kataoka et al. This limitation hinders the ability to anticipate near-misses during their occurrence, thereby preventing truly proactive safety management. In summary, while significant advancements have been made in near-miss detection, data collection, and prediction, critical challenges remain in the construction sector. Traditional methods are limited by their reliance on manual processes, and although IT-driven solutions have improved efficiency, most current approaches are still two-dimensional (operating in planar (x, y) coordinates, lacking vertical (z-axis) analysis, thus missing hazards like overhead crane interactions or falls from height), failing to fully capture the complex, three-dimensional dynamics of construction sites. The chaotic and dynamic nature of construction environments further complicates near-miss prediction, as constant site changes and intricate worker- equipment interactions are difficult to model accurately. Moreover, existing predictive models, while innovative, often focus on post-hoc analysis rather than real-time process prediction. Introduction Rock tunnel construction faces complex and unpredictable geolog- ical conditions that can jeopardize construction safety and affect project efficiency [ 1 – 7 ]. Advanced geological forecasting plays a key role in detecting adverse conditions ahead of the tunnel face [ 8 – 11 ]. However, traditional methods mainly rely on limited data sources and the sub- jective judgments of geological engineers, which often fail to fully capture the inherent uncertainty and complexity of rock mass behavior [ 12 – 15 ]. To address these issues, there is an urgent need for new ap- proaches that integrate multi-source data, enabling a comprehensive and real-time interpretation of geological conditions. In recent years, digital twin technology has emerged as a promising solution in the field of tunnel construction. This technology integrates multi-source hetero- geneous data to construct high-precision virtual geological models and employs intelligent algorithms to enable real-time interactions between physical entities and their digital twins, thereby providing engineering managers with intuitive, dynamic monitoring and early warning capa- bilities. However, most current geological digital twin models lack rigorous mathematical descriptions of the randomness and fuzziness inherent in rock mass parameters and do not establish a complete system for data acquisition, model updating, and decision feedback [ 16 – 18 ]. This limitation restricts their effectiveness in real-time construction applications [ 19 ]. Based on the aforementioned background and technological status, this paper focuses on the following research question: How can a closed- loop digital twin system for Data-Model-Decision be constructed to achieve real-time and accurate decision of advanced geological condi- tions within a 30-m range ahead of the tunnel face during excavation? The “ Data-Model-Decision ” is a concept designed to progress step by step, starting from data collection, followed by virtual model construc- tion, and ultimately supporting decision-making, forming a closed-loop system. To address this issue, the paper proposes a three-step digital twin approach: (1) Multi-source information collection and database con- struction: Geological information, such as water content, lithology, and integrity, is extracted using image processing and other methods. This information is aligned in three-dimensional space and transformed into consistent point cloud data structures stored in a database. (2) Advanced geological virtual model construction: Point cloud models ahead of the tunnel face are generated based on the geometric characteristics of the tunnel face. Geological attributes are assigned to the point cloud model using the KNN interpolation based on information from the geological database. (3) Intelligent decision-making for surrounding rock classifi- cation: The geological attributes of the constructed point cloud models are used as input to a fuzzy cloud probability model to quantify un- certainties in the geological information, allowing for the calculation of rock classification probabilities for each meter ahead of the tunnel face. The classification is determined based on the maximum probability. Advanced geological forecasting in tunnel construction Advanced geological forecasting in tunnel construction aims to identify the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face in advance, effectively preventing accidents caused by adverse geological factors [20,21]. Traditional advanced geological forecasting methods, such as GPR, TSP, TEM, and while-drilling, use different physical principles to obtain information about the physical properties of the geology ahead of the tunnel face [22–24]. Under normal conditions, the effective detec- tion ranges are as follows: GPR (15-30 m), TSP (100-150 m), TEM (30- 60 m), and while-drilling measurements (3-15 m). These methods pro- vide foundational data for assessing geological conditions, but due to the complexity of geology, a single method often fails to capture the un- certainty and complexity of the geology [25]. Currently, tunnel con- struction sites mainly rely on geological engineers to subjectively assess the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face based on information from advanced geological forecasting. There is a lack of a method that can automatically make decisions regarding the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face based on the data provided by advanced geological forecasting. Therefore, researchers have begun to explore the integration of multi-source data to make tunnel geology more trans- parent, such as combining GPR with drilling to complement the ad- vantages of different methods. Additionally, in recent years, computer technology has been increasingly applied to geological forecasting, utilizing data-driven methods to extract valuable patterns from histori- cal data and improve the accuracy of geological condition predictions [26]. Wang et al. proposed a semi-supervised learning model based on while-drilling data, which combines dense convolutional autoencoders and hybrid deep neural networks. This approach significantly improved the accuracy and stability of surrounding rock discontinuity detection [27]. Xue et al. adopted a novel spatiotemporal sequence approach to interpret the continuously exposed rock mass during tunnel excavation. They proposed a spatially aware recurrent neural network that uses historical fracture maps as input to predict the unexcavated sections [28]. Hansen et al. proposed a machine learning method that uses while- drilling data to predict the rock type ahead of the tunnel face [29]. These new methods have significantly improved the accuracy and real-time performance of advanced geological forecasting. However, challenges remain in effectively integrating multiple data sources and handling uncertainties. 2.2. Digital twin in tunnel construction Digital twin technology is an emerging technology that has rapidly developed in the engineering field in recent years. By creating a virtual replica of a physical entity, it connects real-time data from the physical world with virtual models, enabling dynamic reflection and prediction of physical processes [30–33]. In tunnel construction, digital twin technology can integrate geological, structural, and construction data in real time to create a dynamic virtual model of the construction site. This allows engineers to identify potential risks in a virtual environment and optimize construction plans [34–36]. The advantage of digital twin technology lies in its ability to simulate and predict the constantly changing geological conditions during tunnel construction with high precision, while also offering powerful data analysis and visualization capabilities [37]. Zhang et al. installed Internet of Things sensors on TBM to transfer the TBM’s actual operating status from the physical object to the digital model [38]. Ye et al. pro- posed a digital twin-based multi-information intelligent early warning and safety management platform. This platform sets up communication lines within the tunnel using multifunctional base stations to automat- ically collect and transmit multi-source data. It then uses this data to virtually simulate dynamic construction conditions and surrounding rock behaviors [39]. Ling et al. integrated digital twin technology into the implementation of rock tunnel Just-In-Time design and proposed a scene-entity-relationship-event-control modeling approach. This new method serves as a cross-domain sharing and reuse model for digital twin applications [40]. Wu et al. proposed a multi-level voxel repre- sentation method based on linear segmentation, which efficiently up- dates and expresses the complex features of the tunnel geological environment, providing real-time dynamic support for digital twin ap- plications [41]. However, the construction of digital twins relies on the efficient acquisition and integration of multi-source data, which de- mands high data processing capabilities and modeling accuracy. Addi- tionally, the geological environment in rock tunnel construction is highly complex and uncertain, making it a challenge to quantify these uncertainties and make intelligent decisions based on geological con- ditions in the context of digital twin applications. 2.3. Cloud model in tunnel engineering applications The cloud model, a mathematical tool for addressing uncertainty and fuzziness, has been widely used in tunnel engineering applications [42]. The core advantage of the cloud model lies in its ability to convert be- tween qualitative concepts and quantitative descriptions, making it particularly well-suited for capturing the variability and uncertainty of geological conditions [43]. In tunnel construction, the geological con- ditions are often influenced by multiple factors, such as lithology, water content, and structural integrity. The interactions between these factors are complex and characterized by high uncertainty [44–47]. The cloud model uses membership functions and probability distri- butions to transform the multi-dimensional features of surrounding rock conditions into a comprehensive probability cloud map, providing en- gineers with an intuitive basis for risk assessment. Compared to tradi- tional single-source risk assessment methods, the advantage of the cloud model lies in its ability to effectively quantify both fuzziness and randomness, thereby enhancing the accuracy and dynamic nature of geological condition evaluation. Sun et al. proposed a dynamic risk assessment system that combines a two-dimensional cloud model for risk early warning in tunnel construction. This approach effectively handles the uncertainties in geological conditions and provides a reli- able decision-making basis for the construction process [48]. Chen et al. proposed a hybrid method that combines an improved cloud model with a Bayesian network for dynamic safety analysis and assessment of adjacent buildings impacted by tunnel construction [49]. Tian et al. integrated resilience theory with a combined weighted-cloud model to assess the flood resilience of metro stations [50]. Zhu et al. proposed a cloud model-based random forest method to address inconsistencies in mapping monitoring data to the health rate of metro tunnels [51]. In this study, the cloud model is improved by incorporating fuzzy mathematics theory, resulting in the development of a fuzzy cloud probability model. This model is used to quantify the uncertainty of the constructed geological digital twins and make intelligent decisions regarding sur- rounding rock classification based on probabilities, providing crucial support for safety decision-making in rock tunnel construction. 3. Introduction Tunnels undergo structural degradation over time due environ - mental conditions, aging, lining excessive loads, and uncertainties in geotechnical properties ( Baji et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2017; Montero et al., 2015 ). Deterioration in the tunnel lining undermines its integrity and may increase the risk of tunnel lining to eventually collapse ( Jiang et al., 2019 ). The inspection of these structures is a crucial process to comprehend how the impact of these factors impairs the structure and to manage maintenance. Conventional inspection methodologies exhibit known disadvan - tages, including lower time efficiency and challenges in precisely quantifying and locate defects ( Koch et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2022 ). To improve the inspection practice, several manuals and standards have a specific classification system which classifies the tunnel condition with different approaches and level of deterioration ( Poncetti et al., 2025 ) (Table A.1). In general, the inspection practices and the condition classification of tunnels rely on visual inspections, with the documentation done in spreadsheets in the field, creating high subjectivity and dependency on the inspector ’ s experience and attention during the defect and condition documentation. Tunnels are critical infrastructures that significantly contribute to the economic and social well-being of populations and play a vital role in the development of transportation networks ( Petroutsatou et al., 2021 ). Regular inspections are essential to monitor the progression of structural pathologies over time, enabling the evaluation of both structural integrity and serviceability ( Sandroni & Labiouse, 2011 ). If left unde - tected, such deteriorations may lead to severe structural degradation, potentially shortening the tunnel service life, disrupting traffic flow, and compromising user safety. These failures often result in economic losses and require costly remediation measures, while also increasing the risk of failure ( McKibbins et al., 2009 ). To reduce the problems in the current tunnel inspection procedures, some researches intend to improve the practices by suggesting novel methods for structural condition evaluation ( Ai & Yuan, 2018 ), opti - mizing the inspection frequency ( Ai et al., 2020 ), or applying technology to the inspection practices, such as using Non-destructive methods ( Llanca et al., 2017 ) and robots ( Menendez et al., 2018 ) to improve the data acquisition, and using Digital Twins (DT) for a better documenta - tion and visualization of the inspection activity. A DT can be defined as a continuously evolving digital representation of the historical and real-time behavior of a physical asset or process ( Dan et al., 2022; Glaessgen & Stargel, 2012 ). Constructed using real measurements and layers of field data acquisition, digital twins offer significant potential for infrastructure management ( Parrott and War - shaw, 2017 ). However, realizing this potential entails challenges, particularly in managing data heterogeneity. Integrating and synchro - nizing data from diverse sources is a complex and time-intensive task, which, as noted by Q. Lu et al. (2020) increases the risk of the digital twin becoming outdated. Additionally, while continuous inspection and monitoring practices generate larger datasets that enhance accuracy, they also demand more advanced data management systems. In the context of tunnel inspection, monitoring, and evaluation, digital twins can significantly improve inspection practices and overall infrastructure management. An iterative virtual model for generating a tunnel DT able to interact with inspection and monitoring database enhances the tunnel evalua - tion and documentation. This interactive Digital Twin is possible by integrating Point Cloud Data (PCD), parametric modeling, and proce - dural modeling techniques. PCD enables precise tridimensional repre - sentations, as demonstrated in studies such as Zhang et al. (2014) and R. Lu & Brilakis (2019) . Furthermore, Sun (2011) presents image acquisi - tion for detecting deformations and defects in circular shield tunnels using mobile laser scanning, focusing on automated DT creation with high detailed geometry and damage integration. To evaluate the geometrical and structural integrity of tunnels, Loprencipe et al. (2018) combined laser scanning, thermographic imaging, and ground- penetrating radar (GPR) with implementation scripts developed specif - ically for clearance verification. Parametric modeling provides a robust approach for generating flexible geometric models, emphasizing the sequence of mathematical operations and the model construction pro - cess, focusing on associative construction rather than on the final outcome ( Mafipour et al., 2023; Schultz et al., 2017 ). While current inspection practices using laser scanning, photo - grammetry, and manual reporting provide valuable data, they often suffer from fragmented workflows, limited automation, and high subjectivity in defect interpretation and quantification ( Insa-Iglesias et al., 2021; Stentoumis et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2021 ). Additionally, these approaches frequently lack seamless integration between data acquisition and model generation, hindering updates and reuse of models throughout the asset lifecycle. This disconnect significantly limits the implementation of Digital Twin methodologies, which depend on continuous synchronization between physical infrastructure and its digital counterpart, particularly in complex environments such as the irregular geometry of the Coastal Tunnel. Understanding that three-dimensional reconstruction and the auto - mation of structural defect identification and quantification are essential for enhancing inspection quality and meeting infrastructure needs, this paper focuses on implementing parametric modeling and field data extraction to automate modeling processes, improve inspection accu - racy, and create a structured databank for the lifecycle management of tunnel digital twins. The objective of this study is to develop an auto - mated procedural modeling framework for tunnel inspection DT, aiming to integrate heterogeneous data sources and support lifecycle manage - ment through improved digital inspection and classification capabilities. The workflow integrates field data obtained from detailed inspection reports and structural scans as inputs for a Grasshopper 1.0.0007 script. It involves surface creation, adaptive subdivision into an inspection grid, and automated classification of areas based on digital inspection data. Metadata containing relevant information is then assigned to areas of interest. The methodology was validated through a real-world application in the Coastal Tunnel in Brazil. This approach enabled virtual inspections and established a virtualization environment that seamlessly integrates real data with detailed modeling. It allows the full execution of the in - spection and classification campaign digitally within the scanned area, enabling updates and refinements based on acquired data and developed programming. By reducing subjectivity and incorporating Industry 4.0 methodologies, this work represents a significant advancement in tunnel management practices. 2. Inspection data generation The standardization of inspection practices has a global scope, with various companies responsible for infrastructure while administrative associations pursue an ideal methodology ( ADIF, 2021; CeTU, 2015; FHWA, 2015; Yuan et al., 2012 ). Commonly applied practice involves categorizing areas within the structure using defect-sensitive classifi - cation. Response data plays a crucial role in guiding and enhancing the decision-making system. Machado & Futai (2024) present a methodol - ogy to create a virtual representation for the inspection data ( Fig. 2 ). The inspection process involves creating a grid that divides the tunnel surface into zones: the roof, the right wall, and the left wall. Introduction Internal leakage represents a leading factor in earth dam failures, a risk exacerbated by increasingly extreme hydro-climatic events ( ICOLD, 2019 ; Moreno-Rodenas et al., 2025 ; Nasser et al., 2025 ). While methods such as Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) ( Bekele et al., 2024 ; Dong et al., 2020 ) and Distributed Optical Fiber Temperature Sensing (DTS) ( Bekele et al., 2024 , 2025 ) characterize internal structures effectively, they are operationally complex for routine monitoring. Electrical Re - sistivity Tomography (ERT) offers a practical alternative. Electrical conductivity is a sensitive indicator of structural integrity ( Guo et al., 2022 ; Xiao et al., 2024 ; Zhang et al., 2026 ), highly responsive to mois - ture variations and leakage. The technique operates by injecting direct current through source electrodes and measuring the resulting potential differences to reconstruct the internal conductivity distribution. Reconstructing this distribution requires solving a highly non-linear, ill-posed inverse problem constrained by the Poisson equation ( Loke & Barker, 1996 ). Conventional methods typically enforce smoothness constraints to stabilize the inversion ( Constable et al., 1987 ; Liu et al., 2021 ). However, this regularization tends to smooth out the sharp boundaries of early-stage leakage and masks high-contrast anomalies. Despite recognition of this topological mismatch ( Chan & Tai, 2004 ; Ishizu et al., 2025 ), accurately resolving such distinct targets within heterogeneous backgrounds remains difficult, especially when using reduced-order parameterizations. To mitigate the computational burden of stochastic inversion for earth dams, recent research has focused on two main directions: algo - rithmic acceleration and surrogate modeling. Fast direct solvers expe - dite Jacobian matrix calculations ( Ambikasaran et al., 2013 ; Saibaba & Kitanidis, 2012 ); however, high-fidelity probabilistic characterization remains expensive due to the cumulative demand of repeated forward simulations. Standard surrogate modeling frameworks project high-dimensional parameters onto truncated linear subspaces, often employing the Karhunen-Lo `eve (KL) expansion ( Ghanem & Spanos, 1991 ) combined with shallow regressors such as Gaussian Processes ( Rasmussen & Williams, 2006 ). Deep learning provides a powerful tool for inverse modeling and structural health assessment. PINNs perform effec - tively in various fields ( Guo et al., 2023 ; Haghighat et al., 2021 ; Ji et al., 2024 ). Yet, because they function as solvers for specific instances rather than learning the general solution operator, each new inversion task mandates retraining from scratch. Furthermore, optimization frequently fails from ill-conditioned loss landscapes ( Karniadakis et al., 2021 ; Krishnapriyan et al., 2021 ; Wang et al., 2021 ). Operator learning frameworks extend neural networks to infinite-dimensional function spaces. Prominent examples include the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) ( Li et al., 2021 ) and spectral graph networks ( Yin et al., 2026 ). The FNO efficiently captures the global dependencies of the steady-state Poisson equation by parameterizing the integral kernel within the frequency domain. This spectral convolution ensures resolution-invariant forward modeling, overcoming the grid-dependence of standard convolutional networks ( Kaewnuratchadasorn et al., 2024 ; Kovachki et al., 2023 ). Applying these operators to ill-posed inverse problems remains chal - lenging. Most existing neural-operator frameworks execute a direct, unconstrained inverse mapping from measurements to physical prop - erties ( Long et al., 2025 ). This unparameterized approach inherently diffuses sharp topological boundaries under the severe ill-posedness of ERT ( Molinaro et al., 2023 ). While classical PDE optimization exten - sively employs geometric parameterizations to maintain sharp in - terfaces, integrating such explicit structural priors into data-driven neural operator frameworks remains underexplored, particularly for the severely ill-posed ERT inverse problem. The absence of topological priors leaves gradient-based optimization vulnerable to local minima, causing failures in identifying localized leakage anomalies. This framework integrates a decoupled geometric parame - terization to disentangle anomalies from stochastic soil backgrounds. Driven by a differentiable FNO surrogate, this topology- guided workflow enables efficient gradient-based optimization, bypassing the computational bottlenecks of conventional finite element solvers. Sensitivity to statistical prior mismatch The final phase of the robustness evaluation examines the sensitivity to statistical prior mismatch. The baseline framework is trained exclu - sively on random background fields with a spatial correlation length of 5.0 m. This model is directly applied to two independent sets of 100 samples featuring unseen correlation lengths of 7.0 m and 3.0 m without parameter fine-tuning. For the smoother background condition of 7.0 m, the inversion metrics slightly exceed the standard baseline. The model achieves a Mean IoU of 0.7407 and an Acc 0.5 of 92%. This demonstrates robust out-of-distribution generalization when transferring the model to spatially simpler domains. The 3.0 m correlation length introduces highly fragmented background heterogeneities. These intense spatial variations generate background potential fluctuations that often mask the actual leakage signals. Consequently, the high-fidelity detection rate Acc 0.5 decreases to 87%, and the Mean IoU drops to 0.6942. The framework nevertheless maintains a 97% Acc 0 , successfully isolating the general defect zone despite the severe background noise. These testing outcomes reveal an asymmetric generalization behavior. The surrogate model adapts readily to smoother environments but experiences a slight precision loss in more chaotic physical fields. For practical field de - ployments, the training dataset should utilize smaller correlation lengths to encompass severe background variations, ensuring optimal down - ward compatibility across diverse geological conditions. In real-world dam monitoring scenarios, the latent dimension d bg and correlation length l c strongly dictate the inversion difficulty. These geological priors must not be arbitrarily assumed. They must be established through historical design records and site-specific material sampling. This empirical validation forms a reliable baseline prior to executing the inversion algorithm. 4.5. Computational and practical challenges in field deployment While transitioning to fully three-dimensional inversion is a logical step for comprehensive dam monitoring, two-dimensional longitudinal profiling remains the main and practical setup for routine, continuous safety assessment of long embankments ( Rahimi et al., 2025 ). Extending the proposed operator learning framework to 3D environments faces significant mathematical and computational challenges, chiefly the curse of dimensionality. The standard three-dimensional FNO architec - ture increases training time complexity to ( N 3 log N ), which requires large amounts of memory during network backpropagation on typical engineering hardware ( Li & Ye, 2025 ). Additionally, capturing the spatial variance of a 3D background field requires a vastly expanded latent parameter space. Integrating this expanded representation with 3D geometric coordinates creates a highly complex, non-convex opti - mization landscape, significantly increasing the probability of local minima entrapment. Maintaining the current sub-second inference ef - ficiency and localization accuracy in 3D necessitates more advanced, memory-efficient operator architectures. Beyond dimensional extensions, field deployment faces a funda - mental simulation-to-reality gap related to boundary conditions. Neural operators approximate Green's functions mathematically, making the surrogate very sensitive to changes in boundary conditions. Achieving out-of-distribution generalization across different cross-sections remains a core challenge. However, operational dam monitoring generally in - volves static structural profiles and predictable environmental states. Therefore, training a universal operator is unnecessary for this specific use. A practical solution is to pre-train a small set of targeted operators tailored to known seasonal or operational boundary conditions. Using these condition-specific surrogates during field inspections effectively overcomes the boundary mismatch issue at a minimal offline compu - tational cost. The proposed 2D framework provides a computationally efficient and practical foundation for operational digital twins. 5. Conclusion This study introduces the TG-FNO framework to address the conflict between smoothness regularization and the sharp topological bound - aries of leakage anomalies in electrical resistivity tomography. By combining decoupled geometric parameterization with a topology- guided warm start, the method explicitly separates stochastic back - ground heterogeneity from deterministic structural defects. This repre - sentation aligns with structural health monitoring needs, which focus on localizing anomalies against known geological backgrounds. The FNO surrogate acts as a high-fidelity alternative to traditional finite element methods. It accurately captures complex non-linear mappings with a mean relative error of 3.18% and achieves a 345 × speedup in forward modeling. This effectively removes computational bottlenecks in sto - chastic inversion. Guided by this surrogate and topological initializa - tion, TG-FNO overcomes the issue of local minima stagnation commonly seen in baseline approaches. Repeated trials confirm its statistical sta - bility and accuracy. The framework achieves a mean Inversion Success Rate ( Acc 0 ) of 99.7% ± 0.6% and a high-fidelity reconstruction rate ( Acc 0.5 ) of 89.3% ± 2.1%, outperforming conventional methods. Addi - tionally, it maintains an overall 72 × computational acceleration over FEM-based inversion. This computational efficiency facilitates rapid routine monitoring, a critical requirement for timely decision-making in dam safety. The framework also shows robust out-of-distribution generalization. Without retraining, it accurately resolves incipient leakage signatures at a 10-fold contrast ratio and severe adverse con - ditions, including 0.20 Gaussian noise and 50% sensor data loss. This confirms that the learned operator approximates the underlying physical laws rather than merely memorizing statistical patterns. Current limi - tations define three primary research directions. First, explicit parame - terization restricts detection to predefined shapes. Future work will adopt implicit representations, such as level set methods, for multi- anomaly scenarios. Second, the underlying Fourier transform is sus - ceptible to spectral truncation. Integrating the synchrosqueezed wavelet transform ( Li et al., 2017 ; Perez-Ramirez et al., 2016 ) will address this by improving localized feature resolution. Finally, the simulation-to-reality gap limits direct field deployment. Because neural operators approximate Green's functions, they remain highly sensitive to physical boundary variations. Future implementations must mitigate this by developing libraries of condition-specific operators to accom - modate seasonal boundary shifts. Together, these advancements will build a practical foundation for operational digital twins in structural health monitoring. Introduction A Smart City is an urban area that uses advanced information and communication technologies (ICT) to improve the quality of life of its residents, optimize resource usage, improve the efficiency of urban services and promote sustainability. It integrates data from various sources, such as IoT (Internet of Things) devices and sensors, to enable real-time monitoring, predictive analytics, and informed decision-making. Additionally, Smart Cities foster citizen engagement and collaboration, ensuring that technological advancements align with the needs and well-being of the community. These technologies are integrated into key sectors, such as transportation, waste management, energy, security, and infrastructure, to create a more sustainable, efficient, and livable environment [1]. Smart Cities and smart buildings are closely related, sharing the common goal of improving efficiency, sustainability, and quality of life through advanced technologies. Smart buildings are critical components of Smart Cities, playing a pivotal role in enhancing efficiency, sustainability, and urban livability [2]. These structures leverage advanced technologies such as IoT, AI (Artificial Intelligence), and automation to monitor and optimize energy consumption, improve occupant comfort, and ensure operational efficiency. By incorporating real-time data analysis and adaptive management systems, they help reduce the environmental footprint of cities while fostering safety and convenience for their users. ∗ Corresponding author. Smart buildings are fully integrated with urban infrastructure via communication networks and IoT-enabled sensors. These systems collect and share data on various operational aspects, including energy usage, climate control, and security, which is then aggregated into the city network. This integration optimizes resource allocation and enhances public services. For instance, building’s energy consumption can be dynamically adjusted based on the city’s overall demand, contributing to electrical grid stability and efficiency [3,4]. One key area of focus within Smart Cities is safety. Buildings with established safety, evacuation, and environmental monitoring plans contribute significantly to urban security [5]. This paper focuses on this crucial aspect, offering supportive tools aimed at providing robust, proactive solutions that directly influence the safety and planning efficiency of first responder teams operating in emergency or high-risk scenarios. These solutions facilitate early detection of incidents or risk assessment and monitoring, enabling a swift return to normalcy and efficient organization of security forces in the event of a disaster. In this context, virtualization through digital twin technology has emerged as an effective tool for transforming urban emergency services, offering faster information and more efficient operations. Integrating monitoring systems to measure relevant environmental variables and asset management strategies are key aspects designed and evaluated in this work to enhance operational efficiency. Various studies have explored the effectiveness of monitoring in emergency situations [6–11]. According to the report on emergency services and fire-related fatalities in Spain, published by Fundación Mapfre in collaboration with the Asociación Profesional de Técnicos de Bomberos (APTB) [12], there were 235 fire and explosion-related fatalities in 2022. Of these, 220 were fire-related deaths, with 176 occurring in residential buildings. These fatalities are often caused by direct exposure to fire, smoke inhalation, burns, stress, or difficulty escaping enclosed spaces. Other common risks during emergencies include gas leaks, such as ammonia, or explosions. Therefore, action and evacuation plans must be highly accurate, emphasizing coordination and communication, along with real-time information about the incident. In recent years, the concept of Smart Cities has expanded to incorporate more advanced strategies for urban safety and emergency response, particularly through the integration of digital twin technologies. These platforms enable synchronized multi- modal emergency monitoring and real-time visualization of urban infrastructure. Recent studies have demonstrated how digital twins can be applied in civil and infrastructure emergency management [13] and in fire safety within smart buildings [14]. Moreover, real- time digital twins are gaining relevance as foundational technologies for the next generation of networked systems, such as 6G and beyond, enabling ultra-reliable low-latency communication essential in time-critical scenarios like emergency response [15]. These developments highlight the growing trend toward integrated, intelligent platforms for situational awareness and decision-making in public safety applications, underscoring the relevance of our proposed platform. One of the main objectives of the system presented here is to provide accessible tools for intervention teams facing emergency scenarios, minimizing response times. This, in turn, reduces the risk of incidents or casualties, both among civilians and emergency responders. Technologies supporting first responder teams have been explored and evaluated in this work, including solutions presented in [16,17], which align with our research focus. Both studies highlight the importance of leveraging technology to enhance environmental monitoring for strategic oversight, both in emergencies and during normal conditions. Other solutions, such as those described in [18], also utilize such resources to provide alternatives. This work explores the impact of employing Building Information Modeling (BIM) methodologies to facilitate information access and operation visualization. BIM enables building virtualization, creating a comprehensive database of assets and installations, accessible and updated in real-time [19]. The creation of a building’s digital twin involves establishing information exchange standards throughout its lifecycle, with ISO 19650 [20] being the relevant European standard. The Universidad Rey Juan Carlos (URJC) has experience in building digitization, both for its campuses and external entities, through collaborative projects [16]. This digitization process involves laser scanning and 3D modeling, providing detailed information on numerous objects and assets, including their material types, models, etc. This data can be further enhanced with information about the building’s use and condition (occupancy, schedules, emergency exits, fire extinguishers, evacuation routes, emergency plans, etc.). Beyond the digital twin, the integration of real-time data from physical variables, such as temperature or CO2 levels in different building areas, is crucial for emergency management. Real-time visualization allows remote area monitoring and segmentation, supporting efficient operational responses. Achieving this requires a flexible software platform capable of processing data from multiple sources, capturing, storing, and displaying sensor and multimedia content (videos, photos). This approach would help better categorize emergencies and establish an environmental and visual media repository. To implement this, IoT devices equipped with sensors and actuators create an interconnected network using various wireless communication technologies such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and LoRa. All this information must be efficiently transmitted, managed, and stored by the platform, ensuring rapid and intuitive user access through a data integration platform and a web interface that visualizes the digital twin, including graphs and other relevant information. This work introduces GUIDE2FR, a platform designed for data acquisition and visualization, tested at the firefighter training tower located at the Unified Security Center (CUS) in Alcorcón (Madrid). Sensor beacons were deployed, and real emergency scenarios were simulated during firefighter training exercises, allowing the Advanced Command Post (PMA) to make informed decisions in line with emergency service protocols. These sensor beacons can be deployed autonomously, manually or pre-intervention as if they were the building’s own monitoring systems. Flexibility and scalability are two of the great advantages of this system, as it adapts to existing and new information systems. Besides real-time visualization, the platform offers environmental parameter prediction capabilities, such as CO2 levels, using machine learning techniques. In summary, GUIDE2FR provides significant innovations compared to existing commercial solutions: full integration of real- time environmental and structural data, predictive capabilities through AI, flexible and scalable architecture, and synchronized Internet of Things 34 (2025) 101768 2 M.D. Álvaro et al. sensor data and video visualization. Being largely open-source, it offers greater customization and adaptability, making it a more comprehensive and effective tool for urban emergency management. Methodology for smart building solutions in emergency scenarios The GUIDE2FR platform combines hardware and software elements to manage and display information. On the hardware side, the system includes sensors and gateways that support various communication protocols. The software tools are organized into multiple layers: a data integration layer and a visualization layer, using web tools to display the digital twin and related data. Each component is described in the following subsections. 2.1. Sensor layer — Beacon systems It consists of a distributed system of beacons integrating smart IoT-based sensors. This setup provides data on various physical variables that need to be measured, such as temperature, humidity, CO2, and total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), as well as other parameters depending on the building’s usage and characteristics. This information can be obtained from various sources, such as sensors strategically placed in specific locations, and can utilize different wireless communication protocols. In this case, both Wi-Fi and LoRa communication technologies have been considered. Additionally, this array of sensors is complemented by strategically placed cameras used for video recording and monitoring critical areas. For instance, cameras with RTSP (Real-Time Streaming Protocol [21]) and Wi-Fi connectivity have been implemented in this work. RTSP is an application-level protocol used in surveillance systems and IP cameras to control real-time video and audio streaming. It allows communication between media servers and client devices. Several types of solutions will be validated in this study: • Beacons comprising sensors will be pre-positioned across various spaces within the building. • Beacons will be deployed by first responder teams at the onset of an emergency. • Beacons will be autonomously distributed by ground or aerial autonomous agents (robots or UAVs) to minimize human intervention. 2.1.1. DBEMERGENCY beacons During the proof of concept, the beacons were designed and implemented in collaboration with Deep-Insight to meet the specific requirements of the platform. These beacons are environmental monitoring devices optimized for emergency environments, featuring a modern IoT architecture. Under normal conditions, the beacons connect to the building’s power supply and Wi-Fi network, transmitting air quality and environmental parameter data. In emergency situations, such as power or communication outages, they continue to operate using internal batteries and LoRa communication, facilitated by an EVA (a custom-designed device that serves as a remote access unit equipped with LoRa and Internet interface). The development and implementation of platforms like GUIDE2FR are particularly crucial in environments where pre-existing connectivity is either insufficient or entirely unavailable. In many emergency or high-risk scenarios — such as firefighter training towers, remote industrial facilities, or large public buildings — the absence of reliable communication networks can significantly hinder monitoring and response efforts. Therefore, establishing a dedicated communication and monitoring network in these areas is vital for the safety and efficiency of emergency operations. The beacon system in GUIDE2FR addresses this challenge by creating its own robust communication infrastructure. This is achieved through the integration of dual communication protocols: Wi-Fi and LoRa. In areas where traditional Wi-Fi networks are unavailable or compromised, the LoRa protocol ensures continuous, long-range, and low-power data transmission. This capability allows the beacons to maintain reliable connectivity across extensive areas or in buildings with structural barriers that could impede standard wireless signals. By establishing a self-sustained network, the system ensures that crucial environmental data — such as temperature, humidity, and air quality parameters — are continuously monitored and transmitted. This information is essential for first responders and emergency teams, as it provides real-time insights into hazardous conditions and informs critical decision-making process ... management practices in smart buildings. The platform not only addresses current limitations but also sets a new standard for integrating real-time data, predictive analytics, and flexible architectures in urban emergency scenarios. Despite the promising results, the proposed platform has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the current implementation relies on ArcGIS for 3D geospatial representation, which may limit portability or require licensing in certain deployment scenarios. As future work, the development of digital twin platforms based on open-source libraries and frameworks could offer more flexible and cost-effective alternatives, fostering wider adoption in diverse contexts. Second, the platform assumes the availability of a reliable wireless network infrastructure within the building, which may not always be the case during an emergency or in older facilities. Additionally, sensor calibration and performance in extreme environments (e.g., high temperature, smoke, or humidity) can impact data accuracy and model reliability. Finally, adapting the system to different building typologies or jurisdictions may require reconfiguration of sensor networks, integration with local emergency protocols, or adaptation to region- specific cloud and privacy requirements. These limitations point to important directions for future research and development to improve the platform’s robustness and generalizability. 5. Conclusions This work has demonstrated that the GUIDE2FR platform is effective for acquiring, storing, and visualizing real-world information using a digital twin. Furthermore, the platform can handle various information sources and effectively combine them to assess emergency situations. GUIDE2FR introduces several significant innovations compared to current commercial solutions for building emergency monitor- ing and management. Firstly, GUIDE2FR provides comprehensive real-time data integration, combining environmental and structural parameters within a single digital twin interface. This integrated visualization capability enables faster and more accurate decision- making by intervention teams. Secondly, our platform incorporates machine learning techniques for predicting critical parameters, such as CO2 levels and total volatile organic compounds (TVOC), an advanced feature not commonly available in commercial solutions. Additionally, GUIDE2FR’s architecture is highly flexible and scalable, allowing for the easy integration of new sensors and systems, unlike commercial solutions that tend to be less adaptable. Another innovative aspect is the synchronized visualization of sensor data and real-time videos using a custom Grafana plugin, facilitating comprehensive emergency situation monitoring. Lastly, most GUIDE2FR components are open-source, enabling greater customization and adaptation to specific user needs—a substantial advantage over less flexible, proprietary commercial solutions. These features demonstrate that GUIDE2FR not only matches but also surpasses the capabilities of existing commercial solutions, offering a more complete and effective tool for urban emergency management. Therefore, this work could be implemented and integrated into the university’s management web system for preventive emergency management, opening up the possibility for research and the design of new control systems to manage more complex environments in real time. The modularity and reliance on open-source technologies such as Fiware, Grafana, MySQL, and OpenVidu facilitate the customiza- tion and deployment of GUIDE2FR in diverse environments, ensuring interoperability with existing emergency response systems. While the current GIS visualization layer relies on ArcGIS, future research will explore the adoption of open-source alternatives, with the goal of increasing system portability and eliminating dependencies on proprietary software. Future work will expand this study to integrate the digital twin with more real assets. This will allow for active response and the integration of all possible emergency assets to establish effective evacuation mechanisms. The research group is already developing related work, focusing on energy efficiency monitoring in public and private building services, given the growing importance of IoT-based monitoring in the context of energy efficiency [30]. Furthermore, to overcome current limitations related to platform dependencies, future developments will also explore the use of open-source libraries and frameworks for digital twin visualization and management. In response, recent practice has introduced digital twins (DT) to fuse video, sensors, and BIM and thus improve site visibility; however, most implementations remain at monitoring/visualization, lacking a mechanism to convert cognition into exe- cutable, verifiable decisions. Introduction The construction industry, a cornerstone of global eco- nomic development, has long struggled with inefficiencies, frequent accidents, and substantial resource waste. Over the past two decades, productivity growth in construction has significantly lagged behind other sectors, with global labor productivity increasing by only 10% from 2000 to 2022 – just one-fifth of the global average (Construction Dive, n.d.). Large-scale projects routinely face overruns: 98% exceed their budgets by 30%, and 77% experience schedule delays of approximately 40% (Linesight, n.d.). Safety performance remains a concern, with construction accounting for nearly 20% of all U.S. workplace fatalities in 2021 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, n.d.). Moreover, the sector is hindered by fragmented workflows and perva- sive information silos that impair collaboration and data sharing (McKinsey, n.d.). Despite growing recognition of the need for digitalization, adoption remains low; between 2005 and 2014, the technology adoption rate in construc - tion was just 1.4% – far below sectors like Information and Communication Technology (ICT) (Madubuike et al., 2022). These persistent challenges highlight the urgent need for transformative technologies to enhance efficiency, safety, and decision-making. Among emerging solutions, digital twins are increas- ingly used to resolve data fragmentation and support re- al-time decisions. A digital twin synchronizes physical as- sets and processes with a virtual model through sensor networks and IoT, consistent with cyber-physical systems principles (Madubuike et al., 2022). Although adoption in construction is still early, results across the lifecycle are 2026 Volume 32 Issue 3 Pages 374–393 https://doi.org/10.3846/jcem.2026.26154 Journal of Civil Engineering and Management, 2026, 32(3), 374–393 375 encouraging. At Frasers Tower in Singapore, more than 900 sensors and 179 Bluetooth beacons support real-time building operations. During construction, reported uses include real-time workflow monitoring (Wang & Chien, 2014), structural health tracking (Wang et al., 2024), and risk warning (Wang et al., 2021), enabling automated prog- ress verification, continuous safety oversight, and more ef- ficient allocation of labor and equipment. Yet most de- ployments remain monitor-centric. They consolidate data but rarely produce decisions that formally respect safe- ty boundaries, resource coupling, and spatial precedence. In recent years, foundation models in artificial intel- ligence – particularly those based on the Transformer ar - chitecture – have achieved remarkable progress, offering promising opportunities to enhance perception and intel- ligent decision-making in the construction industry. Cen- tered on the attention mechanism, Transformer networks excel in multimodal representation and complex temporal learning. Since their initial introduction (Xu et al., 2023), they have consistently set new benchmarks across fields such as natural language processing and computer vision. Representative large-scale models, including BERT, GPT, and the Vision Transformer, can automatically learn hier - archical features and patterns from massive multimodal datasets, demonstrating strong potential for cross-modal fusion, semantic reasoning, and decision support. In the construction domain, Transformer-based methods have been applied to video-based recognition of workers and equipment, sensor time-series prediction, and optimiza- tion of scheduling and logistics (Amer et al., 2021), show- ing advantages in improving predictive foresight, seman- tic understanding, and decision accuracy under complex on-site conditions. However, when these models are not deeply integrated with engineering constraints, such as safety boundaries, resource coupling, and spatial prece - dence, their recognition or prediction results often fail to translate into executable on-site actions. This gap repre- sents a key bottleneck in current intelligent construction practices: strong perception but weak decision closure and limited execution feedback. Building on the above challenges, prior research re- veals three gaps that remain insufficiently addressed. First, multimodal semantic alignment is still limited. Data streams from video, physical sensors, BIM, and text are often processed in isolation, and few frameworks achieve reliable synchronization across time, space, and semantics at the component or operation level. This lack of align- ment introduces cumulative errors and latency amplifi - cation throughout downstream analysis and scheduling. Second, the generation of constraint-aware and feasible strategies remains underdeveloped. Without explicitly en- coding safety envelopes, resource limits, and spatial pre - cedence into optimization or policy learning, systems tend to produce unconstrained and idealized recommendations that are impractical or costly to execute on site. Third, the execution and feedback loop is fragile. In most existing studies, decisions end at simulation or visualization; the absence of a measurable recognition–decision–execution– relearning cycle makes it difficult to verify benefits, quan - tify uncertainties, or define applicability boundaries. To address these gaps, this study selects the self-climb- ing platform (SCP) as a representative scenario of high concurrency and strong coupling and develops a DT× Transformer closed-loop system that has been validated on a live project. At the data and semantics layer, video, sensors data, 4D BIM, and task text are unified through a Component-ID plus timestamp alignment to ensure spa- tiotemporal coherence. At the cognitive layer, a multimod- al Transformer is trained for operation-state recognition and short-horizon risk forecasting, with explicit modeling of missing data, occlusion, and noise effects on accuracy and latency. At the decision layer, safety, resource, and spatial precedence constraints are embedded into poli- cy generation through optimization and reinforcement- learning techniques, yielding feasible and safety-compli- ant task sequences. At the execution and feedback layer, these strategies are delivered to crews via an augmented reality (AR) interface, allowing real-time confirmation and automatic synchronization of on-site actions back to the digital twin. This forms a closed information loop that con- tinuously refines perception and decision performance. Al- though the framework is tailored for SCP operations, its logic is conditionally transferable to other semi-enclosed, standardized construction settings, provided adequate BIM fidelity, sensor density and power supply, workforce training, and data governance are ensured. In this study, the focus extends beyond constructing a digital framework to validating its engineering effective- ness and practical feasibility under real construction condi- tions. To this end, the proposed DT × Transformer system is evaluated through a comprehensive field deployment that links perception, reasoning, and on-site execution into a verifiable feedback loop. The assessment emphasiz- es measurable improvement rather than theoretical nov- elty – examining how multi-source sensing, digital-twin in- tegration, and large-model decision support collectively enhance project performance. The evaluation strategy in- tegrates controlled comparisons and stepwise field imple - mentation to quantify gains in safety, schedule stability, communication efficiency, and traceability. Beyond perfor- mance metrics, attention is given to reproducibility and transparency: the study documents configuration details, data-alignment protocols, and validation criteria to enable subsequent replication and multi-project generalization in future applications of intelligent construction systems. 2. System verification To validate the feasibility and overall benefits of the pro - posed DT × Transformer intelligent construction system in a real construction environment, a comprehensive verifi - cation was conducted focusing on the system’s four func- tional modules and their collective enhancement of con- struction performance–covering safety management, op- erational efficiency, communication and execution, and in- formation transparency. The experiment adopted a staged implementation under consistent working conditions, en- abling controlled comparisons and cross-period interven - tions. The validation was carried out within the self-climbing platform (SCP) operation area of the high-rise core tube, where a closed-loop experimental environment featuring observability, interactivity, and verifiability was established. The selected test zone, located between the 18th and 20th floors, covered approximately 210 m2 and included the hydraulic synchronization system, formwork hoisting area, material staging zone, and operation corridor. A digital twin system integrating multi-source data interfaces and foundation-model reasoning modules was deployed, as il- lustrated in Figure 20. The deployment comprised both hardware and soft- ware components. On the hardware side, eight 1080p industrial cameras and twelve sets of acceleration, dis- placement, and load sensors were installed, operating re- spectively at 25 fps and 100 Hz sampling rates. On the software side, a 4D BIM model, task schedules, and log texts were integrated into the DT using a unified Com- ponent-ID + Timestamp indexing mechanism. All data streams were aggregated via an on-site edge gateway and processed on a local cloud platform for fusion and infer- ence. Bidirectional communication between the physical and virtual layers was achieved through MQTT and Web- Socket protocols. To evaluate system performance com- prehensively, four categories of key performance indi- cators (KPIs) were defined: (1) Safety leading indicators, including warning lead time (the time gap between sys- tem warning and event occurrence), violation frequency per thousand work-hours, and near-miss reporting rate; (2) Schedule and resource coordination indicators, such as average cycle time per floor, process waiting ratio, and variance of labor and equipment utilization; (3) Communi- cation and execution efficiency indicators, including end- to-end decision latency (from risk identification to confir - mation), instruction misunderstanding rate, and redundant command frequency; and (4) Transparency indicators, in- cluding traceability rate with complete evidence chains and task-level audit coverage. 4.3.1. Communication and execution efficiency Information delays and misinterpretations are com - mon in high-rise operations, often resulting in redun- dant or ineffective commands. To assess improvements in decision timeliness, end-to-end decision time – the duration from risk identification (system or manual) to on-site acknowledgment – was used as a key metric. The experiment was conducted across the 18th–20th floors in sequential phases. The baseline phase used tra - ditional voice calls and instant-messaging coordination; during the intervention phase, the system automatically generated task cards, verified by safety officers, and is - sued them through AR terminals. The system automati- cally logged timestamps for recognition, decision genera - tion, and acknowledgment, alongside voice/video archives for validation. Random stratified sampling (120 events per floor) was applied to minimize bias. Taking the 20th floor as an example, the median end-to-end time decreased from 11.6 s (baseline) to 4.8 s (intervention), as shown in Figure 23. Transparency and reproducibility To verify the system’s advantages in data governance and auditability, the platform automatically generated a digital evidence-chain manifest for each medium- and high-risk task.
