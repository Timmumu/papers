Abstract This study addresses the challenges of real-time response and closed-loop decision- making in smart construction. We propose a dual-loop collaborative framework integrating Building Information Modeling (BIM), the Internet of Things (IoT), and LSTM-Attention networks, validated via a 261-sensor deployment in the Taipei Bridge and Tunnel Joint Project. The architecture achieves sub-second synchronization and enables multi-objective optimization using NSGA-II. Experimental results demonstrate statistically significant improvements ( p < 0.001): a 46.2% reduction in system latency, a 41.4% decrease in safety incidents, an 18.5% reduction in direct costs, and an 11.1% decline in CO₂ emissions, while maintaining a 99.1% safety compliance rate. The framework successfully bridges the gap between digital models and physical realization, providing a scalable solution for adaptive and sustainable construction management. Keywords Smart construction, Digital twin, BIM-IoT, LSTM-attention, Multi-objective optimization 1 Introduction Intelligent construction is undergoing a key transformation from “endpoint perception” to “overall collaboration” [ 1]. This transformation is critically important for the con - struction industry, which not only contributes about 40% of the global carbon emissions [2], but also faces severe challenges such as high energy consumption, low efficiency and frequent safety accidents [ 3, 4]. Although the deep integration of building information modeling (BIM), Internet of Things and artificial intelligence is regarded as the core path to improve the transparency of construction and the agility of decision-making [ 5], and promotes the evolution from “one-way monitoring” to “digital twin collaboration” [ 1], practical problems such as sensor noise, model heterogeneity and decision-making delay remain prominent. Taking the Taipei Bridge and Tunnel Joint Project in 2024 as an example, although more than 260 sensors were deployed on site, the average delay of BIM updating and real-time decision-making exceeded 13 s [ 3]. Moreover, the manual recheck during the peak hours of crane operation will increase the delay by 5 to 7 s, which will seriously endanger the “golden window period” of safety intervention [6], highlighting the defects Page 2 of 19 Su Discover Applied Sciences (2026) 8:769 of one-way data flow in fast-paced construction [ 1, 7]. Existing research has several core limitations, including a lack of real-time capability, limited attention to end-to-end delay, packet loss compensation, and prediction algorithms [ 3, 8]. At the same time, there are issues such as insufficient closed-loop integrity, with control strategies mostly rely - ing on manual scheduling or static rules, and a lack of adaptive “prediction-execution- feedback” mechanisms [5, 9]. In addition, cost, schedule and safety objectives are often optimized in isolation, lacking unified indicators and dynamic trade-off analysis, which makes it difficult to achieve multi-objective coordination [ 10, 11]. The disconnection between the digital model and the physical realization seriously restricts the sub-second response ability required by the high-density construction environment. Based on the above findings, a new dual-loop collaborative framework is designed and verified in this study. The architecture realizes real-time synchronization through the data loop of sensor → gateway → MQTT → BIM, and realizes closed-loop control with the help of time series prediction [ 12]. This mechanism not only shortens the round- trip time of information between physical domain and digital domain, but also uses deep attention network to predict the construction progress, equipment health and environ - mental safety in real time, thus realizing multi-objective parallel optimization [ 13, 14]. It is worth noting that the dual-loop architecture we designed is scalable, allowing sensor nodes to be added and enabling additional business rules to be integrated into decision- making processes [15, 16]. This study aims to clarify the semantic interoperability mechanism of dual-loop archi - tecture, and define the field mapping and timestamp synchronization strategy between edge gateway, MQTT proxy and IFC entity. Secondly, the collaborative advantages of the framework in delay convergence, risk identification and economic benefits are veri - fied by experimental data. Finally, the quantitative relationship between interface delay, packet loss rate and security events can be summarized, which provides practical guid - ance for the implementation of large-scale intelligent construction sites [17, 18]. 2 Literature review 2.1 Technological foundations and persistent gaps in enabling components The intelligent transformation of the construction industry is driven by the integration of dual carbon targets and core digital technologies. The integration of BIM and Internet of Things (IoT) is the key to create digital twins, with research progressing from seman - tic mapping [ 19] to practical synchronization protocol [ 5] and spatial integration [ 3]. At the same time, deep learning models (such as LSTM network to enhance attention mechanism) have also made breakthroughs in equipment and process risk prediction [20, 21], and multi-objective optimization algorithms (such as NSGA-II and MOEA) have evolved to include sustainable development goals such as carbon emissions [ 22, 23]. However, there are still significant gaps in these components. BIM-IOT integration usually lacks real-time, and the reporting delay exceeds the critical threshold [ 3], pre- venting the full incorporation of environmental data. Predictive models usually analyze a single risk factor independently, ignoring the coupling between equipment operation and environmental conditions. Optimization algorithms mainly rely on static data, and rarely achieve integrated dynamic optimization of safety, cost, schedule and environ - mental impact within a unified framework. Page 3 of 19 Su Discover Applied Sciences (2026) 8:769 2.2 Systemic architectural challenges: from cloud-edge deployment to closed-loop integration In addition to individual technology, agile computing architecture is needed to realize real-time closed-loop system. Edge computing provides a low-latency basis for respon - sive decision-making [ 15, 16]. However, the current mainstream system architecture usually limits the function of edge devices to data preprocessing. This not only fails to fully tap the potential of localized intelligent processing but also fails to establish a robust and fault-tolerant cloud-edge collaborative mechanism to achieve uninterrupted control. This reveals a key systemic gap: although various component technologies (such as BIM, Internet of Things, and edge computing) are constantly improving, the overall architecture framework that can seamlessly integrate and operate together is still lack - ing. Specifically, the lack of real-time semantic synchronization, the lack of dynamic closed-loop control, and the limitations of extensible cloud-side cooperation mechanism all constitute the bottleneck of the core architecture layer. These shortcomings hinder the fundamental transformation of intelligent building systems from isolated techni - cal applications to collaborative network physical systems that can coherently complete “perception-analysis-decision-execution” . 2.3 Evolution of multi-objective optimization research: from economic scheduling to sustainability synergy Facing the challenge of multi-objective decision-making in construction management, multi-objective optimization algorithm provides an effective solution tool. Many stud - ies, such as Nusen et al. [ 17], combine genetic algorithm with BIM to schedule renova - tion projects, while Alothaimeen et al. use non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II) to optimize the cost and sustainability scores of green building projects [ 23]. Besides the classic NSGA-II, advanced optimization algorithms such as multi-objective ant colony optimizer (MOALO) and multi-objective equilibrium optimizer (MOEO) show excellent convergence and coverage in solving complex engineering problems. These provide additional algorithm options for dealing with nonlinear multi-modal opti- mization objectives in buildings [ 11, 24]. In addition, recent research attempts to com - bine more advanced algorithms such as NSGA-III with BIM to solve the optimization problem of sustainable design involving more objectives [ 25], showing the potential of algorithm evolution in solving complex collaborative challenges. Recently, the research scope has been expanded to include the environmental aspect. For example, Weigert et al. established a method to calculate carbon emissions during construction [ 2], while Zhao et al. simultaneously optimized the project duration, cost and carbon emissions in highway construction progress [22]. These studies have proved the potential of multi- objective optimization in promoting low-carbon construction and sustainable resource allocation [ 26– 28]. This change marks a change from pure economic efficiency to broader value pursuit including environmental responsibility. However, a common limi - tation still exists: most optimization models rely on static or historical data and cannot be deeply integrated with real-time BIM Internet of Things data stream to respond to dynamic changes. In addition, safety objectives are often dealt with separately or sim - ply combined, lacking systematic trade-offs within the same optimization framework as cost, schedule and carbon emissions. With regard to scheduling methods, besides genetic algorithm, new meta-heuristic algorithms, such as Sine-Cosine Algorithm Page 4 of 19 Su Discover Applied Sciences (2026) 8:769 (SCA), are explored, and the construction sequence is generated by BIM, which shows the potential to meet the space-time constraints [ 29]. This research directly solves this challenge. We built a five-dimensional evaluation system at the decision-making rules level, including direct cost, safety indicators, project duration, carbon emissions and return on investment (ROI). The system adopts NSGA-II algorithm for real-time multi- objective optimization, and automatically identifies the Pareto optimal balance among economic efficiency, safety and environmental performance in dynamic construction environment. This represents a leap from single-objective or double-objective optimiza - tion to multi-dimensional collaborative decision-making. 2.4 Synthesis and positioning of the proposed framework In short, the literature reveals an obvious disconnect: although enabling technologies have made progress, integrating them into a unified, adaptable, versatile, and real-time operating system remains an unsolved challenge. These limitations are not only technical but also architectural. Clearly, an overall system design that can seamlessly connect data, models, and decisions is needed. In this study, a dual-loop (data-control) collaborative architecture is proposed to solve this systematic gap identified above. The framework aims to fill the gaps in real-time synchronization, closed-loop optimization, semantic interoperability and elastic cloud-edge execution, and is positioned as a comprehensive solution for the next generation of intelligent construction sites. Accordingly, to further clarify the academic positioning of the proposed dual-loop collaborative framework, we systematically articulate its novelty by comparing it with existing BIM-IoT and digital twin architectures, with a particular focus on the fundamental innovations at the archi - tectural level that distinguish it from traditional designs. 2.5 Novelty of the proposed dual-loop architecture To clarify the framework’s novelty and state-of-the-art position, this subsection com - pares it with typical BIM-IoT/digital twin architectures and elaborates on core innova - tions (Table 1). Most existing architectures use single-loop synchronization or separate modules, leading to real-time/adaptability trade-offs. In contrast, the framework has three key innovations: data-control loop decoupling/dynamic coupling (real-time syn - chronization + intelligent decision-making), elastic cloud-edge collaboration with fault- tolerant rollback (distributed computing), and real-time ifcXML/SensorML semantic alignment (resolving inconsistency), distinguishing it from traditional architectures and offering unique academic and technical value. 3 Materials and methods 3.1 Dual-loop collaborative framework In the dynamic and high-noise environment of construction site, data undergoes multi- layer protocol conversion and semantic mapping in the round-trip process from sensors to decision nodes. Any delay may amplify the security risk. To overcome this bottleneck, we present the design of a parallel architecture that integrates a Data Loop and a Con - trol Loop. The design is driven by the principle of separation of concerns, where the Data Loop ensures reliable state synchronization and the Control Loop handles intel - ligent analysis. As shown in Fig. 1, Data_Loop establishes millisecond-level flow chan - nel through edge gateway and MQTT Broker. BIM server creates a one-to-one mapping Page 5 of 19 Su Discover Applied Sciences (2026) 8:769 between sensor tags and IfcGUID, forming a complete “perception model” closed loop. On the control end, the analysis engine uses LSTM attention network to process time characteristics. The decision rule triggers the actuator according to the prediction and feeds back the state to the engine for secondary calibration. The double loop is coupled through two channels: synchronization and update. The former ensures the seman - tic consistency between the model and the physical state, while the latter dynamically adjusts the weight of the algorithm in real time to realize the adaptive evolution ability. The analysis engine adopts a “double window” training mechanism: the short window (30 s) captures the impact load, while the long window (15 min) captures the environ - mental trend. When the root mean square error (RMSE) of three consecutive instances exceeds the dynamic threshold of 0.12, the system will automatically trigger incremental fine-tuning and solidify the weight during off-peak hours at night. This integrated architectural design not only realizes the real-time synchronization between physical construction sites and digital twins but also supports the closed-loop intelligent control of construction safety and multi-objective optimization, advancing the development of intelligent construction from fragmented monitoring to integrated management. The framework of Hong and Guo [ 5] realizes sub-second data synchronization, but ultimately depends on manual decision-making. Unlike this, this study adopts an algo - rithm model to automatically generate control commands, which significantly simplifies the decision-making chain. In addition, unlike previous studies on single-dimensional optimization (such as schedule, cost or indoor environment), this paper establishes the first unified quantitative framework of construction collaborative management. It dynamically balances economic, safety and environmental performance in the shared optimization space, and provides a new method for comprehensive efficiency evaluation of intelligent building sites. In fact, the research results show that when the decision- making process of the system is transparent and the feedback is timely, the acceptance and satisfaction of front-line personnel (such as foremen) to automatic scheduling instructions are significantly improved. This highlights an important insight: the success of intelligent construction site systems depends not only on the technical maturity, but also on their ability to build transparent and credible man-machine cooperation inter - faces, which enhance rather than weaken workers’ sense of control over the site. 5.3 Research limitations and reflections However, this study clearly recognizes its boundaries and limitations, and points out the direction for future exploration. First of all, the current design validation focuses on viaducts, mainly involving the pouring and hoisting process. The generalizability of the architectural model across different project types and universality of the frame requires further cross-project deployment and design adaptation. Moreover, the scalability of the proposed framework needs to be further explored; the current system is tailored to the specific regulatory context of the research area, and potential integration barriers may arise when applied to different international construction contexts with diverse regula - tory requirements, which has not been fully verified in this study. Second, the autono - mous evolution ability of the edge model is constrained. The current lightweight edge model relies on periodic knowledge extraction and weight update of the cloud-based main model. Under the extreme conditions of prolonged network isolation, this method has the risk of model performance degradation. Future research should explore more powerful marginal autonomous learning and federal renewal mechanisms. Third, data privacy governance has not been thoroughly addressed; although personal location data is anonymized during storage, the study lacks a systematic privacy governance framework to adapt to different regional data protection regulations (e.g., GDPR, local data security laws), which may limit the application of the system in cross-border con - struction projects. Finally, the dimension of multi-objective optimization model has Page 17 of 19 Su Discover Applied Sciences (2026) 8:769 expansion space. In addition to the direct cost and carbon emissions currently consid - ered, the increasingly important indirect cost factors in high-risk large-scale projects, such as dynamic insurance premium, social reputation gains/losses and the far-reaching social impact of security incidents, have not been included in the model. Solving these problems is very important for achieving wider “sustainable” cooperation. 5.4 Future research directions Looking forward to the future, the path pioneered by this study can be deepened in many directions. Technically, exploring the weight tracking mechanism of blockchain integration model can enhance the trust-based cooperation between distributed nodes. Developing a scene playback and simulation platform integrating high-fidelity digi - tal twinning and VR/AR technology will be helpful for solution pre-test and personnel training. In terms of optimization algorithm, future research can explore the integra - tion of recently improved multi-objective evolutionary strategies (for example, Zhang et al. and Liu et al.) [ 34, 35], in order to improve the convergence efficiency and solution set distribution when solving high-dimensional nonlinear structural coordination prob - lems. At the application level, it is urgent to carry out reproducible construction and comparative research across various typical engineering scenes (such as underground projects and coastal high humidity areas) to verify and improve the universality of the framework. Theoretically, the future multi-objective collaborative model should con - sider a wider range of socio-economic factors and establish a more inclusive “engineer - ing value” evaluation system. This will eventually promote the intelligent building site from a tool to improve the efficiency of a single project to an infrastructure to promote the overall green and intelligent transformation of the construction industry. 6 Conclusion This study proposes and empirically investigates a novel dual-loop (data-control) archi - tectural framework for smart construction sites. The framework is designed to bridge the systemic disconnects between data, models, and decisions by establishing a sub-sec - ond closed loop from real-time sensing to automated execution. The empirical results indicate a potential for co-optimization, with the framework associated with reductions in system delay (46.2%), safety incidents (41.4%), direct cost (18.5%), and carbon emis - sions (11.1%), while maintaining a high safety compliance rate (99.1%). These findings lend support to the possibility that safety, economic, and environmental objectives may be synergistically addressed through integrated system design. Beyond a specific project application, this work offers a potentially scalable architectural blueprint and a quantifi - able decision-making model. It thereby contributes to advancing the field of intelligent construction from fragmented monitoring toward more adaptive, collaborative, and sus- tainable project management practices. Introduction In large-scale outdoor working environments within urban con- texts, managing worker authorization presents significant challenges due to the vast and open nature of such sites. Ensuring that only authorized, properly trained personnel are present on-site is critical not only for operational efficiency but also for safety, especially in densely populated urban areas. Unauthorized workers typically lack the necessary safety training and may be unfamiliar with the site’s specific hazards, increasing the risk of accidents. This is of particular concern in urban environments like open-pit mines [1], wind farms [2], and large infrastructure projects such as highways [3] and railways [4], where real-time monitoring of workers is essential to prevent incidents and maintain safety across vast and complex city landscapes. While smaller, enclosed sites allow for straightforward worker identity verification through controlled access points or physical security measures [5]. However, the sheer scale and openness of urban-level work sites make traditional methods like manual patrolling or closed-circuit television (CCTV) surveillance impractical and costly [6]. For example, mines situated in city outskirts or construction zones with dynamic excavation areas pose continuous worker monitoring challenges [7]. Similarly, wind farms and large-scale burial sites require real-time monitoring of workers, but the vast geographical spread of these projects limits the effectiveness of fixed surveillance methods [8]. ∗ Corresponding authors. The application of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in large-scale urban site monitoring has proven to be a flexible and sustainable so- lution, effectively addressing the limitations of traditional surveillance methods such as fixed CCTV systems, which are often restricted by their limited coverage areas and lack of adaptability to dynamic site layouts [9,10]. UAVs offer unmatched versatility in covering extensive and constantly evolving urban construction environments, enabling real-time aerial monitoring of site progress, worker activities, and environmental conditions. Related works 2.1. UAV in construction monitoring UAVs have emerged as a transformative tool in construction mon- itoring due to their ability to provide real-time, comprehensive data collection, high flexibility, and extensive coverage across various con- struction environments [20]. UAVs offer significant advantages over traditional monitoring methods, such as manual inspections and fixed- camera surveillance systems, particularly in terms of efficiency, ac- cessibility, and safety [21]. By enabling aerial views and integrating with advanced imaging technologies like LiDAR and photogrammetry, UAVs can rapidly collect large amounts of data that can be processed to monitor site progress, worker activity, and material usage [22]. In urban and confined construction projects, UAVs have achieved considerable success [23,24]. They are widely used for progress track- ing [25], wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) prediction for climate adaptation [26], safety inspections [13], and structural assessments [27], where high-resolution imagery and precise mapping are cru- cial [28]. By reducing human involvement in dangerous environments, UAVs enhance safety while promoting resilient, low-impact construc- tion workflows. UAVs offer a safer and faster alternative to manual inspections, reducing the need for workers to access hazardous ar- eas [29], further supporting urban sustainability goals by enhancing resource allocation and reducing environmental footprints. As UAVs have proven effective in smaller, confined sites, their application has expanded into large-scale outdoor and open construc- tion environments, such as open-pit mines [30], wind farms, and highway projects [31,32]. In these vast and dynamic environments, UAVs offer unparalleled coverage and flexibility, providing real-time monitoring of extensive areas that are otherwise impractical for tra- ditional surveillance methods [33]. UAVs are now being used for topographical mapping, 3D modeling, environmental assessments, and real-time worker and equipment tracking [34], contributing to sus- tainable city management by integrating environmental monitoring with infrastructure development. The ability to monitor large distances, shifting terrain, and evolving site conditions makes UAVs essential for ensuring operational efficiency and maintaining safety standards across expansive construction sites, contributing to the resilience and sustainability of urban landscapes. The deployment of UAVs in large-scale, open construction environ- ments presents significant challenges. The vast size and complexity of such sites generate enormous volumes of data, demanding advanced processing capabilities and sophisticated algorithms for timely and accurate analysis. UAVs must operate in dynamic, uncontrolled en- vironments where factors such as complex terrain, variable weather conditions, and shifting lighting can hinder data capture and compro- mise system reliability. Their mobility further complicates real-time localization and data alignment due to constant adjustments in position Automation in Construction 174 (2025) 106108 2 M. Han et al. and orientation. Integrating UAV data into broader monitoring frame- works is particularly challenging, as construction sites require continu- ous adaptation and robust data synchronization to maintain accuracy. Object detection in UAV application Object detection methods in UAV applications can be broadly cate- gorized into single-stage and two-stage approaches. Single-stage meth- ods, such as YOLOv10 and SSD, are known for their real-time per- formance and low computational requirements, making them highly suitable for applications that demand fast inference [35,36]. In con- trast, two-stage methods, such as Faster R-CNN, improve detection accuracy by using a region proposal network (RPN), although this comes at the cost of slower inference speeds [37,38]. Transformer- based models like DETR aim to enhance real-time performance by replacing the RPN with an end-to-end detection approach, offering high accuracy but requiring more computational resources [39,40]. While these algorithms perform well on standard benchmark datasets like Pascal VOC 2007 and COCO, they often face limitations when applied to UAV-based detection. UAV images tend to have large background areas with complex features, while the target objects (e.g., workers or equipment) occupy only a small portion of the image, making detection more challenging [41,42]. Furthermore, UAVs are constrained by limited endurance and computational capacity, which require detection models that are both computationally efficient and capable of fast inference [43,44]. The critical challenge in UAV-based detection tasks is finding the balance between detection precision and real-time performance. As such, models like YOLO, with their minimal computational overhead and fast inference speeds, are often preferred for UAV inspection tasks. However, even YOLO models, typically pre-trained on conventional datasets, may not align well with the unique operational conditions of UAV-based inspections. UAVs capture images from an overhead perspective, which results in distinct object features for the same categories and challenges like object occlusion [41,44]. This leads to the need for retraining these models on specialized datasets tailored to the specific context of UAV inspections. Currently, datasets specif- ically designed for UAV perspectives or site inspection environments — such as UAVDT [45], VisDrone [46], and UAV123 [47] — remain limited, often lacking generalizability across diverse operational envi- ronments [48,49]. While these datasets provide important benchmarks for certain UAV tasks, their applicability in broader, more complex real- world scenarios remains constrained. As a result, there is a growing need to develop task-specific datasets that are customized to meet the unique challenges of UAV-based inspections. In terms of applications, many studies have explored UAV-based object detection, though most have focused on specific tasks like crack detection. For instance, He et al. Verification in physical space Building upon the calculations presented in the previous section, we are able to estimate the latitude and longitude coordinates of all workers within the drone video stream. Each individual worker is tracked, and their geographic coordinates are estimated in real time based on the visual data captured by the drone. As the drone completes its flight and exits the worksite, this information is uploaded and subsequently converted into GeoJSON format, enabling its visualization on a 3D model of the entire construction site. However, relying solely on video streams is insufficient to ascertain whether workers possess valid work authorization. To address this limitation, each worker is equipped with a work permit, in the form of a GPS locator, which provides real-time positioning data with centimeter-level accuracy. By cross-referencing the GPS location with the estimated position derived from the video stream, it becomes possible to determine whether workers are engaging in unauthorized activities. If the location data from the video stream aligns closely with the GPS coordinates, it suggests that the worker is operating within the designated area, and their activities are authorized. Conversely, significant discrepancies between the video-based location estimate and the GPS data may indicate potential unauthorized actions. This verification process is then uploaded to the site management platform for further analysis and reporting. This integrated system not only enhances the security of the construction site but also provides project managers with a robust tool for efficient monitoring and inspection. By combining drone-based video tracking with precise GPS technology, this method offers an automated and highly accurate solution for worker identity verification and work authorization management. The approach minimizes human error, increases the precision of monitoring, and enables rapid detection of potential risks, thereby ensuring both safety and compliance on the construction site. 4. Related Work Tower Crane –Related Safety Management Tower cranes play a crucial role in construction projects, serving as indispensable tools for lifting building materials. Research on tower crane safety has spanned decades, primarily focusing on two key areas: the identification and assessment of safety risks and the im- plementation of monitoring measures to ensure safety. Two primary methods are commonly used to identify and assess tower crane risks: statistical analysis and interview studies. Through analyzing large volumes of accident data, researchers have found that complex procedures and poor worker performance are the main contributors to high risks, particularly during the installation phase (Aneziris et al. 2008 ; Beavers et al. 2006 ; Swuste 2013). This con- clusion is supported by several studies on tower crane collapses, where worker errors in understanding crane status and executing operations were frequent causes of failure ( Frendo 2013; Marquez et al. 2014 ; McDonald et al. 2011 ; Zrnic et al. 2011 ). Additionally, a quantitative analysis of 212 incidents confirmed that the most haz- ardous factors are technical and human errors (Raviv et al. 2017a, b). In fact, 148 operator errors were identified, with risks quantified accordingly (Borgheipour et al. 2020). Interviews and surveys have further highlighted and ranked the factors influencing tower crane safety. These include inadequate worker training, complex proce- dures, deteriorating equipment, insufficient supervision, and poor working conditions ( Kim and Singhose 2010 ; Sertyesilisik et al. 2010; Sharma et al. 2019 ). It is widely acknowledged that these is- sues play a major role in incidents occurring during installation and dismantling ( Shin 2015 ; Tam and Fung 2011 ). Moreover, several studies have explored broader factors contributing to tower crane accidents, with findings largely consistent with earlier research (Zhang et al. 2020 , 2021; Zhou et al. 2018 ). In terms of monitoring measures, passive monitoring is widely used to gather information on the status of hooks, lifting capacity, and limiting devices, with extensions to static monitoring of struc- tural safety indicators such as stress, displacement, and wind speed. Condition monitoring systems provide detailed real-time data on factors like lifting moment, swing amplitude, and lifting weight, using sensing, signal processing, and wireless technologies (Zhang et al. 2013 ). Sensor data, including lifting weight, boom inclination, rotation angle, and wind speed, are captured and used in real-time FEM analysis to assess the structural safety of the tower crane ( Guanghui et al. 2020 ). Key parameters monitored for structural safety include stress, deformation, temperature, wind load, and acceleration (Chong et al. 2021 ; Zhang et al. 2021 ). Structural stress, verticality, and wind speed are compared against predefined thresholds to determine warning levels ( W e ie ta l .2 0 2 1). By monitoring hook height, trolley position, and jib angle, visual monitoring systems can analyze driver behavior through a scoring mechanism to improve operational safety ( Yannian et al. 2022 ). Additionally, this monitoring data can be utilized for model analysis and simulation of crane operations and accidents. The safety level of tower cranes at individual sites can be predicted using an integrative model that analyzes the impact of various overall safety indices (Shapira et al. 2012). Additionally, a multiuser virtual safety training system has been developed to simulate real-world conditions for workers involved in tower crane dismantling, offering a practical and immersive learning environment ( Li et al. 2012 ). Machine learning (ML) has been applied to safety hazard iden- tification, error detection, and the recognition of unsafe behaviors. A smart packaging method was proposed to enable adaptive decision-making and path replanning in dynamic crane operation environments ( Li et al. 2020 ). An audio-based safety recognition system was also developed to continuously monitor and manage safety risks by detecting abnormal events ( Lee et al. 2020 ). Addi- tionally, a deep learning algorithm has been used to predict oper- ator fatigue during crane operations ( Liu et al. 2021 ). Further studies have expanded these efforts ( Jiang and Ding 2024 ; Wu et al. 2022 ). With advancements in computing and storage capa- bilities, there has been increasing integration of traditional meth- ods with emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), blockchain, and digital twin (DT). A blockchain-based framework was developed to enhance the effectiveness of on-site inspections of tower cranes ( Wu et al. 2022 ). Using DT technology, a general framework was proposed to analyze unsafe behaviors and improve human-machine interactions in construction activities ( Jiang et al. 2022). Moreover, a DT-driven model was created to predict the reduced lifting capacity of aging tower cranes using real-time data, thereby enhancing safety ( Hussain et al. 2024 ). In summary, extensive research has been conducted on the iden- tification and assessment of safety risks in tower crane operations. It is widely acknowledged that complex procedures and inadequate execution are the two most significant factors contributing to tower crane incidents, particularly during installation and dismantling. Most existing safety management approaches rely on passive mon- itoring and issuing warnings, which often prove ineffective in emer- gency situations. Introduction Periodic structural surfaces inspection is crucial for ensuring the structural safety of large-scale infrastructures such as buildings and bridges. Traditionally, such inspection has been largely conducted manually, which is time consuming and prone to errors. More recently, due to the agility and efficiency of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in capturing surface images, some studies have begun to apply UAV tech- nology for structural surface inspection and shown promising results [ 1 – 3 ]. This process typically involves orienting the UAV camera towards the target structure where it captures inspection images of the visible coverage area on the structural surface, which is defined as the Field of View (FOV). During the process of using UAVs to fly around structures and acquire inspection image data, three main control indicators are emphasized [ 4 – 6 ]: (1) safety, to ensure that UAVs do not collide with obstacles; (2) comprehensiveness, to acquire complete images of the whole surface to avoid missing inspection; (3) efficiency, to avoid un- necessary flight burdens. When using traditional manual UAV flight, such multiple and possibly conflicting goals can be challenging to be achieved simultaneously, especially for large-scale and complex struc- tures [ 7 – 9 ]. To tackle this issue, some researchers have comprehensively reviewed and studied the pre-planning of rational UAV flight missions to enable automatic execution [ 10 – 12 ]. Compared to the manual control method, it has been shown that pre-planning can significantly enhance the safety and efficiency of the inspection [ 6 ]. For an effective pre- planning of flight mission, the first step is to generate suitable struc- tural inspection waypoints, which are the specified location coordinates where a UAV hovers and acquires images. Properly choosing of waypoints is crucial for the complete while economical covering of the structural surface. Naturally, a reference model to the real structure is necessary for determining the distribution of the waypoints. Such a reference model can be classified into five levels of detail (LoD) according to the Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC) standard for City Geography Markup Language (GML) 2.0 [ 13 ]. A building example is used to illustrate the different levels from LoD0 to LoD4, as shown in Fig. 1 . The LoD0 model [ 14 – 16 ] represents the structural projection on a 2D plane, which is typically acquired using footprint extraction methods [ 17 , 18 ]. Such a simple model can be used for the generation of waypoints that focus on the acquisition of rough top view images. To generate waypoints that focus on the acquisition of both rough facade and top view images, the LoD1 model [ 19 ], which is a block model derived by extending the LoD0 model vertically, can be used. Although in 3D, the LoD1 model lacks differentiated facades and the roof structures [ 20 ], which can create difficulties for structures with * Corresponding author. To deal with this, the LoD2 model, in which the ac- curate geometric shape of the structure is represented, can be used. With openings in the structure, such as doors and windows, the LoD2 model can be further upgraded to LoD3 [ 21 ]. By further including details of the structural interior, the LoD4 model can be created [ 6 ]. From their characteristics it can be seen that it is challenging to use LoD0 and LoD1 models for effective structural surface inspection, while LoD2, LoD3 and LoD4 can serve as better reference models. Previously, some studies have generated waypoints based on models constructed by design drawings defined under LoD3, such as manually constructed Building Information Modeling (BIM) [ 22 – 25 ] and 3D geometric model [ 26 , 27 ]. While these models can serve as effective reference models can be automatically generated through methods such as deep learning [ 28 ] and progressive model-driven approach [ 29 ], their openings and inter- nal details are not essential information for external surface inspection waypoint generation. Moreover, the large amount of training data and modeling complexity required do hinder the automatic construction of the efficient reference models. Thus, it can be concluded that the LoD2 model is the most suitable choice for the task of external surface inspection. Some studies have utilized 3D point clouds to extract the geometric shape of the structure [ 4 , 30 – 32 ], voxelized it or adapt observation planes to the geometry of the scene for waypoints generation and iter- atively optimizing the number and positions of waypoints based on data quality [ 33 ]. The 3D point clouds can be produced by depth devices [ 34 , 35 ] and multi-view reconstruction [ 36 ], and extensive research has been conducted on the methods of point clouds generation [ 37 – 39 ] and factors affecting the quality of point clouds [ 11 , 12 , 40 ]. Processing 3D point clouds and voxelized models can effectively generate inspection waypoints. However, due to the inclusion of significant non-structural components and surrounding environments, it becomes challenging to focus on the structural surface. Additionally, since these methods involve a large amount of discrete data, they tend to be less accurate and efficient compared to waypoints generation using LoD2 models. Several studies have addressed waypoints generation based on reference models. Tan et al. [ 22 ] proposed a “ three-steps ” region divi- sion method. First, the polygonal surface of the building is divided into several rectangular regions, with the corner point of each rectangular region serving as initial point to create the first rectangle primitive. These primitives are then translated until them cover all rectangular regions on the surface, and the offset of each primitive ’ s center point is extracted to generate inspection waypoints for the surface. Additionally, by pre-dividing the plane into grids, a polygonal surface can be directly divided in a “ two-steps ” process. Yang et al. [ 41 ] used UAVs to collect overhead inspection images of debris fans, manually inputting the boundary information of the debris fan into the pre-divided grid and generating waypoints based on the grid ’ s center points, effectively covering the irregularly shaped debris fan. Yu et al. [ 15 ] also conducted research on the generation of overhead waypoints, using a four- neighborhood growing method to determine the areas to be covered and dividing the grid to generate waypoints. However, further research is needed on these methods to adapt them to the “ two-steps ” division of 3D polygonal surfaces of GDT model, thereby improving the efficiency of waypoints generation. In these methods, feature points are first extracted from 3D point clouds ’ plane and boundary [ 4 , 31 , 32 ], the center points of triangle mesh [ 30 ], or the sub region centroids of BIM surface [ 22 , 23 , 25 ], which are then shifted outward in parallel to determine the waypoints. As illustrated in Fig. 2 (a), waypoints generated with such strategies may be within obstacles, such as trees, power lines sur- rounding the building, and non-structural components installed on the building, or too close to them. Furthermore, the flight paths planned in these manners reduce flexibility and are less than optimal [ 42 ]. There- fore, it is necessary to adjust the positions of the waypoints according to the obstacle information around the structure to prevent unsafe or inaccessible waypoints. Some studies have considered the environ- mental obstacles in the process of flight path planning. For instance, Sun et al. [ 43 ] used a semantic segmentation method to divide obstacle areas in orthophotos, providing references for UAV flight path planning at the 2D level. Some other studies have considered manually constructed obstacle models at the 3D level for path planning [ 44 , 45 ]. Tan et al. [ 46 ] researched multi-UAV path planning issues using virtual obstacles that simulate the environment. Obstacle information can also be expressed through automatic methods. Phung et al. [ 4 ] expressed the obstacle information using the maximum bounding boxes of 3D point clouds, ensuring that waypoints and flight paths avoid obstacles. While these methods can lower the flight risks to a certain degree by considering the environment obstacles, manually constructed virtual obstacle models may differ from actual conditions and be inefficient. Additionally, using rough models such as bounding boxes to represent environmental ob- stacles may result in the loss of valuable accessible areas, especially for inspecting complex structures. Therefore, it is vital to further consider more accurate obstacle information of the real-life environment when using the parallel shift strategy. Once the ultimate waypoints are determined, a flight path optimi- zation shall be conducted to find the optimal route that sequentially connects all generated unordered inspection waypoints. A typical unsafe or inaccessible flight path is shown in Fig. 2 (b), in which parts of it pass through obstacles or being too close to obstacles. It is necessary to optimize the flight path to ensure UAV flight safety while also mini- malize flight burden. These optimization challenges bear resemblance to the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and are commonly addressed using heuristic algorithms, such as Genetic Algorithm (GA) [ 47 ], self- adaptive genetic simulated annealing algorithm (AGASA) [ 2 ], and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) [ 15 ]. Research on these algorithms focuses on improving their execution speed, convergence, and avoiding local optima. These methods can enhance the accuracy and efficiency of path optimization. However, the issues of obstacle avoidance and energy consumption in UAV during practical engineering applications also warrant further attention. Optimization of flight path that also tackles safety issues is a constrained optimization problem, in which the con- siderations of obstacles as constraints in heuristic algorithms are incorporated. Koch et al. [ 48 ] used semantic maps overlaid on 3D model to determine if there are restrictions on UAV flight along these paths, and provided the results to heuristic algorithms to optimize the flight path. In environments where obstacles cannot be completely avoided using heuristic algorithms alone. To address this issue, some studies use a combination of automatic path finding algorithms and heuristic al- gorithms to ensure that the optimized flight paths avoid obstacles [ 4 , 5 , 23 ]. Among these, Phung et al. [ 4 ] considered UAV energy con- sumption related to flight direction when planning paths using such methods. Furthermore, Tan et al. [ 23 ] not only planned UAV flight paths but also devised routes for the UAV to reach designated locations for battery replacement. However, it is vital to ensure that flight paths maintain a certain safe distance from real-world environmental obsta- cles and reduce costs associated with UAV turns due to obstacle avoid- ance, which requires further research. In other words, it is crucial to consider more real and accurate obstacle information and minimize transitional waypoints for turns as much as possible to optimize the flight path, which has not been sufficiently considered in the past studies. To tackle these issues, this paper proposes a novel method for automatically planning UAV inspection flight missions for capturing surface images based on Geometric Digital Twin (GDT) model and Voxelized Obstacle Information (VOI). First, a LoD2-class GDT model, which can be automatically and rapidly constructed [ 49 – 51 ], is inno- vatively applied to the inspection waypoints generation. Based on the accurate geometric shape of the GDT model, a preliminary waypoints generation method that involves shifting FOV centroids in parallel is proposed, enhancing the automation and efficiency of the waypoint generation process. Second, a method utilizing more precise VOI for inspection waypoints adjustment is proposed. This method employs voxelization and regional division of the space containing the 3D point clouds and GDT model to relocate unsafe or inaccessible waypoints to safer areas. Finally, a heuristic path optimization method combining GA with A* based on VOI is proposed to define the safe flight path, incor- porating consideration of real-world environmental obstacles and reducing the additional energy and time costs associated with automatic routing through turn penalties. The method proposed in this paper efficiently and effectively generates waypoints based on the GDT model and ensures the safety of both the waypoints and the flight path con- necting them through VOI. INTRODUCTION The Architecture Engineering and Construction (AEC) sector is evolving towards cloud -based, scalable, and ubiquitous architecture to facilitate model generation, data exchange, and information intake (V enugopal et al., 2012). A typical construction project comprises multifaceted inputs from various streams such as materials, information, equipment, availability of space, labour, etc. (Schlenger et al., 2022). Different domains in the AEC industry store data in heterogeneous data formats, for example, BIM (X ML or relational databases), cost estimations and project schedules in relational databases, material suppliers’ product data in text, HTML or XML or relational formats (Niknam and Karshenas, 2017). In multi -data environments like construction projects, the data integration process and delivering a meaningful output from these data can be challenging (Schlenger et a l., 2022). This is often referred to as the issue with interoperability within the construction industry (Niknam and Karshenas, 2017). The concept of the semantic web allows various domains engaged in AEC projects to semantically represent building information on a specific entity in a manner that could be integrated with data from other domains (Niknam and Karshenas, 2017). The need to overcome interoperability issues, the desire for linking data across domains, and logical inference and proofs have fuelled the interest of AEC industry researchers to explore the application of semantic web technology for the AEC domain (Pauwels e t al., 2017). Along with semantic web technologies, linked data technologies have the potential to create an open and collaborative environment for sharing, integrating, and linking data from many domains and data sources (Zhang and Beetz, 2016). The concept of the semantic web lies behind the linked data concept, which is the creation of a web of data with the help of data schemas , termed ontologies , that represent formal knowledge, concepts and their relationships within a domain (Schlachter, Rasmussen and Karlshøj, 2022). Furthermore, the application of ontologies and the semantic web within the AEC sector has progressively increased (Abanda et al., 2013; Pauwels et al., 2017; Farghaly et al., 2023). Over the last decades, digital project models and model interchange formats have been the subject of study and standardisation, with the Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) schema at the heart of interoperability and supporting project stakeholder engagement (Laakso and Kiviniemi, 2012; Boje et al., 2020). The importance of advancing information management through semantic web technologies aligns with the maturity stages defined in the ISO 19650-1 document. As outlined in the standard, the progression from Stage 1 to Stage 3 reflects increasing integration, automation and collaboration. Stage 2 marks the transition to federated info rmation models, forming the groundworks for more advanced digitisation such as web -scale, semantically enriched data exchange , anticipated within Stage 3. Currently, the concept of digital twins is also gaining more traction in the construction industry (Lu et al., 2019). The digital twin concept goes beyond the static modelling capabilities of BIM , which comprises BIM models, schedules, contracts, construction documents (submittals, change orders, RFIs, etc.), operational data collected by the embedded sensors, and data from AI and machine learning technology, etc. (Keen, 2021). Therefore, the availability of a well -defined data schema or structure is essential for the representation of complex information in a much more trustworthy and concise manner to apply the concept of Level 3 BIM and digital twins in the construction industry (Schlenger et al., 2022). In the execution phase of a construction project, various stakeholders generate a large volume of data in diverse formats across multiple systems. This diversity presents significant challenges for data integration and makes it difficult to produce meaningful insights for decision-making. Thus, this paper focuses on exploring how semantic web technologies, especially ontologies, have been utilised for construction management use cases during the construction execution phase through a systematic literature r eview, specifically to support BIM/Digital Twin implementation. This paper primarily focuses on three objectives: 1. Review the extent to which semantic web technologies, linked data and ontologies have been applied in the BIM -based and Digital Twin -based construction management context and their related use cases during the construction execution phase. 2. Analysis of how semantic web tools have been utilised to cater the purposes of different use cases. 3. Identify current gaps and directions for future research. Following the introduction, a brief literature review is presented to review the concepts of BIM, digital twin, semantic web, li nked data, and ontologies, followed by a methodology chapter demonstrating the literature ITcon Vol. 30 (2025), Palihakkara et al., pg. 1210 selection and analysis. The literature findings have been discussed under the identified use cases, accompanied by a brief trend analysis. Finally, an overview of the existing barriers and future research avenues relating to BIM and digital twin-based semantic web research is explored. 2. RESEARCH BACKGROUND 2.1 Building Information Modelling (BIM) A sophisticated digital depiction of a built facility with significant information depth is called a building information model (Borrmann et al., 2018). However, a BIM model is not just the 3D representation of an asset; it is capable of expressing the sem antics of a building model. One of the major goals of the concept of BIM is to achieve an open BIM, where vendor-neutral data formats are employed for seamless integration between different stakeholders representing a variety of disciplines. To address this issue, buildingSMART has developed an object- oriented data format termed Industry Foundation Classes (IFC), which offers prolific data structures that span practically all facets of constructed facilities (Pauwels et al., 2017). The ISO 19650 series conceptualises information management maturity through three progressive stages. Stage 1 represents the conventional information management practice, which involves unstructured or structured data exchange through paper-based or digital means. Stage 2 (BIM according to ISO 19650 series) proposes the use of federated information models supported by a combination of manual and automated processes within a common data environment. Currently, Stage 2 BIM is being targeted to be applied with in the construction industry, which involves local software platforms, file -based information interchange, and coordination settings (Pauwels and Petrova, 2020). The goal of Stage 3 maturity is to support more integrated and intelligent data environments through advanced information management technologies, moving beyond file -based approaches towards web - scale, object-based and data-driven platforms. However, a fileless and web-based building information platform is currently not completely supported by any technology (Pauwels and Petrova, 2020). This very idea of a web of Stage 3 maturity aligns closely with the principles of the Semantic Web, which advocates for machine -readable, interconnected data to enhance interoperability and cross-domain integration (Rasmussen et al., 2020). 2.2 Digital Twin A digital twin is a digital portrayal of a physical asset that acts as a ‘living’ digital simulation model and is made possible by a large amount of data (e.g., operational data from bridges) and powerful data processing and interpretation procedures (Ye e t al., 2019). The origin of the concept of digital twin traces back to Dr Michael Grieves’s presentation in 2002. Despite the lack of a widely accepted or agreed-upon definition for the term digital twin (Gerber et al., 2019), as per Tao et al. (2019), a d igital twin comprises three major components: a physical artefact, a digital counterpart, and the connection which ties these two. This connection is referred to as the exchange of data, information, and knowledge between the physical and digital twin, whi ch is powered by the advancements of sensing technologies such as computer vision, IoT devices, advanced analytics such as machine learning and high-speed networking (Rosen et al., 2015). Figure 1 illustrates the concept and artefacts of a digital twin. Digital twins will enable the capturing of real-time data and communication with the stakeholders to optimise the decision-making process (Shirowzhan et al., 2020). Digital twins can be applied for different use cases such as facilities management and oper ation, asset condition monitoring, decision -making, simulations in design, and sustainable development (Brilakis et al., 2020). In addition, digital twins can be used to test what-if scenarios and novel techniques to simulate and make predictions regarding the asset (Taylor et al., 2019). Currently, the concept of digital twin in the construction industry is mostly applied during the operation phase of a building ( Khajavi et al., 2019). To address this limitation, Sacks et al. (2020) have introduced the core concepts and workflow in implementing digital twins for the design and construction phases of buildings and infrastructure, coining it as Digital Twin Construction (DTC). As per Sacks et al. (2020), DTC is a data -driven construction management approach that uses continuous monitoring, data analytics, and proactive control systems to enhance situational awareness and decision -making throughout the design and construction phases. Despite the appealing nature of digital twins, there are numerous barriers to implementing digital twins in the construction industry. A construction project involves a strong collaboration between various stakeholders such as designers, consultants, contractors, suppliers, etc. (Ballard and Howell, 2003). These stakeholders produce information regarding the construction ITcon Vol. 30 (2025), Palihakkara et al., pg. 1211 product and the process (Sacks et al., 2020). However, the information is produced using a wide array of digital tools with various data formats that are not interoperable with one another (Sacks et al., 2018). This issue can be referred to as the interoperability issue with the data (Schlenger et al., 2022). Semantic web technology and linked data are active research areas in the construction industry, offering tools that facilitate improved data integration, exchange and interoperability (Pauwels et al., 2017). While these technologies do not inherently solve the interoperability challenges, they provide a framework that can support more seamless data sharing when accompanied by standardised practice. Figure 1: Concept of digital twins. 2.3 Semantic Web In the early 2000s, Tim Berners -Lee (2009) presented the concept of the semantic web, with Linked Data as a subset. The semantic web is often termed as the ‘Web 3.0’, which is the Web of connected or linked data; whereas Web 1.0 is the Web of documents and Web 2.0 is the Web of application silos (Nath et al., 2014). The concept of the semantic web can be considered an extension of the concept of the World Wide Web, which is the Web of documents, to the Web of data (Niknam and Karshenas, 2017). It expands th e network of hyperlinked human - readable web pages by introducing machine -readable metadata regarding pages and the way they are related to one another, allowing automated agents to browse the Web with greater intelligence and execute activities on behalf of users (Berners -Lee, 2009). Semantic web technologies allow individuals to establish Web -based data repositories, develop vocabularies, and design data-handling rules. There is a well-established set of standards and functionalities, such as Web Ontology Language (OWL), Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL), SPARQL, etc., which accommodate knowledge inference, reasoning, rule checking, and data querying (Schlenger et al., 2022). Semantic web stack is deployed to illustrate the technical standards that enable Linked Data and the Semantic Web, which can be found in Borrmann et al. (2018). The initial framework of the digital twin was identi- ﬁed as comprising physical products, virtual products, and their interconnections [ 14]. Gabor et al. [ 15] described the digital twin as an advanced simulation created using domain expertise and real-world data, capable of achieving accu- rate simulations across varying temporal and spatial scales. Tao et al. [16] emphasized that digital twin modeling serves as the core of representing physical entities and, driven by data and models, enables monitoring, simulation, and pre- diction. Subsequently, Tao and Zhang [ 17] proposed that a comprehensive digital twin comprises ﬁve key compo- nents: physical entities, virtual entities, interconnections, data, and services, with data being fundamental for gener- ating new knowledge. These elements collectively form the core structure of the digital twin [ 18]. A digital twin is not merely a digital representation of a physical entity; it also facilitates real-time acquisition and analysis of operational, surrounding, and status-related information, thereby support- ing decision-making processes. With advancements in the Internet of Things (IoT), big data, artiﬁcial intelligence, and high-performance computing, the concept of the digital twin has evolved and found applications beyond manufacturing. Its adoption has expanded into diverse ﬁelds, including smart cities, healthcare, aerospace, and transportation. Digital twin technology represents a relatively novel approach in the domain of building ﬁre safety. To address the increasingly stringent requirements for ﬁre safety in complex and dynamic building environments, a digital twin framework integrating IoT, building information modeling (BIM), and advanced machine learning techniques has been developed. This framework enables real-time monitoring and dynamic simulation of critical elements, including the internal environment, ﬁre protection systems, and personnel distribution within a building, by constructing a digital repre- sentation of the physical structure. These ﬁndings underscore the framework’s potential to provide robust technical support and a reliable decision-making foundation for intelligent ﬁre- ﬁghting applications. 2.2 Intelligent fire fighting In recent years, research on the application of artiﬁcial intelli- gence (AI), virtual reality (VR), IoT, and BIM in conjunction 123 Digital twin-enabled deep learning for real-time ﬁre situation … 11859 with digital twins (DT) for building ﬁre protection has gained signiﬁcant attention. The framework, which integrates the Internet of Things, is demonstrated with 123 Digital twin-enabled deep learning for real-time ﬁre situation … 11867 a real-time ﬁre recognition model that utilizes deep learning algorithms to enable ﬁre detection and monitoring. A ﬁre- monitoring digital twin platform was developed within the Unity 3D environment, which facilitates human–computer interaction. The proposed digital twin-based ﬁre protection system breaks through the limitation that the traditional system is triggered only when the ﬁre characteristics are obvious; integrates multi-source sensor data to build a real-time 3D ﬁre model; visualizes the development of the ﬁre dynam- ics (including smoke diffusion, temperature distribution, etc.); solves the defects of the traditional system of a single alarm and the lack of spatial information; and integrates the Internet of Things (IoT), artiﬁcial intelligence and 3D visu- alization technologies; and builds a closed-loop “sensing- analysis-warning-disposal” system.
