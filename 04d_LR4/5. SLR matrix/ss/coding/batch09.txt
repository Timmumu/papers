Whilst digital twin (DT) technology is increasingly adopted across the built environment, most existing research and practice focus on its application during the operational phase of built assets such as energy monitor- ing, space and occupancy management, and predictive maintenance. The RDT integrates real-time data and dashboards to monitor 13 Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) across ﬁve domains: cost, schedule, quality, safety, and environment. Designed with GDPR compliance and usability in mind, the platform aims to support Architecture, Engineering, Construc- tion, and Operations (AECO) stakeholders by improving transparency, early issue detection, and decision-making throughout project delivery. The RDT was evaluated using a quantitative questionnaire based on the Technol- ogy Acceptance Model (TAM) involving 102 industry professionals to assess perceived usefulness, ease of use, and overall user acceptance. Results demonstrated high user conﬁdence, with Attitude Toward Use (RII = 0.894) ranking highest amongst TAM constructs. Deployment on the French RINNO demonstration site, achieving 73% emissions reduction but facing economic viability challenges, demonstrates the platform’s capacity to surface complex multi-domain trade-oﬀs. Whilst minor usability improvements were identiﬁed, initial ﬁndings provide evidence of the platform’s viability and user acceptance, though broader validation across diverse renovation contexts is required. This research contributes an initial, stakeholder-informed DT framework for renovation, advancing the role of digital technologies in improving coordination, monitoring, and performance in occupied retroﬁt environments. 1. Introduction The construction industry plays an essential role in global economic growth and the development of urban environments. However, the in- dustry is considered the world’s largest consumer of natural resources and a major contributor to environmental challenges such as resource depletion, carbon emissions, and energy use [1]. According to the 2019 Global Status Report for Buildings and Construction, the sector is respon- sible for 36% of ﬁnal energy use and approximately 40% of energy- and process-related CO2 emissions [2]. By 2045, the global urban popula- tion is projected to exceed six billion, potentially resulting in increased construction activities to meet housing and infrastructure demands [3]. In response to these environmental pressures, governments worldwide have implemented ambitious decarbonisation policies. The EU has es- tablished the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD), set- ting energy savings targets for 2030 and 2033 [4], whilst the UK has ∗ Corresponding author. Meeting these targets necessitates extensive building renovation: the UK alone requires retroﬁtting almost 29 million homes [6], exemplifying the scale of renovation needed glob- ally to achieve climate goals [7]. However, achieving these ambitious renovation targets faces com- plex, multi-dimensional barriers. Understanding these implementation challenges requires recognising four critical barrier categories: human factors (social norms, awareness deﬁcits, technology adoption resis- tance), technological factors (system integration challenges, interop- erability issues), organisational factors (resource allocation, manage- ment support, professional training deﬁciencies), and external environ- ment factors (regulatory complexity, policy inconsistencies, market con- straints) [8]. This barrier framework demonstrates that technological solutions alone cannot address the low renovation rates constraining EU-wide climate targets. Digital twin (DT) technology oﬀers a promising approach to over- coming these multi-dimensional barriers, provided it can eﬀectively bridge the gap between technological capability and practical adoption. A DT represents an up-to-date virtual representation of a physical en- tity and its functional properties, leveraging dynamic data streaming and analytics capabilities to support informed decision-making [9,10]. DT applications in construction include optimised resource manage- ment through real-time monitoring [11]; enhanced stakeholder collab- oration via shared digital platforms [12]; accelerated project delivery through virtual simulations and predictive analytics [13]; comprehen- sive progress monitoring using 4D BIM integration [14]; improved qual- ity control and safety management [15,16]; and enhanced sustainability monitoring of carbon emissions and supply chains [17,18]. These capa- bilities position DT as particularly valuable for managing the complex, multi-domain aspects essential for successful renovation projects. Despite this potential, applying DT technology in renovation con- texts presents distinct challenges compared to new-build construction. New-build projects oﬀer greater ﬂexibility for technology integration without occupancy disruptions, whilst renovation projects must accom- modate existing building constraints and occupied environments, signif- icantly complicating DT implementation [19]. Current research demon- strates limited eﬀective application of DT in renovation settings, with existing tools often inadequately adapted from new-build contexts [20]. Furthermore, most DT implementations focus on single-domain moni- toring rather than the comprehensive, multi-domain approach required for managing renovation complexity, particularly during the onsite retroﬁtting phase where coordination challenges are most acute. This creates a critical research gap: while DT technology holds promise for addressing renovation barriers, its application for multi-domain perfor- mance monitoring during onsite renovation, including both technical implementation and user acceptance, remains underexplored. This paper addresses this gap through a pilot study investigating the development, deployment, and evaluation of a Renovation Digital Twin (RDT) that enables multi-domain performance monitoring and deliv- ers real-time feedback to project stakeholders. The research examines whether integrated DT platforms can eﬀectively bridge technological capability with practical adoption in renovation contexts, evaluating both technical viability and user acceptance factors that inﬂuence tech- nology diﬀusion. Using Design Science Research (DSR) methodology, the study grounds design requirements in stakeholder needs and eval- uates platform performance through technical validation on a French demonstration site and quantitative user acceptance assessment with construction professionals. The paper proceeds as follows: Section 2 reviews related research; Section 3 presents the research methodol- ogy; Section 4 details RDT design, development, and deployment; Sec- tion 5 covers validation and evaluation; and Section 6 presents conclu- sions. 2. Literature review 2.1. DT Applications in building retroﬁt Building retroﬁt currently relies on static tools with broad as- sumptions and generic inputs, often lacking digital modelling capabil- ities or methods for identifying optimal retroﬁt measures for speciﬁc projects [21,22]. DT technology oﬀers potential to address these limita- tions through dynamically updated digital replicas for retroﬁt planning and decision-making. However, existing research reveals critical lim- itations in current DT implementations for renovation contexts. Most studies fail to address the multi-stakeholder, multi-domain complexity inherent in renovation projects, where coordination between occupants, contractors, and project managers becomes essential for success. 2.1.1. Project phases and DT coverage Renovation projects involve distinct phases requiring diﬀerent mon- itoring approaches and stakeholder coordination mechanisms. Design and planning phases (D&P) focus on scenario evaluation and retroﬁt strategy development. The onsite retroﬁtting phase (OR) involves ac- tive construction work where real-time monitoring becomes critical for managing quality, safety, cost, and schedule performance. Operation and maintenance phases (O&M) address post-renovation building per- formance optimisation. Current DT research shows uneven distribution across these phases. Most studies address design/planning activities or post-renovation operations, whilst the onsite retroﬁtting phase, where coordination challenges are most acute, receives limited attention. This gap is particularly signiﬁcant given that renovation projects typically experience greater time and cost overruns, increased safety risks, and quality issues compared to new-build construction [23]. 2.1.2. Use cases and applications Building on Pan et al.’s [24] theoretical framework for DT in renova- tion, current applications have emerged across four primary domains. Indoor condition monitoring maintains occupant health and safety during renovation through environmental parameter tracking. Arsiwala et al. [25] developed a DT solution integrating IoT, BIM, and AI to au- tomate CO2 emission monitoring, demonstrating spatial data visuali- sation for air quality management with machine learning-based emis- sion prediction. Studies by Mainini et al. [26], Daniotti et al. [27], and Banﬁ et al. [28] developed systems for monitoring temperature, humid- ity, and CO2 emissions. Tan et al. [29] proposed a DT lighting system for dynamic occupant comfort management. Energy modelling and monitoring represents the most developed application area, reﬂecting retroﬁtting’s energy eﬃciency goals. Jradi et al. [22] proposed Dan- RETwin for decision-making and energy optimisation in existing build- ings through IoT sensors and AI-driven performance analysis. Bocullo et al. [30] developed a city block renovation approach using renew- able energy sources with scenario evaluation capabilities. Trombadore et al. [31] and Kaewunruen et al. [32] focused on energy modelling for retroﬁt scenario evaluation. Cost analysis applications address eco- nomic evaluation during planning phases. Trombadore et al. [31] devel- oped a methodology for heritage building renovation, providing tools to evaluate cost-eﬀectiveness whilst monitoring air quality, building per- formance, and user behaviour. Safety monitoring remains the least de- veloped domain. Khajavi et al. [33] developed a DT architecture em- phasising real-time ﬁre detection, incorporating data fusion, localisa- tion, and propagation simulation. Their approach demonstrated safety beneﬁts during renovation when building systems are oﬄine, though it addressed only ﬁre hazards. 2.1.3. Technical components DT implementations integrate four core technological components: data sensing devices for reality capture and performance tracking; BIM and 3D visualisation for digital representation; visualisation dashboards for stakeholder communication; and AI for automated data processing. Most studies rely on IoT sensors for data exchange between physical and digital systems, collecting parameters including temperature, hu- midity, occupancy, luminance, CO2, and energy consumption [22,27]. However, BIM capabilities remain underutilised, with most studies fo- cusing solely on 3D visualisation rather than leveraging design coordi- nation, clash detection, or 4D simulation functions [34]. Visualisation dashboards are underdeveloped, with limited implementations provid- ing comprehensive monitoring across multiple performance domains. AI adoption remains restricted to speciﬁc applications such as emission prediction and computer vision for hazard detection [25,33]. 2.2. Technology acceptance frameworks The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), developed by Davis [35], provides a framework for understanding technology adoption through Energy & Buildings 354 (2026) 116979 2 O. In construction con- texts, studies of BIM technology acceptance demonstrate that perceived usefulness typically emerges as the primary adoption driver [20]. How- ever, the complexity of occupied renovation environments may alter tra- ditional TAM relationships, potentially emphasising attitude toward use where trust and organisational alignment become critical factors. Lim- ited research has addressed DT technology acceptance in construction contexts [36]. Most validation approaches focus on technical function- ality testing without systematic user acceptance assessment, creating a gap in understanding how human factors inﬂuence DT adoption in multi-stakeholder renovation environments. 2.3. Research gaps and contributions Table 1 reveals three critical gaps in existing DT research for ren- ovation contexts. First, project phase analysis demonstrates uneven re- search distribution with only 27% of studies targeting onsite retroﬁtting activities where coordination challenges are most acute. Among onsite- focused studies, only the current RDT platform provides comprehensive multi-domain performance monitoring. Second, most studies focus on single-domain monitoring rather than the multi-domain approach re- quired for managing renovation complexity. Only one previous study attempted triple-domain monitoring [31], whilst none addresses all ﬁve performance domains simultaneously. Third, systematic assessment of DT acceptance using established theoretical frameworks remains lim- ited. This gap is signiﬁcant given that technology adoption in renova- tion projects depends on alignment with diverse organisational needs and stakeholder motivations beyond technical functionality. The contri- bution of this research is threefold: (i) present a pilot study of a compre- hensive RDT solution enabling multi-domain monitoring during onsite retroﬁtting activities; (ii) provide detailed methodology for DT imple- mentation in renovation contexts; and (iii) evaluate the platform using TAM methodology, addressing the gap in technology acceptance evalu- ation for multi-domain platforms in renovation environments. 3. Limitations and future work Several considerations should be acknowledged when interpreting these ﬁndings and planning future implementations. The RDT platform was tested on a single renovation project (Project Sarrazins, Lille), lim- iting generalisability to other retroﬁt contexts involving diﬀerent build- ing types, scales, and renovation scopes. The proof-of-concept demon- stration focused on functional veriﬁcation rather than comprehensive Energy & Buildings 354 (2026) 116979 10 O. Doukari et al. empirical validation. Whilst the TAM-based evaluation provided in- sights into user acceptance, the study lacks quantiﬁed performance im- provements (cost savings, schedule adherence, safety incident reduc- tion) that could validate the platform’s core beneﬁts. The short eval- uation period may not capture long-term usability issues or eﬀective- ness throughout complete renovation lifecycles. The TAM sample ex- hibited bias toward early-career professionals (72.5% with less than 5 years experience), potentially limiting applicability to experienced prac- titioners with diﬀerent adoption patterns. Technical limitations include absence of AI capabilities, with the simulation service supporting only historical KPI visualisation without predictive analytics for proactive is- sue identiﬁcation. Reliance on manual data entry through mobile appli- cations may introduce inconsistencies compared to automated sensor- based collection systems. The evaluation relied on self-reported percep- tions rather than observed usage behaviours, which may not accurately reﬂect actual adoption patterns in real-world scenarios [56]. The sus- tainability outcomes from the French demonstration site (Section 4.3, Table 5) represent a single case study with speciﬁc economic and regu- latory characteristics that may limit generalisability of the cost-beneﬁt ﬁndings. Future research should address these considerations through multi- site validation across diverse renovation projects (residential versus commercial, varying scales and types) in diﬀerent climate zones and regulatory environments to establish broader applicability. Longitudi- nal performance studies tracking actual project metrics before, dur- ing, and after RDT implementation would provide empirical evidence of platform eﬀectiveness. Research involving experienced profession- als and comparative analysis between RDT-supported projects and tra- ditional approaches would quantify relative beneﬁts and identify opti- mal implementation strategies. Technical enhancements should priori- tise automated data integration through IoT sensors alongside predictive analytics using deep learning for proactive project management sup- port [57]. Integration of predictive monitoring methods into the RDT platform to assess the impact of renovation works on indoor conditions could inform occupants and stakeholders before retroﬁtting processes begin [58]. Likewise, incorporation of material circularity metrics into the RDT platform to monitor reuse rates, recycled content percentages, and end-of-life recoverability would align renovation tracking with EU Circular Economy Action Plan targets [59]. The current RDT framework provides foundation for developing cognitive digital twin systems capa- ble of learning from project patterns, providing intelligent recommen- dations, and supporting autonomous decision-making processes [60]. 6. Conclusions This paper presents the development, deployment, and evaluation of an RDT platform designed to support multi-domain performance monitoring in building retroﬁt projects. The research addresses iden- tiﬁed gaps in DT applications for renovation contexts, where exist- ing frameworks predominantly focus on operational phase monitoring rather than onsite construction performance across quality, cost, sched- ule, safety, and environmental domains. The RDT was developed using DSR methodology, incorporating stakeholder input from RINNO consor- tium partners to deﬁne 13 KPIs across ﬁve performance domains. Tech- nical validation on the French demonstration site conﬁrmed functional operation across all seven system components, successful BIM integra- tion, multi-stakeholder access control, and GDPR compliance through mobile-based data collection. User acceptance evaluation with 102 construction professionals re- vealed strong positive perceptions across all TAM constructs. Attitude toward use emerged as the strongest adoption driver (RII = 0.894), with Purpose Conﬁdence and Needs Alignment as highest-rated items (both RII = 0.92), demonstrating strong conceptual acceptance of the plat- form’s value and relevance. System Integration (RII = 0.91) emerged as the most valued ease of use factor, whilst Independent Operation (RII = 0.82) identiﬁed training and support as priority enhancement areas. These ﬁndings conﬁrm that interoperability with established BIM work- ﬂows critically facilitates adoption [55]. Theoretically, the research extends TAM to multi-stakeholder con- struction environments, revealing that attitude-driven acceptance may be as important as perceived usefulness in complex renovation con- texts. This ﬁnding provides empirical support for the RINNO barrier framework [8], demonstrating that human barriers (trust, alignment) can equal technological barriers (functionality, usability) in determining adoption outcomes. The study advances socio-technical systems theory by demonstrating successful coordination of multiple performance do- mains through GDPR-compliant architecture with stakeholder-speciﬁc access control. Practically, the stakeholder-informed design process positioned users as active co-creators, embodying the ‘human-in-the-loop’ prin- ciple essential for eﬀective DT implementation [61]. Strong perfor- mance of integration-related factors provides empirical guidance em- phasising that seamless workﬂow integration represents a critical suc- cess factor. The platform demonstrates potential for establishing new project management routines supporting sustainability’s three pillars: enhanced collaboration (social), waste reduction (environmental), and improved cost and schedule management (economic). Deployment on the French demonstration site illustrates this multi-domain capability: whilst achieving substantial environmental performance (73% emis- sions reduction), the project faced signiﬁcant economic challenges (39% cost increase, NPV: -e326,000), exemplifying the complex trade-oﬀs the RDT’s KPI tracking framework is designed to make transparent to stake- holders. Successful DT implementation requires not only technical capa- bility but also systematic attention to human factors, training provision, and organisational change management processes that enable the socio- technical transformation necessary for deep renovation acceleration. Cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) lining is an emerging trenchless technology used to rehabilitate deteriorated pipes. However, the buckling behavior of CIPP liner-rehabilitated pipes is insufficiently investigated. This study presents an integrated framework for real-time structural health monitoring and damage detection for underground pipelines by leveraging the knowledge of distributed fiber optic sensors (DFOS), finite element analysis (FEA), deep learning (DL) algorithms, and digital twin. The study experimentally and computationally investigates the structural behavior of a single steel substrate and a CIPP-rehabilitated steel substrate under eccentric compression con - ditions. The application of DFOS enables the self-sensing function of the pipelines with high-resolution, continuous strain measurement. The finite element model and DL-based strain field correlation are employed to complement the function of DFOS from strain field monitoring to structural deformation assessment, facili - tating the development of virtual replicates of self-sensed pipes. The study further explores the location and severity of structural damage through numerical and theoretical strain analysis, enabling the detection of minor but critical defects. The developed digital twin framework enables real-time structural visualization and en - hances structural damage assessment. By accurately identifying the early-stage plastic region of deformation and quantifying crack severity, the framework supports predictive maintenance and improves pipeline reliability. This proactive approach improves the safety of underground pipelines, extends their service life, and reduces maintenance costs, demonstrating its practical potential for long-term pipeline infrastructure management. 1. Introduction Underground pipeline networks form the backbone of modern urban infrastructure, enabling the efficient transportation of water, gas, sewage, and various industrial fluids [1] . The underground pipeline systems are essential to economic growth, public health, and environ - mental protection. However, with the progression of time, underground pipelines become increasingly susceptible to deterioration due to ma - terial degradation, external loads, corrosion, and soil-structure in - teractions [2] . The failure of underground pipelines can result in severe economic losses, environmental contamination, service disruptions, and even loss of life. Consequently, ensuring the long-term structural integrity of underground pipelines has become a critical challenge in civil and infrastructure applications. Conventional methods, such as replacing deteriorated pipelines, often result in substantial inconvenience to local residents and industrial facilities due to service interruption. In contrast to conventional methods, various trenchless technologies have been developed to restore the function of pipelines while minimizing excavation and associated disruptions [3] . Among them, the cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) lining method has gained wide - spread recognition for its adaptability to different pipe materials and sizes, cost-effectiveness, and capacity to restore the structural integrity of deteriorated pipes [4] . The CIPP lining method involves inserting a resin-based liner mate - rial into an underground pipe. After being cured in place appropriately, the liner effectively forms a new structural layer within the host pipe [5] . Enabled by the enhanced structural integrity provided by the CIPP liner, this method enhances pipeline durability, improves hydraulic ef - ficiency, and extends the service life of the liner-rehabilitated pipe sys - tem. Common damage mechanisms include cracking, delamination, and deformation due to internal or external interferences, with buckling being one of the most critical failure modes [6–8] . Buckling in underground pipelines, whether orig - inal or rehabilitated with a liner, can be caused by soil loads, ground - water pressure, traffic-induced stresses, or vacuum-induced forces [9–12] . Since such failures often occur suddenly and can lead to critical structural instability, early detection and mitigation are essential for ensuring the pipeline’s reliability. Prior studies have evaluated the structural performance of CIPP liners through experimental testing and analytical or numerical modeling, including investigations of ovaliza - tion, local buckling instability, liner–host pipe interaction, and critical capacity [13–16] . These studies have improved the understanding of the progression of buckling from initial imperfection to post-buckling deformation in liner-rehabilitated pipes. However, existing in - vestigations generally rely on visual inspection or discrete sensors, which are unable to capture the distributed strain evolution that governs buckling initiation. Therefore, real-time tracking of buckling behavior and deterioration in liner-rehabilitated systems remains an open chal - lenge, due to the inaccessibility of pipelines and the complexity of their operating environment. Structural health monitoring (SHM) is an approach to assess the condition of critical infrastructure, providing valuable insights that can help detect early signs of damage and prevent catastrophic failures [17–19] . However, traditional SHM techniques face significant chal - lenges when applied to underground pipelines. Conventional point-based sensors, such as strain gauges or fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors, are difficult to deploy in underground environments and offer limited spatial coverage. Therefore, these sensors are not suitable for monitoring underground pipelines, where the locations of potential failures are unpredictable. To address these challenges, distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) has emerged as a promising solution to monitor the structural health condition of underground pipelines in real-time [20] . Unlike conventional sensors, DFOS enables spatially continuous strain measurements along the entire length of the optic fiber. This continuous coverage allows for real-time strain monitoring over a large area. Anomalous strain signals are indicative of structural irregularities, enabling both damage identification and localization. As a result, DFOS facilitates the timely detection of localized damage, enables the assess - ment of overall strain distribution, and monitors structural performance with higher resolution and accuracy. Additionally, DFOS offers several advantages, including its ability to work in harsh environments, its high sensitivity to strain variations, and its long-term stability, making it an ideal choice for underground applications. Recent studies have employed DFOS as a strain monitoring tool for various underground applications, including fractured steel pipelines subjected to operational pressure [21] , polyvinyl chloride liners under grouting pressure [22] , and corrugated steel culverts under traffic loading [23] . These previous studies demonstrate the capability of DFOS to provide continuous strain monitoring in underground structures, establishing a foundation for its effective integration into real-time SHM applications. The effectiveness of DFOS relies on its underlying principle of analyzing backscattered light along the optical fiber. In an optical distributed sensor interrogator, a laser source with a continuously varying frequency is injected into the fiber, and the backscattered Rayleigh signal is analyzed. Since the Rayleigh scattering pattern is unique to each segment of the fiber, any mechanical strain changes the local optical path, resulting in a shift in the backscattered signal. By comparing these shifts with the reference state, highly precise distributed strain measurements can be obtained at sub-millimeter-scale spatial resolution (SR). This technique makes Rayleigh-based DFOS particularly suitable for detecting fine-scale strain variations and tracking minor defect initiation and propagation. As a result, it is highly promising for SHM applications of underground pipes, where direct visual detection is often infeasible. DFOS provides a powerful tool for monitoring the structural health condition of underground pipelines. However, it is unrealistic to have DFOS fully cover the entire pipe due to the high cost of fiber optic sensors. Additionally, raw sensing data alone may not fully capture the complexities of pipeline behavior under real-world conditions. There - fore, in practical deployment, DFOS is installed on instrumented seg - ments that terminate at accessible interrogation points. Since pipeline distress typically occurs locally, the sensing data from these instru - mented segments are used to update finite element models of the broader system. By combining localized measurements with numerical predictions, the integration of DFOS and finite element analysis (FEA) provides a comprehensive assessment of the structural performance of the underground pipeline system. FEA-based numerical modeling en - ables the simulation of various loading conditions, damage mechanisms, and failure modes, allowing for the prediction of how a pipeline will behave under different scenarios [24,25] . This approach shows a promising approach to conduct virtual testing on pipelines and generate a dynamic, real-time digital replica through digital twin technology. In the context of infrastructure, the digital twin framework can continu - ously update and provide a virtual representation that offers direct insight into the structural condition [26] . Wang et al. [27] applied polynomial fitting to process experimental and numerical strain data and reconstruct the structural deformation of a pipe-liner system in a three-dimensional digital twin model. Wang et al. [28] developed a digital twin system for pipeline fatigue crack monitoring through the internet of things, advanced analytics, cloud computing, and virtual reality. However, there is a lack of efficient and timely digital twin modeling methods for SHM in pipeline systems, as current practices require substantial computational time for data post-processing. To address this issue, the incorporation of machine learning (ML) algo - rithms represents a further step toward real-time data processing be - tween the physical twin and the digital twin [29] . Limited studies have advanced digital twin-based SHM using ML approaches in infrastructure domains, most of which focus on bridge components [30–32] , moni - toring deflection, moment, and crack propagation. Similar advance - ments have not yet been fully realized for underground pipeline infrastructure applications. Building on these established practices, extending the use of ML toward continuous condition monitoring of pipeline structures, enabling more effective and preventive SHM stra - tegies, is necessary. In order to enhance the effectiveness of SHM for underground pipeline infrastructure, it is important to enable preventive damage identification and localization. Hoult et al. [33] created artificial dam - ages in steel plate samples and detected subsequent deterioration using DFOS by measuring strain variations. Lan et al. [34] installed DFOS on a perforated steel pipeline to measure the longitudinal strain distribution under ground movement. They found that while the global behavior of the perforated pipeline remained unchanged, the strain variation around the perforation was significant, highlighting the capability of DFOS for damage identification in underground pipeline applications. However, these studies were based on predefined, visually identifiable damages. To bridge this gap, DFOS needs to be utilized to continuously monitor the full structural damage evolution, from initially intact con - ditions through progressive deterioration leading to failure, enabling comprehensive SHM of damage propagation and facilitating early-stage damage assessment. One key component of structural damage originates from the plastic deformation of steel, which induces permanent defor - mation. Once the localized strain exceeds the yield strain, the affected region enters plasticity. This plastic region of deformation cannot restore its original shape after the external load is removed, resulting in permanent deformation. This irreversible deformation compromises the strength, bearing capacity, and transmission efficiency of the under - ground pipe [35] . Another key focus is the investigation of crack initi - ation at critical structural interfaces, such as the adhesive layer. The adhesive layer is the intermediate layer between the pipe and CIPP liner, used to bond the liner firmly to the host pipe [36] . This layer has been largely overlooked in previous studies despite its impact on the overall rehabilitation performance of the CIPP liner. Large structural J. Duan et al. Therefore, it is essential to identify the conditions and lo - cations of such cracks. In this context, integrating DFOS with the CIPP liner is suggested as a potential solution to monitor the condition of the entire liner-rehabilitated pipe. While the installation of DFOS directly on the underground pipe remains challenging, embedding DFOS within the liner prior to installation offers a promising alternative. Since DFOS is not directly attached to the pipe surface, the strain monitoring results may be affected, making it critical to investigate the performance of DFOS-integrated liners in effectively monitoring pipe conditions. Introduction With the continuous expansion of global infrastructure networks and the growing number of structures that are reaching or exceeding their design service life, the operation and maintenance (O&M) of civil infrastructure (CI) is facing increasing complexity. Data from the New Climate Economy and McKinsey show that global infrastructure assets now exceed USD 100 trillion. Around 60% of bridges and roads are already in mid- or late-life stages and are entering periods of concentrated aging and performance decline [1]. According to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and the ASCE report based on the National Bridge Inventory (NBI), the United States has more than 619,000 bridges. Over 42% of these bridges have been in service for more than 50 years, and about 7.5% are classified as structurally deficient [2]. Studies by the OECD and the European Union further show that many key bridges and tunnels in the core Trans-European Transport Network (TEN-T) were built in the 1960s and 1970s and now face pressure from outdated functions and delayed rehabilitation [3]. Smart monitoring devices, large-scale sensor networks, and automated inspection systems are driving exponential growth in data volume. In intelligent transportation systems and infrastructure IoT deployments, city- and nation-level networks rely on millions of sensor nodes, and a single large monitoring system can generate gigabytes to terabytes of high-frequency data each day [4,5]. These trends indicate that modern CI has become a large-scale and highly complex system with multi- scale characteristics, wide spatial coverage, diverse structural forms, and heterogeneous data sources. Although many countries are promoting smart monitoring, health diagnosis, and digital management, current CI O&M systems are approaching the practical limits of purely data-driven automation when applied to infrastructure networks of this scale and complexity. This challenge highlights the need for digital twin (DT) frameworks that go beyond data aggregation and visualization, and that can explicitly incorporate human expertise, engineering knowledge, and explainable reasoning mechanisms. DTs have become an important technical pathway for CI O&M. A DT establishes a dynamic mapping among the physical structure, the virtual model, and real-time multi- source data. This mapping enables state visualization, behavior prediction, and quantitative decision support, which strengthens monitoring, diagnosis, and risk assessment. However, recent reviews in leading international journals show several major limitations in current DT applications for infrastructure: (1) high cost and complexity of multi-source data fusion and real-time updating; (2) weak integration between DT workflows and organizational or human decision processes; and (3) limited capability to formalize structural codes and engineering knowledge within DT systems. In parallel, deep learning and data- driven methods in Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) have achieved notable progress but still face key challenges, including incomplete data, weak explainability, poor scene generalization, and limited ability to absorb expert knowledge and engineering standards. Recent studies report that common SHM models for images and sensor data exhibit poor scalability, weak real-time performance, insufficient coupling of multi-variable features, and limited interpretability, which restrict real engineering deployment [7]. Similar conclusions have been repeatedly emphasized in multimodal SHM research. Compared with fully automated monitoring algorithms, traditional manual inspection and expert-based judgment retain strong advantages in understanding structural codes, recognizing subtle abnormalities, and handling complex field conditions. However, this human-centered approach faces severe scalability constraints when applied to large-scale infrastructure networks. According to the White Paper Development of China’s Rural Roads in the New Era issued by the State Council Information Office, China completed the rehabilitation of 58,000 unsafe rural bridges over the past decade. The proportion of Class I, II, and III rural road bridges increased from 83.2% to 98%, and the number of unsafe bridges declined each year [8]. This achievement required extensive human and material resources within a concentrated time period, showing that large-scale O&M relies heavily on massive resource input. We do not focus on quantitative performance comparison in this paper. This will be studied in future work. 2. Literature Review 2.1. Digital Twin Modeling DTs are a key technology in CI. They link physical structures with virtual models and support real-time reflection and prediction of structural conditions. From a system engineering point of view, a DT has continuous and two-way mapping between the physical entity, the virtual model, and related data streams. Because of this mapping, the digital model can change over time and follow the operating state of the infrastructure [9]. In construction and infrastructure projects, DT modeling usually uses geometric information, material parameters, and sensor networks to build accurate models of the physical system [10]. In the virtual domain, many studies use hybrid modeling methods. These methods combine mechanism-based, data-driven, and knowledge-based approaches. Abualdenien and Borrmann show that clearly defined meta-models and multi-level model structures are important for keeping consistency and allowing models to work at different levels of detail [ 11]. Chiachío et al. show that virtual models should be updated and checked continuously with multi-source monitoring data. In this way, a closed loop can be built between the physical system and the digital system [ 12]. Jiang et al. also propose a multi-domain DT framework. This framework organizes different types of data, models, and semantic information in one unified structure, so it can support complex infrastructure systems [13]. Recent studies show that DT development is changing. In the past, it mainly focused on a “physical-virtual” two-domain framework. Now, it is moving to a more integrated “human-machine-knowledge” framework. In this new view, human interaction and knowl- edge are treated as core parts of the DT system. They are not seen as external inputs anymore. Abstract: With rapid urbanization, the utilization of underground space has become an important part of infrastructure. However, the stability of underground spaces such as large caverns remains a key challenge in civil engineering throughout the lifecycle of a project. Traditional methods of stability assessment rely on static models and periodic monitoring and often fail to capture real-time changes in rock behavior, leading to potential safety risks and, in severe cases, even the collapse of underground infrastructure. To address this challenge, this study introduces a digital twin (DT) framework to improve stability assessments and monitor deformations in underground structures. The framework enables the continuous monitoring and adaptive optimization of rock support systems by combining real-time sensor data with virtual simulations. A five-dimensional DT framework comprises physical objects, virtual objects, service systems, DT data, and their interconnections. It incorporates six key modules, which are structure, geology, material, behavior, performance, and environment, to enhance the understanding of cavern stability. The framework is based on Industry Foundation Classes standards to ensure seamless data exchange, interoperability, and the standardized representation of geotechnical and structural data. A seven-step methodology is developed for this framework, encompassing geological assessment, virtual modeling, Building Information Modeling (BIM)-based de- sign, construction processes, real-time monitoring, and optimization strategies. To evaluate its effectiveness, the framework is applied to a case study, demonstrating improvements in deformation monitoring and rock support efficiency. The findings highlight the poten- tial of integrating DT with BIM to enhance safety, reliability, and long-term stability in underground construction projects. The Red Trigger Level is typically triggered when deformation exceeds 80% of the predicted maximum, indicating a critical risk of structural compromise. It serves as the final threshold, above which construction activities must be suspended to safeguard both personnel and structural integrity. Emergency protocols must be executed, which may include shotcrete reinforcement, structural support installations, or site evacuation. On breaching this level—or if monitoring data suggest that the Red Trigger Level is likely to be reached—the structure must be immediately inspected, and emergency response and contingency plans should be instigated without delay. Additionally, a comprehensive risk assessment must be conducted to address any further movement or adverse trends resulting from ongoing construction activity. 3. Background BIM reconstructions of engineering slopes using LiDAR point cloud data involve two critical phases: initial geometric parameter extraction of drainage channels through point cloud segmentation, followed by parametric BIM reconstruction using specialized software. This two- phase method ensures accurate digital representation of structural elements. 2.1. Related research Accurately identifying object boundaries within point cloud data is typically achieved through two primary methods: (1) directly seg - menting 3D point clouds, and (2) mapping 2D segmentation results into 3D space [ 20 ]. Deep learning has emerged as a dominant approach for both 2D and 3D semantic segmentation, offering advanced capabilities for object recognition and boundary delineation. These segmentation results can then be utilized in parametric modeling methods to automate the reconstruction of BIM models. 2.1.1. 3D Point Clouds Segmentation While pioneering 3D methods like PointNet [ 24 ] introduced permutation-invariant networks through multi-layer perceptrons and max pooling, subsequent advancements such as PointNet ++ [ 25 ] improved local feature capture through set abstraction. Modern archi - tectures including PointWeb [ 26 ] and RandLA-Net [ 27 ] address com - plex scene processing through context-aware aggregation and efficient sampling. Large-scale benchmarks like SemanticKITTI [ 28 ] and KITTI360 [ 29 ] have significantly advanced data-driven segmentation, with recent supervised learning approaches achieving notable success in multi-object segmentation for indoor and autonomous driving scenarios [ 30 , 31 ]. However, challenges persist in processing large-scale, un - structured point clouds efficiently [ 21 ], particularly for extracting slender elements like drainage channels. The alternative 2D projection approach, which converts 3D data into 2D depth maps, leverages mature image segmentation techniques to significantly reduce computational loads compared with direct 3D processing. 2.1.2. 2D Image Segmentation The evolution of 2D segmentation architectures has progressed through three distinct generations: (1) Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) [ 32 ] or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [ 33 – 38 ] excel - ling in texture pattern recognition, (2) Vision Transformers (ViTs) [ 39 , 40 ] that capture long-range dependencies, and (3) foundation models like Segment Anything Model (SAM) [ 41 , 42 ] that enables zero- shot adaptation for downstream tasks. These methods have been widely applied in tasks related to construction and infrastructure maintenance, such as crack segmentation [ 43 , 44 ], road network segmentation [ 45 , 46 ] and in-door pipeline segmentation [ 20 , 21 ]. To preserve the connectivity of linear objects, some studies focus on refining imperfect road seg - mentation results to maintain their inherent connectivity [ 47 ]. Notably, most existing models are trained on datasets, such as ImageNet [ 48 ] for general vision tasks and Chest X-ray8 [ 49 ] for medical imaging, which significantly differ from point cloud-derived imagery. 2.1.3. Parametric Modeling Grasshopper and Dynamo have become pivotal tools in parametric design and cross-platform collaboration within engineering sectors. Their integration of Visual Programming Languages (VPLs) enables automated workflows and complex geometric manipulation [ 50 – 54 ]. These tools seamlessly connect conceptual design with technical execution through computational logic, supporting applications from generative design to data-driven optimization. For instance, Grass - hopper facilitates AI-enhanced workflows, such as converting 2D sketches into 3D structural models via deep learning [ 51 ], while Dy - namo streamlines BIM data interoperability, enabling efficient data transfer between Revit and external platforms like Excel for documen - tation [ 54 ]. Their adaptability extends to advanced computational sce - narios, including LiDAR-based point cloud processing for scan to BIM [ 50 , 53 , 55 ] and reinforcement learning-driven spatial optimization [ 52 ]. Custom workflows are necessary to address slope-specific challenges, such as the integration of irregular geometries and domain-specific analysis. 2.2. First, computa - tional bottlenecks persist in processing city-scale LiDAR point clouds, where conventional 3D segmentation models struggle to efficiently extract narrow elements like drainage channels from millions of un - structured points. Methodology The proposed framework for digitizing massive engineered slopes comprises four principal components ( Fig. 2 ): (1) domain knowledge- incorporated image dataset generation, (2) deep learning-based image segmentation, (3) Grasshopper-based fully automated BIM reconstruc - tion, and (4) digital twin platform interactive visualization. This study employs an image-based deep learning method to delin - eate slope surface drainage channels. The initial phase involves gener - ating depth images from LiDAR point clouds, incorporating domain knowledge to optimally distinguish the characteristics of slope surface drainage channels. Slope surface drainage channels are then delineated manually as labels for segmentation. The segmentation phase implements customized CNN model Drain - Net to address the challenge of detecting local linear features while preserving global contextual awareness. DrainNet integrates directional convolution blocks (horizontal and vertical) for local feature extraction, dilated convolution blocks for expanded receptive fields, and channel attention modules for better generalization on small datasets. DrainNet achieves optimal performance in linear structure identification, partic - ularly for slope surface drainage channel delineation. The BIM reconstruction phase establishes an automated workflow combining Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data with projected 3D channel mappings. A Rhino-Grasshopper visual programming pipeline processes pre-conditioned topography and channel data through customized Python and C# scripts, incorporating parametric channel cross-sections to generate lightweight BIM models. The final integration phase implements a web-based city-scale digital twin through Vue.js frontend interfacing with Cesium and Google Earth as base maps. Georeferenced BIM models are superimposed on Hong Kong ’ s photogrammetric base maps, enabling interactive 3D visualiza - tion with attribute query functionalities for enhanced slope safety management and decision support. 4. Analysis of failure cases in Drainage Channel delineation As illustrated in Fig. 14 , failure cases in image segmentation fall into two main categories: overestimation and underestimation. Both occur in areas where the point cloud data is sparse or vacant, leading to unex - pected topographic variations that disrupt curvature-based analysis. This results in either false positives resembling drainage channels or large blanks in the image. From a model design perspective, such sparsity can amplify noise in feature extraction, causing the network to misinterpret subtle edges or fail to connect fragmented structures. To address these challenges, a twofold strategy is adopted. First, during dataset annotation, incomplete or discontinuous drainage channels are manually labeled as continuous structures to help the model recognize gaps due to data sparsity ( Fig. 14 ). Second, DrainNet with perpendicular 1D convolutions and channel attention is developed to better capture sparse drainage channel features and improve robustness against incomplete data. Nevertheless, data completeness remains crucial, as it fundamentally affects segmentation accuracy and subsequent modeling. 7.2. Limitations and future work The method proposed in this research is suitable for drainage chan - nels on slopes that are exposed and uncovered, which can be detected through surface curvature analysis and subsequently segmented using image-based methods. The available data from SIS provides only approximate cross-sectional dimensions, thus empirically determined parameters are applied uniformly for modeling purpose. Using the parametric modeling method, the creation of channel section can be easily generalized when detailed parameters are available. The current segmentation model has limitations in accurately resolving channel width parameters. Alternatively, channel geometry could be derived by converting deep learning segmentation results into shapefiles, though this approach requires further research. Notably, the application of closure operations for maintaining hydraulic connectivity is a simplified way. This post-processing step, while effective, is a heuristic simplifi - cation that operates under the assumption that all discontinuities are segmentation errors. Consequently, it may erroneously connect chan - nels that are geomorphologically distinct or introduce non-physical al - terations to the channel ’ s shape. The performance of this operation is also contingent upon a relatively complete initial segmentation. Future work should explore more sophisticated approach to establish connec - tivity, which would more robustly handle complex drainage networks and reduce the reliance on such geometric heuristics. This research developed an edge-cloud collaborative framework for vibration anomaly detection and vibration source localization. Firstly, a high-precision vibration sensor with wireless time synchronization and edge computing capability is introduced. Secondly, a vibration anomaly detection algorithm is proposed based on edge computing of sensor nodes. Thirdly, a vibration source localization algorithm is proposed based on cloud computing of a structural dynamics digital twin model. Finally, the effectiveness of the proposed methods is validated through a case study of a 17-story high-rise building, and the results indicate that vibration anomaly events can be reliably detected and localized. The outcomes of this study are expected to provide a useful reference for vibration anomaly management of high-rise buildings. 1. Introduction According to the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, there are more than 6400 high-rise buildings over 150 m built worldwide by March 1, 2023 [1] . High-rise buildings serve crucial urban functions, including hospitals and office spaces. Any sudden occurrence of vibra - tion anomalies in these structures can impair their functionality, resulting in substantial direct and indirect economic losses [2] . Several high-rise buildings have experienced vibration anomalies that differ from those caused by typhoons or earthquakes. These vibra - tions are more sudden and usually cause widespread panic. It is usually impossible to find the cause of the vibration in the first place, and subsequent investigations result in prolonged closures of the buildings, leading to significant economic losses. For example, on May 18, 2021, the Saige Building ( Fig. 1 a) in Shenzhen encountered a vibration anomaly caused by wind-induced vortex resonance of the top-floor mast [3] . After nearly four months of closure, the building resumed operation, but the owner suffered a net profit loss of approximately 55 million RMB that year [4] . Similarly, on February 7, 2013, occupants on the 20th floor of the Information Building in Shanghai experienced intermittent abnormal shaking, prompting an emergency evacuation. Nonetheless, the cause of the building's shaking remains unknown [5] . Additionally, on September 5, 2013, a 50-story office building in Nanjing underwent several intense up-and-down shaking events within an hour due to construction work involving a road roller at a nearby construction site [6] . Moreover, on July 5, 2011, TechnoMart ( Fig. 1 b), a 39-story shopping mall building in Seoul, experienced abnormal vertical shaking lasting 10 min due to crowd movement inside the building, leading to a two-day evacuation of residents [7,8] . Lastly, on January 20, 2022, a 33-story high-rise residential building in Seoul encountered structural resonance caused by group dancing within the building after two months of investigation and lockdown [9] . High-rise buildings typically exhibit relatively low lateral stiffness and damping ratios, making them susceptible to external excitations such as wind loads, crowd activities, and mechanical vibrations. Struc - tural vibrations induced by earthquakes or typhoons exhibit distinct characteristics that can be quickly identified to prevent unnecessary panic. Vibrations caused by equipment operation or human activities within the 1 – 10 Hz frequency range not only coincide with many high- order vibration modes of tall buildings but also fall within the sensitive * Corresponding author. The inability to promptly determine the cause and location of such vibration sources often leads to public panic events. The aforementioned vibration anomaly events of high-rise buildings in Shenzhen, Seoul, and Nanjing demonstrate that vibration sources can be located at the top, middle, and base of the buildings. Yet, in each case, the inability to immediately identify the vibration source led to building evacuations and months-long investigations before resuming normal operations, resulting in substantial economic losses. If a vibration anomaly can be detected and localized while still weak (below the human perception threshold), timely elimination of the source can prevent such events from escalating into public incidents. In the field of structural health monitoring (SHM) for high-rise buildings, extensive research has been conducted on operational modal analysis (OMA) [11 – 14] and damage detection and localization [15 – 17] . However, limited research has been reported on the detection and localization of vibration anomalies specifically for high-rise build - ings. Existing research on vibration source localization primarily focuses on seismic localization, occupant localization, and pipeline leakage localization. For instance, travel-time inversion was proposed to deter - mine earthquake hypocenter coordinates [18] . The double-difference earthquake location method was proposed to minimize the residuals between observed and theoretical travel-time differences of seismic pairs at each station [19] . Schloemann et al. [20] introduced a time difference of arrival (TDoA)-based method to localize impact sources within buildings. Mirshekari et al. [21] proposed an occupant localiza - tion approach using TDoA and footstep-induced floor vibrations. Lah et al. [22] introduced a pipeline leakage localization method by esti - mating TDoA through vibration signal correlation. Wang and Zhu [23] developed a two-stage impact source localization method based on the time of arrival (ToA) of impact waves recorded from multiple vibration monitoring points. The localization accuracy was validated through numerical simulations and field experiments conducted at a construc - tion site with ongoing impact piling work. These algorithms were designed for transient pulse excitations, whereas high-rise building vibration anomalies are typically non-pulse- like and persistent, limiting the applicability of TDoA/ToA methods. Consequently, vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings remain underexplored. In most SHM applications, the SHM system is designed based on the cloud computing pattern, where all sensor data are transmitted to a cloud server for centralized processing [24,25] . While this pattern is feasible in wired SHM systems, it has been found to be challenging and inappropriate for wireless sensor networks (WSNs) when it comes to transmitting large time-history data [26] . On one hand, implementing a WSN based on the cloud computing pattern would result in high energy consumption and reduced battery life of sensor nodes due to the continuous wireless connection to the cloud server and the significant data transmission requirements for time-history vibration data. On the other hand, the cloud computing pattern is not suitable for real-time detection of vibration anomalies due to the inherent delay in data transmission and cloud analysis. Edge computing is a computing paradigm that performs computation at the network edge, which has gained significant research interest in the field of SHM. For instance, Wei et al. [27] proposed a multi-resource computing offload algorithm for optimizing energy consumption in mobile edge computing, resulting in more than 12 % of energy saving. Zonzini et al. [28] introduced an innovative extreme edge data compression strategy tailored for low-power and low-cost SHM appli - cations. Experimental results from wind turbine monitoring demon - strated that this strategy reduced the energy consumption of a sensor node by over 16 %. Additionally, Abner et al. [29] presented three strategies for conserving battery power in wireless monitoring sensors for infrastructures using edge computing, significantly enhancing the sensor's battery lifespan and reducing the frequency of on-site hardware maintenance. Based on the above research, edge computing shows promise in addressing challenges related to response time, battery life, and bandwidth costs [30] . However, due to limited computing and memory resources, as well as network bandwidth constraints, edge computing faces challenges in achieving satisfactory performance when dealing with complex problems. Owing to the complementary advantages and disadvantages of cloud computing and edge computing, an increasingly common approach is to integrate them in real-world applications [31] . This approach is known as edge-cloud collaboration (ECC), which combines the low latency characteristics of edge computing with the powerful computing capa - bilities of cloud computing [32] . Early explorations of ECC have been conducted in both academia and industry. For example, Yu et al. [33] designed an ECC framework for industrial anomaly detection, where cloud algorithms are responsible for extracting and optimizing anomaly diagnosis rules, while edge algorithms detect anomalies based on these rules. This method significantly reduces network load and computa - tional pressure on the cloud. Gang et al. [34] proposed an ECC frame - work for electric load monitoring, which performs processing at the edge and optimization in the cloud. Ding et al. [35] introduced an ECC framework for healthcare monitoring that enables lesion detection at the edge and high-performance modeling in the cloud. As observed, current ECC frameworks primarily operate in the mode of edge detection with cloud-based dataset training or modeling. However, no reports have been made on edge-cloud collaborative intelligence for anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings. The aforementioned edge and clouding computing can help to ac - quire, process and analysis data from physical world. When combined with more complicated simulation and visualization models as the dig - ital replica of the real world entities, the concept of digital twin can be involved. A digital twin represents a cyber-physical system that enables visualization, modeling, simulation, analysis, prediction, and optimi - zation [36] . It has found applications in various civil engineering fields, including buildings [37 – 40] , infrastructure [41,42] , and construction sites [43,44] . The digital twin is considered a promising technology with the potential to transform the construction industry and provide solu - tions to various challenges [45] . Furthermore, Jiang et al. [46] argued that technologies such as digital twin, building information modeling (BIM), and cyber-physical systems (CPS) are not mutually exclusive; instead, they can mutually reinforce civil engineering digitalization. Therefore, the digital twin represents a broader concept that can be integrated with edge-cloud collaborative computing, BIM models, and simulation models to establish a comprehensive digital system for enhanced management and disaster prevention of civil structures. Therefore, an edge-cloud collaborative digital twin framework is presented for the purpose of vibration anomaly detection and localiza - tion in high-rise buildings. Discussion and limitations This study presents an integrated wireless smart sensor system with edge-cloud computing algorithms for vibration monitoring in high-rise buildings, featuring three key innovations. First, we developed an edge-cloud collaborative framework that distributes vibration detection algorithms to edge nodes, which reduces cloud computing workload and data transmission requirements while enabling near real-time vibration source localization and visualization. Second, we designed a compact, highly integrated wireless sensor that combines high-precision vibration measurement (0.2 μ g/(Hz) 1/2 noise density) with onboard edge computing capabilities (Linux-based Python environment), achieves less than 20 μ s time synchronization through a novel LoRa-based chain network, and enables direct 4 G cloud connectivity without requiring gateway nodes. Third, we established a digital twin-based vibration source localization methodology employing an updated flexural-shear MDOF model for building simulation, performing dynamic analysis under floor-specific excitations, and implementing SAF vector matching between simulated and measured responses to identify vibration sources. This study demonstrates the feasibility of an edge-cloud collabora - tive framework and proposes a novel vibration source localization method. However, three key limitations warrant discussion to guide future research directions: (1) The current SAF vector comparison method achieves only zonal- level localization because SAF vectors exhibit similar patterns when excitations are applied to adjacent floors. This limitation underscores the need for more precise floor-specific identification techniques. (2) The framework currently supports only single-frequency steady- state excitations. Extending it to multi-source and non-stationary vi - bration scenarios will require further theoretical and experimental development. Fig. 23. Conclusions An edge-cloud collaborative digital twin framework for vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings is proposed in this study. The framework includes a wireless time-synchronized smart sensor (WTSS), a series of vibration anomaly detection and localization methods, and a BIM-empowered online platform. The inferred conclu - sions are as follows: (1) A digital twin framework for vibration monitoring of high-rise buildings is proposed in this study. The framework features vibration monitoring and anomaly detection at the edge sensors. Meanwhile, the digital twin model updating, vibration source localization, and platform management are performed at the cloud server. The assessment results can be visualized through a BIM-based management platform. (2) A wireless time-synchronized smart sensor (WTSS) is proposed in this study. The sensor integrates a high-sensitive accelerometer, an edge computing module, a wireless data transmission module and a wireless time synchronization module. The WTSS sensor is very compact in size with the dimension of 10 cm*8 cm*4.5 cm. The sensitive and vibration measurement performance of the WTSS sensor is validated by comparing with the PCB393B31 piezoelectric sensor. (3) An edge-cloud collaborative assessment method for vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings is proposed. The method was validated through a series of harmonic excitation tests for a 17-story high-rise teaching building. A very weak 8 Hz harmonic excitation with the excitation force amplitude of 82.9 N was applied using a shaking table deployed on 5th, 8th, 11th, 14th, and 17th floors respectively. Although the excitation is very weak, the proposed WTSS sensors can clearly identify the features of the excitation and the vi - bration anomaly event can be detected through the edge computing of Fig. 25. More - over, the location of the vibration source can be estimated through the cloud computing of the proposed vibration localization algorithm using the structural dynamics digital twin model. In summary, the outcomes of this study are expected to provide a useful tool for the rapid detection and localization of vibration anomaly in high-rise buildings and assist in safety operation and maintenance of building structures.
