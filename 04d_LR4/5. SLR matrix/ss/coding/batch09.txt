Introduction With the rapid growth of the world’s population and the acceleration of urbanization, vehicles and traffic flows are soaring, increasing traffic emergencies such as bad weather, traffic accidents, or road failures. They can lead to disruptions in the operation of the trans- portation system, placing great pressure on the reliable travel and robust operation of urban roads (Stefaniec et al. 2020), directly threat- ening the resilience of urban road systems ( Osman 2021). The resil- ience of urban road systems is generally considered to be the ability to absorb, adapt, and quickly recover after being disturbed (Bešinovi´c 2020). When a traffic accident occurs, resilience is mainly ensured through technical and organizational means, that is, urban road emer- gency management (Y u et al. 2024). Effective urban road emergency management for enhanced resilience necessitates the integration of the unique characteristics of the urban road during traffic accidents, real-time situational awareness, and foresight of potential accidents. (Ford and Wolf 2020 ). To satisfy these requirements, scholars have been applying digital twin (DT) technology to improve the resilience of urban transportation systems ( Miglani and Kumar 2019 ). Braik a n dK o l i o u(2023) proposed a DT framework for emergency manage- ment of transportation infrastructure during natural disasters that fo- cuses on data acquisition, analysis, and reasoning, integrating physics-based and data-driven models to simulate and predict facility performance and assisting in the adjustment of emergency strategies and planning. Feng et al. (2023) analyzed the data sharing problem in the Internet of vehicles transportation system from a DT perspective, developed travel behavior models under uncertain events, and estab- lished a blockchain-enabled DT transportation system to improve traffic network resilience. However, the urban road system is com- plex, consisting of people, vehicles, roads, and environments. The interactions between various elements during traffic accidents are un- certain and dynamic, which challenge the ability to effectively model, understand, and predict system performance ( Alibrandi 2022). Cognitive digital twin (CDT) empowers DT with enhanced cog- nitive capabilities and realizes DT integration with the help of seman- tic technologies such as ontology and knowledge graph (Zheng et al. 2022), which facilitates the integration of life-cycle data, informa- tion, and knowledge of urban road emergency scenarios. When urban road emergencies occur, twin modeling of emer- gency scenarios requires recognizing the complex dynamic coupling relationships between multiple scenario elements and focusing on the chain process of scenario evolution and its mutation characteristics. In response to addressing the issue of completeness in twin modeling, this paper proposes the multilevel and multidimension definition for emergency scenario twin models, informed by resilience theory and accounting for the multifaceted, uncertain, and dynamic nature of urban road emergency scenarios. Subsequently, in view of the char- acteristics of twin models at different levels and dimensions, a sys- tematic method of model construction and integration is proposed, aimed at unraveling the underlying mechanisms governing the inter- actions among elements. Finally, with the twin model as the core, a CDT framework is proposed for urban road emergency management. The remaining part of this paper is organized as follows. First, the research related to DT-based emergency management is reviewed in terms of both models and modeling approaches, and the research gap is identified. The next section introduces and meticulously discusses the CDT model construction and integration method for urban road emergency scenarios, together with an ex- position of the CDT framework tailored for this setting. Then, an emergency scenario twin modeling case under a collision accident is introduced to verify the proposed method. Finally, the results and future directions of the study are discussed and summarized. Related Work DT provides a new way to synchronize the physical and virtual worlds ( Grieves 2014 ). In the field of emergency management, DT leverages high-fidelity models and dynamic simulations up- dated in real time to help decision-makers generate insights into current scenario during and after the emergency occurrence, thus enabling enhanced emergency management and significantly in- creased resilience ( Braik and Koliou 2024b ). However, existing re- search shows that ensuring consistency and interoperability of models is both important and challenging for DT-based emergency management ( Deng et al. 2021 ). To address this challenge, recent studies have combined semantic modeling and system engineering with DTs to improve interoperability and connectivity among twin models ( Rožanec et al. 2022 ). On this basis, the concept of CDT has been introduced ( Adl 2016 ), and recent works have explored the potential of CDT in smart manufacturing systems. Nevertheless, in the field of emergency management, there remains a need for further investigation into the CDT model and associated modeling methodologies, which are the core of the CDT application. Twin Models for Scenario-Based Emergency Management DT can be defined as a multiphysics, multiscale model where digital models are simulated, analyzed, and updated based on data obtained from the physical world (Longman et al. 2023). Therefore, the foun- dation of DT-based emergency management is the twin modeling of emergency scenarios in the physical world. Classical disaster theory identifies hazard elements, affected elements, and environment ele- ments as the constituent elements of emergency scenarios in the physical world ( Blaikie et al. 2014 ). The hazard element and af- fected element, respectively, denote the elements causing the emer- gency and those affected by it, while the environment element serves as the backdrop conditions that affect them. Moreover, response plans outline action schemes aimed at mitigating the impact of an emergency (Ni et al. 2023 ), their implementation driving system recovery from the emergency ’s effects, and these plans themselves becoming pivotal elements driving the evolution of scenarios (Wang et al. 2020 ). Thus, the hazard element, affected element, environ- ment element, and response plan element constitute the key physical objects in constructing an emergency scenario twin model. The in- terrelationships among these various scenario elements drive the evolution of emergency scenarios. Therefore, the construction of a twin model based on the interaction mechanism between the el- ements of the emergency scenario can provide a panoramic descrip- tion of the emergency scenario ( Kang et al. 2023 ). The core of DT is a high-fidelity model that contains a multidi- mensional fusion of geometry models, attribute models, behavior models, and rule models, so it can truly depict and simulate the state of physical entities (Tao et al. 2019). In the field of emergency man- agement, Li et al. (2018) analyzed the dynamic interactions between flood inundation and traffic flow and conducted real-time simulation modeling of flood scenarios from the geometric and behavior dimen- sions to predict traffic disruptions. Lv et al. ( 2022) used traffic ac- cident intelligent algorithms to analyze the attribute characteristics of involved elements, establishing a traffic accident prevention and prediction system driven by DT and artificial intelligence. To en- hance the understanding of the full-stage and overall posture of emer- gency scenarios ( Arvin et al. 2023 ), resilience-based scenario analysis and modeling methods have gradually gained attention. Resilience embodies a system’s and its subsystems’ capacities to ab- sorb, adapt to, and recover from adverse or abnormal states, serving as an effective means of integrating information about scenario el- ements and fostering a holistic comprehension of emergency scenar- ios (Field et al. 2022 ). Jiao et al. ( 2021) established a storm disaster scenario model by identifying the key factors influencing the resil- ience of urban rail transit stations and their action paths. Y u et al. (2023) applied the pressure-state-response theory to analyze the in- teraction mechanisms between multidimensional scenario elements and the characteristics of the resilience stage and established an emergency scenario model from a resilience perspective. Resilience provides a new perspective for in-depth exploration of the coupling relationship and dynamic evolution pattern among scenario ele- ments. Therefore, this paper considers that it is promising to integrate multidimensional scenario element information within the resilience framework to construct multilevel twin models of emergency sce- nario cognitive digital twin (ESCDT). Twin Modeling Approach for Emergency Scenario Appropriate modeling methods facilitate the practice of twin mod- els. Multiple studies have been carried out to explore the construc- tion of emergency scenario twin models. For the construction of geometry models, White et al. ( 2021) integrated building informa- tion modeling (BIM), big data, and IoT sensor data to create a DT-supported smart city, in which BIM was used to present the collected information in 3D format. For the construction of attribute models, Liu et al. ( 2020) collected indoor real-time operating in- formation to extract the key attributes of the building and used ma- chine learning models to automate the classification and severity assessment of emergency scenarios. For the construction of behav- ior models, Fang et al. ( 2023) introduced a structural model to simulate the safety performance of workers ’ behavior under differ- ent intervention strategies by considering the behavioral decision- making process under emergency scenarios and the interaction between workers and the environment. Existing research predominantly proposed modeling methods for a single dimension in a certain application field. However, an emergency scenario contains multiple elements, spans multiple phases, and is a complex object with uncertainty, which requires a systematic modeling approach to construct and integrate models in different dimensions. Proposed Framework for ESCDT Based on the concept explanation and construction method for ESCDT, the overview of the proposed framework is illustrated in Fig. 11. It primarily consists of the physical layer, data layer, twin layer, knowledge layer, and service layer. 1. The physical layer aims to process information about relevant entities in the physical scenarios through data collection, map- ping, and transmission. In urban road emergencies, relevant entities include the vehicle hazard element, vehicle affected element, road affected element, people affected element, road environment element, meteorology environment element, re- sponse action element, and resource element. Data acquisition equipment includes roadside equipment and external systems. Roadside equipment mainly collects various traffic parameters, vehicle types, weather, etc. External systems collect data from external channels, such as traffic and travel speed provided by the toll collection system, real-time speed and congestion con- ditions reported by the navigation map platform, operation plans recorded by the maintenance management system, and real- time location data provided by the rescue vehicle management system. Then, standard data interfaces are required to map these initial data into data catering to DT modeling. Moreover, real- time data transmission is made possible by edge computing and 5G technology ( Liu et al. 2021 ). Thus, dynamic monitoring information about physical entities can be provided to the virtual space. 2. The data layer is used to collect and store twin data. The twin data can be divided into physical data, virtual data, and knowl- edge data ( Y u et al. 2021 ). The physical data originate from the physical layer, which are collected by access devices. These data reflect state changes of relevant entities, furnishing a fundamen- tal basis for emergency decision-making. The virtual data are generated through the construction and operation of twin mod- els. After a traffic accident, decision-makers use CDT models to simulate and evaluate the effects of various emergency plans, thereby gen- erating new insights and refining current emergency plans to enhance the efficacy of future responses. Case Study Shanghai Urban Express Road Network is the backbone of trans- portation in Shanghai, China. The North-South Elevated Road in Shanghai Urban Express Road Network starts at the intersection of Longyao Road Tunnel and Jiyang Road and ends at the S20 Outer Ring Expressway (shown in red in Fig. 12). The total length of the road is about 22.2 km (Guan 2022), and the total flow rate of the main line is about 4,277 veh=h during the morning peak (Xu et al. 2024). It is one of the main elevated roads in Shanghai, bearing a substantial portion of the traffic and experiencing frequent traffic accidents. At about 12:25 a.m. on January 17, 2022, a box truck and a car col- lided on the Huma section of Gongjiang Road on the North-South Elevated Road. The accident resulted in one person being injured, garbage and oil stains appearing on the road, and damage to one anti- noise screen. After rescue, firefighting, towing, and rescue emer- gency units carried out rescue operations, the lane returned to normal. Introductions Space steel mesh frame structure is one of the earliest and most widely used structural forms of various space structures, which is commonly used in the roofing system of some large-scale infrastructures and public buildings, and is often seen in some airports [1,2] , high-speed rail stations [3] , gymnasiums [4] , and exhibition centers and other architectural facilities. The mesh frame structure itself has excellent structural properties such as good integrity, high spatial stiffness, strong stability, light weight and beautiful shape [5] . However, since the full stress design method is generally used for calculation and analysis in the design stage of the net frame structure, this also leads to the fact that the net frame structure is very sensitive to the initial defects of the rods and the adjustment of the use of the load and other factors, and in the process of construction or operation and maintenance of the construction load [6] , the use of the function of the changes in the use of the renovation of the event, resulting in the load borne is different from the design stage, so that the key rods and the key node The load carrying capacity is exceeded, leading to local instability or overall collapse of the structure, which ultimately causes major casualties and economic losses, there - fore, the control of structural safety is particularly important. Currently, general construction projects collect the safety data of the structure through contact sensors, total stations [7] , etc., and compare the data with the limit values corresponding to the code, so as to derive the current safety status of the structure, i.e., the structural health detection (SHM) in the traditional sense. However, this method has certain drawbacks: (1) structural health detection requires the installa - tion and maintenance of a large number of sensors, but due to the eco - nomic impact, they are often installed at critical locations only based on engineering experience, and both periodic inspection and data analysis require a large amount of manpower and material input, making the cost of the traditional SHM method relatively high [8,9] ; (2) the contact sensors usually need to be manually mounted directly on the structure. Some of the sensors have inconvenient installation problems [10] , which may lead to inaccurate data collection due to irregular installa - tion factors, and they may also be dropped outdoors due to wind, rain, snow, and human influence; (3) The traditional SHM method mainly focuses on the current state of the structure [11] , which makes it difficult to accurately predict the future performance of the structure and the possible problems that may occur. Intelligent safety control method for large-span steel structures driven by digital twins Digital twin technology refers to the simulation, analysis and opti - misation of the whole life cycle of physical entities through digital means in order to achieve two-way mapping and interaction between physical entities and digital models. In the field of large-span steel structures, digital twin technology can realise real-time monitoring, data analysis, fault diagnosis and predictive maintenance of large-span steel structures, and provide powerful support for the safe and intelligent control of large-span steel structures [32] . In this paper, the DT framework for large-span steel structures is built, as shown in Fig. 1 , which takes the space steel mesh frame structure as the physical entity for DT mapping, corresponding to the construction data of the project on the left side of the figure, including static data and dynamic data. Static data refers to the geometrical in - formation of the relevant components of the structure, such as rod length, inner and outer diameters and monitoring equipment models, which are basically unchanged and inherent; dynamic data refers to the data that will change as the construction advances, such as ambient wind speed, temperature, and the data of rod stresses and displacements. It also contains a document database with architectural drawings, specifications and geographic information data, such as the top and bottom chord layout of the grid, structural construction staircase sample drawings and the underground pipe network of the terminal building. The right part is the digital twin model, which is a virtual object mapped by the physical space, containing geometric and finite element models (geometric, physical, behavioural, and rule-based), predictive control algorithms, and the physical building, which integrates all kinds of static and dynamic data. 2.1. Geometric models To realise the perfect mapping of virtual space to physical space, it is necessary to construct a geometric model of the long-span steel structure first. The model should be able to comprehensively reflect the geometric characteristics and material properties of the long-span steel structure. It mainly includes the construction form, span, height, material, connection form of nodes, etc. The material characteristics mainly include material type, yield strength, elastic modulus, etc., and it can achieve real-time synchronisation with the physical entity. In the con - struction process, the technical means of 3D scanning and laser mea - surement are used to obtain the accurate geometric information of the large-span steel structure. Physical models A physical model is a digital representation of a real-world physical entity or system that simulates the structure, properties, behaviour of physical entities and their interactions with the environment. The physical model is one of the core components of digital twin technology, which provides the basis for real-time synchronisation and interaction between the virtual space and the physical space. In this paper, numerical simulation modelling has been carried out using finite element software, in which all components of the steel roof cover are meshed using beam cells. Control system The overall jacking method in this paper adopts multiple hydraulic jacking frames to provide jacking force in groups, and according to the stress collected by the sensors and the height linkage of the jacking frames, the steel mesh frames are assembled and shaped in-situ, and then jacked up to the unloading height by the jacking system, so as to realise the overall jacking of the large-span steel mesh frames structure. The overall synchronous jacking system consists of jacking system (jacking frame group), power system (hydraulic pump station), moni - toring system (sensors) and control system (computer) composed of four parts, the overall jacking synchronous control flowchart as shown in Fig. 8 . The jacking frame group is the jacking component of the system, which can be configured in different quantities and arranged in the corresponding positions according to the load of the steel mesh roof required by the project, and the counterforce at each jacking point can be accurately obtained through the finite element analysis of the digital twin physical model, which is used in the hydraulic jacking jacks in this project. The hydraulic pump station provides power for the whole jacking system, and the jacking and descending of the jacking frame through the extension cylinder action or contraction cylinder action is divided into total control and sub-control, which carries out the overall synchronous jacking control and the sub-control of each jacking frame locally, which can effectively improve the synchronous adjustment performance of the whole jacking system. The monitoring system obtains mechanical information (stress, displacement, etc.) by deploying sensors around each jacking frame, obtains displacement information through the computer control system, and provides real-time feedback of mechanical and displacement data to the digital twin model. The digital twin model regulates the next action according to the current structural air attitude information. The main control computer sets the stress limit value according to the result of finite element construction process analysis. When the stress near the jacking point is too large or the height difference between individual jacking frame and other jacking frames is too large, it will automatically alarm and send out the shutdown command, and adjust the height of individual jacking frames so that the whole structure is at the same height again. 5. Introduction The concept of digital twin (DT) was firstly proposed by Professor Grieves in early 2003 [1]. Digital twin refers to the application of establishing a virtual mapping (twin model) of a physical entity in cyberspace based on sensor data updates and historical information, enabling simulation, guidance, control, optimization, prediction, and full lifecycle management of the physical entity through the virtual model. The rise of digital twin has provided more possibilities for visualization, information-based management, intelligent construction, and maintenance of bridge engineering. However, it is undeniable that, since the first application of the digital twin model in the aerospace field [2], current research and applications in bridge engineering remain in the early stages. Existing studies mostly focus on abstract concepts and applications during the operation and maintenance phases [3–5], while the use of more realistic and precise digital twin models in the bridge construction phase awaits further exploration. Buildings 2025, 15, 1492 https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings15091492 Buildings 2025, 15, 1492 2 of 17 Current bridge construction monitoring systems often rely on fragmented data and text-based documentation, which struggle to address challenges such as real-time coordina- tion between physical operations and digital controls, multi-disciplinary collaboration (e.g., structural safety, traffic management), and predictive risk mitigation during critical phases like rotation construction. Cable-stayed bridges built using the rotation method face unique risks, including unbalanced structural loads or instability during rotation. These require precise real-time monitoring and rapid decision-making. However, traditional approaches lack an integrated framework to synchronize sensor data, simulation models, actual bridge components, and operational workflows. This gap highlights the need for a digital twin system that unifies physical and virtual components. Therefore, it is necessary to establish a digital twin system for bridge construction monitoring. Unlike other multi-layered, complex engineering fields, bridge construction is less complex. However, the architecture of a bridge construction monitoring digital twin system is substantial, requiring the interaction of a physical system, a digital system, and other components to achieve functionality. Once the framework for the digital twin system is defined, due to its multi-disciplinary and cross-domain integration, a unified system design methodology is necessary. In recent years, model-based systems engineering, a new systems engineering methodology, has carried out a lot of theoretical research and practice at home and abroad. The concept of MBSE (model-based systems engineering) was formally proposed by the International Society of Systems Engineering (INCOSE) in 2007 [6]. MBSE is a model-driven system development process, which uses models to express the requirements, design, analysis, verification, and validation processes in the whole life cycle of the system. MBSE uses the method of replacing documents with models to link all stages of the system design life cycle, which is model-centric, with traceability between the models of each design phase. When requirements change, the models can be automatically updated through the traceability relationships. Moreover, the graphical, standardized, and digital representation of the model is more intuitive and concise, which facilitates the communication between system designers and users [ 7]. MBSE is widely applied in industries such as aerospace, aviation [8], nuclear power [9], and defense [10], but there is little research linking MBSE to bridge engineering. The characteristics of MBSE are particularly suitable for the design of a digital twin system, and there are already many studies in other fields (nuclear power, airport construction, robotics, shop-floor construction) linking MBSE with digital twin [ 11–15]. Managing the design of a digital twin system based on the MBSE method will undoubtedly accelerate the realization of such a system. MBSE’s emphasis on visualization and traceability is critical for designing a digital twin system in bridge engineering. A bridge digital twin system requires synchronized integration of its physical components (e.g., structural sensors), digital models (e.g., sim- ulations), and external systems (e.g., traffic networks). MBSE’s structured framework addresses these multi-disciplinary challenges proactively, reducing ambiguities and en- abling early risk detection—a necessity for rotation construction monitoring. This paper proposes a method for designing a digital twin system for rotation construction monitoring based on MBSE, and establishes a V-model architecture for the design of the digital twin system for rotation construction monitoring based on MBSE. A 90m+90m cross-railway single-tower cable-stayed bridge built by the rotation construction method is taken as an example, and the system requirements, functions, and architecture are modeled based on the RFLP process. The whole scheme is related step by step, and finally the digital twin system is verified. Buildings 2025, 15, 1492 3 of 17 2. Overview of MBSE Overview of MBSE and Three Elements of Modeling The MBSE method has three core components: modeling language, modeling method, and modeling tools [16,17]. MBSE employs standardized languages like UML (Unified Modeling Language) and SysML (Systems Modeling Language) to model complex systems. While UML focuses on software engineering, SysML extends UML 2.0 to support system- level architecture, making it ideal for holistic projects like a bridge digital twin system [18]. This paper employs SysML modeling language for system modeling. The “communication” method between SysML and stakeholders’ visualization is accomplished through nine types of diagrams, which can be summarized as “three categories, nine types.” The “three categories” are structural diagrams, behavioral diagrams, and requirement diagrams, each used to model the system’s structure, behavior, and requirements, respectively. Structural diagrams mainly include package diagrams, internal block diagrams, block definition diagrams, and parametric diagrams. Behavioral diagrams mainly include activity diagrams, use case diagrams, sequence diagrams, and state machine diagrams [19]. Abstract The rapid deterioration of building facades presents substantial safety hazards in urban environments, necessitating advanced, automated inspection solutions. While computer vision (CV) and deep learning (DL) techniques have shown promise for defect analysis, crit- ical gaps remain in achieving real-time, quantitative, and generalizable damage assessment suitable for robotic deployment. Current methods often lack precise metric quantification, struggle with diverse material appearances, and are computationally intensive for on-site processing. To address these limitations, this paper introduces a fully automated, end- to-end inspection framework integrating a wall-climbing robot, a real-time vision-based analysis system, and a digital twin management platform. The primary contributions are threefold: (1) a novel, fully integrated robotic framework for autonomous navigation, multi- sensor data collection, and real-time analysis; (2) a lightweight, synthetic data-augmented DL model for real-time defect segmentation and metric quantification, achieving a mean Av- erage Precision (mAP) of 0.775 for segmentation, an average defect length error of1.140 cm, and an average center position error of 0.826 cm; (3) a cloud-based digital twin platform enabling quantitative defect visualization, spatiotemporal traceability, and data-driven project management, with the on-site inspection cycle demonstrating a responsive latency of 2.8–4.8 s. Validated through laboratory tests and real building projects, the framework demonstrates significant improvements in inspection efficiency, quantitative accuracy, and decision support over conventional methods. Introduction Regular and effective inspection of building facades is a significant procedure in build- ing health monitoring. Traditional wall inspection techniques rely mostly on working at heights. Construction workers are suspended from the roof and wall elements are inspected using human eyes or handheld equipment. In recent years, the Architectural, Engineering, Construction, and Facility Management (AEC/FM) industry has seen a growing trend of automation with robotics. Various robot vehicles have been investigated for monitoring and inspection tasks in as-built facilities, such as Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) [1–3], wheeled robots [4,5], quadruped robots [6,7], etc. They are usually equipped with cameras or laser scanners to collect images [8,9] or point cloud data [10,11] for post-processing. However, they are not appli- cable under weak Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) signals (e.g., close high-rise buildings) or in no-fly zones (e.g., around airports or military zones). In addition, UAVs are not suitable for carrying contact-based sensors as they need to maintain a safe distance from walls. Suspended platforms are alternative solutions that can carry human workers or automation equipment such as robot arms [12]. However, suspended platforms require pre-installation of heavy block-and-tackle systems, and they are not flexible in horizontal movement. As a result, a wall-climbing robot, which directly moves on vertical surfaces in any desired orientation and enables inspection sensors with multiple modalities, is proposed as the robot platform for automated wall inspection. The purpose of wall inspection is to recognize, measure, and locate defects of exterior walls, including cracks, spalling, etc. Traditional inspection techniques rely on human workers for decision making, which is subjective and inefficient. In recent years, the de- velopment of deep learning models such as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) [13] has made it possible to automatically detect and segment wall defects from images. In addition, computer vision algorithms such as Structure-from-Motion (SfM) can accurately determine the camera’s motion and locate defect features. However, current defect detec- tion techniques suffer from limitations in both spatial and temporal accuracy. Spatially, conventional techniques fail to provide precise quantitative data, such as the exact size, location, and shape description of defects. They also lack the generalization ability to diverse surface materials. Temporally, the inspection results of a building are not updated regularly to reflect subsequent maintenance or renovations in a timely manner. To address these shortcomings, a customized defect segmentation technique and an intelligent project management system are required. The proposed solution can accurately analyze the data collected by a wall-climbing robot in real time and verify an up-to-date digital twin model of the building. To address these challenges, this study introduces an automated inspection frame- work for building exterior walls. As illustrated in Figure 1, the proposed framework comprises three key components: (1) a wall-climbing robot employing negative pressure adhesion technology, equipped with visual and penetrating sensors; (2) a ground station that facilitates multi-sensor data processing through deep learning-based defect detec- tion algorithms and quantitative analysis; and (3) a cloud platform that leverages digital twin representations to enable quantitative defect condition assessment and inspection project management. Literature Review This section provides a literature review on different inspection vehicles, detection algorithms, and digital twin techniques, as presented in Sections 2.1–2.3, respectively. 2.1. Wall Inspection Vehicles There have been growing attempts in the AEC/FM industry to conduct exterior wall inspection using automated vehicles. UAV is a popular choice in inspection of high-rise buildings, bridges, and various infrastructure. For example, Bolourian et al. [14] proposed a UAV path planning framework for aerial laser scanning and bridge inspection. Tan et al. [15] proposed a UAV-based framework to collect images of building surfaces. Their work was further extended to achieve mapping and modeling of defect data [16]. However, the presence of no-fly zones and payload restrictions are two major disadvantages of UAVs. Therefore, some researchers focused on wall-climbing robots as an alternative solution, due to their flexibility, durability, and payload capacity. Wall-climbing robots are typically categorized into four types based on their adhesion mechanisms [17]: magnetic, negative pressure, electrostatic, and bio-inspired adhesion. Negative pressure adhesion is the preferred method for wall inspection robots, as it offers high payload capacity and is effective on a wide range of common building surface materi- als [18]. The core principle of negative pressure adhesion involves generating a pressure difference between the sealed chambers under the robot and the external environment. This creates a suction force that enables the robot to remain firmly attached while moving. Recent research has demonstrated the effectiveness of wall-climbing robots in building inspection applications. For example, Yang et al. [19] proposed a wall-climbing robot for concrete inspection and utilized an RGB-D camera for 3D point cloud reconstruction. Hu et al. [20] proposed a coverage-oriented path planning technique for wall-climbing robots to improve the efficiency of inspection tasks. However, these methods did not realize quantitative analysis of defect types, shapes, and locations. This study aims to develop an integrated robotic platform capable of precise localization, autonomous navigation on vertical surfaces, and automated wall defect inspection. 2.2. Defect Detection Algorithms Developments in deep learning and computer vision have pioneered automated defect detection using image data. CNN-based models were first investigated for classification, bounding box detection, and pixel-level segmentation tasks. For example, He et al. pro- posed Mask R-CNN [21], which improved over bounding box detection models [ 22] by adding a segmentation head and became a benchmark for instance segmentation tasks. Redmon et al. proposed YOLO (You Only Look Once) [23], which innovatively combined https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16030646 Buildings 2026, 16, 646 4 of 23 the region proposal and classification steps into one network structure. It significantly improved inference speed and became the dominant model for real-time object detection. These CNN-based models have been verified in crack detection tasks for buildings [24] and infrastructure [25]. Since the introduction of Transformer [26], models based on a self-attention structure have become popular in deep learning. Transformer-based models such as Vision Trans- former (ViT) [27], RT-DETR [28], and Grounding-DINO [29] were soon introduced into visual tasks and achieved impressive accuracy and generalization ability. Chu et al. [30] proposed a Transformer-based model to improve the accuracy of crack segmentation for bridges. Zim et al. [31] proposed to combine CNN and Transformer into one hybrid model and applied it for crack segmentation tasks. Although these studies demonstrated im- proved performance, Transformer architecture usually requires extensive training datasets and substantially greater computational resources for both training and inference com- pared to convolutional models. This is not favored for on-site applications, where the best available computational device could be a laptop. Therefore, this study proposes to train lightweight models such as YOLO on domain-specific datasets, aiming to achieve sufficient accuracy and real-time processing on low computational resources. 2.3. Digital Twin Applications Digital twin technology transforms traditional facility management by creating a dy- namic, virtual replica of a physical asset. It shifts management from a reactive, experience- based approach to a proactive, data-driven system. Foundational construction represen- tations like Building Information Modeling (BIM) and Geographic Information Systems (GIS) [32] often serve as the geometric and semantic backbone for these digital twins. The application of this technology spans several domains. In robotics, for instance, Chen et al. [33,34] incorporated physics engines to simulate and optimize coverage path planning for wheeled inspection robots, improving both accuracy and efficiency. Wang et al. [35,36] proposed a hardware-in-the-loop simulation environment for mobile laser scanning using Unreal Engine. Another significant area of development is human–machine interaction. Liu et al. [37] combined UAV-captured images with augmented reality to conduct building inspection. Alizadehsalehi et al. They are detailed in Sections 3.1–3.3, respectively. 3.1. System Architecture The wall-climbing robot in this study utilizes negative pressure adhesion for surface attachment. Its locomotion system consists of two impellers that generate vacuum pressure and four wheels enabling lateral movement (Figure 2). The platform houses an Ultra- wideband (UWB) radar on one side and features a mounting base supporting an extended https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16030646 Buildings 2026, 16, 646 5 of 23 rod with a camera sensor. The robot is directly powered with cables to ensure long durability. The sub-modules of the robot system are described as follows: (1) Sensing subsystem The robotic platform is equipped with a multi-modal sensor set combining contact and non-contact technologies for comprehensive building inspection. An RGB and infrared (IR) camera simultaneously captures both visible light and infrared radiation, enabling concurrent detection of surface defects such as cracks and spalling through visual analysis, and subsurface defects such as hollowing through thermal variations. The UWB radar com- plements this by penetrating building materials to reveal hidden voids and delamination within deeper construction layers. This strategic sensor fusion provides cross-validation ca- pabilities across different physical modalities, significantly enhancing inspection reliability and defect characterization accuracy. The current study focuses specifically on RGB image data acquisition and processing pipeline development. (2) Localization subsystem Accurate localization is a fundamental prerequisite for path planning and scene recon- struction, as a robot must precisely determine its position to execute navigation commands effectively. However, typical GNSS solutions offer only meter-level accuracy, which is insufficient for detailed inspection tasks on building exteriors. Therefore, this study em- ploys Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) technology to enhance GNSS positioning precision. Size Quantification and Localization The performance of defect size quantification was evaluated through controlled lab- oratory experiments. This evaluation aimed to validate the accuracy and reliability of converting pixel-based segmentation results into precise, real-world dimensional measure- ments. A set of wall specimens with known, pre-measured crack and spalling defects was imaged under controlled lighting conditions. To establish a clear and consistent benchmark, the evaluation focused on the center position and the maximum horizontal and vertical spans of each defect. Conclusions This paper presented an end-to-end framework for automated facade inspection, in- cluding a wall-climbing robot, deep-learning defect analysis, and a digital twin platform. The wall-climbing robot, utilizing negative pressure adhesion, demonstrated reliable navi- gation on vertical surfaces while carrying a suite of sensors, including an RGB camera and penetrating sensors. A customized lightweight model was trained for real-time, instance segmentation of cracking and spalling defects at a precision of 0.775 mAP , followed by metric quantification with an average length error at 1.140 cm and center position error at 0.826 cm. The RTK-based positioning module enabled precise robot localization, facilitating accurate mapping within a building coordinate system. Furthermore, a cloud-based digital twin platform was developed to visualize inspection results, manage facility data, and support proactive maintenance through quantitative defect assessment and spatial localiza- tion. The proposed framework has been validated through multiple building inspection projects. It significantly improves upon traditional workflows by enhancing operational safety, inspection efficiency, data traceability, and decision-making support. Though promising results have been demonstrated, this study still has some limita- tions. First, the robot’s adhesion mechanism may not be applicable to highly rough surfaces, limiting its applicability. Second, the current study focused on surface-level defects, without a detailed investigation of subsurface conditions such as delamination or internal voids. Therefore, future works will focus on two directions: (1) enhancing the robot’s hard- ware for improved adaptability to complex wall geometries and surface textures; (2) an- alyzing data from UWB radar and infrared sensors to enable non-destructive subsurface characterization. These multi-modal data streams will also be used for cross-validation to improve the robustness and accuracy of defect assessment across both visible and hidden structural flaws. Digital twin (DT) technology oﬀers a promising approach to over- coming these multi-dimensional barriers, provided it can eﬀectively bridge the gap between technological capability and practical adoption. DT applications in construction include optimised resource manage- ment through real-time monitoring [11]; enhanced stakeholder collab- oration via shared digital platforms [12]; accelerated project delivery through virtual simulations and predictive analytics [13]; comprehen- sive progress monitoring using 4D BIM integration [14]; improved qual- ity control and safety management [15,16]; and enhanced sustainability monitoring of carbon emissions and supply chains [17,18]. This creates a critical research gap: while DT technology holds promise for addressing renovation barriers, its application for multi-domain perfor- mance monitoring during onsite renovation, including both technical implementation and user acceptance, remains underexplored. Literature review 2.1. DT Applications in building retroﬁt Building retroﬁt currently relies on static tools with broad as- sumptions and generic inputs, often lacking digital modelling capabil- ities or methods for identifying optimal retroﬁt measures for speciﬁc projects [21,22]. DT technology oﬀers potential to address these limita- tions through dynamically updated digital replicas for retroﬁt planning and decision-making. However, existing research reveals critical lim- itations in current DT implementations for renovation contexts. Most studies fail to address the multi-stakeholder, multi-domain complexity inherent in renovation projects, where coordination between occupants, contractors, and project managers becomes essential for success. 2.1.1. Project phases and DT coverage Renovation projects involve distinct phases requiring diﬀerent mon- itoring approaches and stakeholder coordination mechanisms. Design and planning phases (D&P) focus on scenario evaluation and retroﬁt strategy development. The onsite retroﬁtting phase (OR) involves ac- tive construction work where real-time monitoring becomes critical for managing quality, safety, cost, and schedule performance. Operation and maintenance phases (O&M) address post-renovation building per- formance optimisation. Current DT research shows uneven distribution across these phases. Most studies address design/planning activities or post-renovation operations, whilst the onsite retroﬁtting phase, where coordination challenges are most acute, receives limited attention. This gap is particularly signiﬁcant given that renovation projects typically experience greater time and cost overruns, increased safety risks, and quality issues compared to new-build construction [23]. 2.1.2. Use cases and applications Building on Pan et al.’s [24] theoretical framework for DT in renova- tion, current applications have emerged across four primary domains. Indoor condition monitoring maintains occupant health and safety during renovation through environmental parameter tracking. Arsiwala et al. [25] developed a DT solution integrating IoT, BIM, and AI to au- tomate CO2 emission monitoring, demonstrating spatial data visuali- sation for air quality management with machine learning-based emis- sion prediction. Studies by Mainini et al. [26], Daniotti et al. [27], and Banﬁ et al. [28] developed systems for monitoring temperature, humid- ity, and CO2 emissions. Tan et al. [29] proposed a DT lighting system for dynamic occupant comfort management. Energy modelling and monitoring represents the most developed application area, reﬂecting retroﬁtting’s energy eﬃciency goals. Jradi et al. [22] proposed Dan- RETwin for decision-making and energy optimisation in existing build- ings through IoT sensors and AI-driven performance analysis. Bocullo et al. [30] developed a city block renovation approach using renew- able energy sources with scenario evaluation capabilities. Trombadore et al. [31] and Kaewunruen et al. [31] devel- oped a methodology for heritage building renovation, providing tools to evaluate cost-eﬀectiveness whilst monitoring air quality, building per- formance, and user behaviour. Safety monitoring remains the least de- veloped domain. Khajavi et al. [33] developed a DT architecture em- phasising real-time ﬁre detection, incorporating data fusion, localisa- tion, and propagation simulation. Their approach demonstrated safety beneﬁts during renovation when building systems are oﬄine, though it addressed only ﬁre hazards. 2.1.3. Technical components DT implementations integrate four core technological components: data sensing devices for reality capture and performance tracking; BIM and 3D visualisation for digital representation; visualisation dashboards for stakeholder communication; and AI for automated data processing. Most studies rely on IoT sensors for data exchange between physical and digital systems, collecting parameters including temperature, hu- midity, occupancy, luminance, CO2, and energy consumption [22,27]. However, BIM capabilities remain underutilised, with most studies fo- cusing solely on 3D visualisation rather than leveraging design coordi- nation, clash detection, or 4D simulation functions [34]. Visualisation dashboards are underdeveloped, with limited implementations provid- ing comprehensive monitoring across multiple performance domains. AI adoption remains restricted to speciﬁc applications such as emission prediction and computer vision for hazard detection [25,33]. 2.2. Limitations and future work Several considerations should be acknowledged when interpreting these ﬁndings and planning future implementations. The RDT platform was tested on a single renovation project (Project Sarrazins, Lille), lim- iting generalisability to other retroﬁt contexts involving diﬀerent build- ing types, scales, and renovation scopes. The proof-of-concept demon- stration focused on functional veriﬁcation rather than comprehensive Energy & Buildings 354 (2026) 116979 10 O. Doukari et al. empirical validation. Whilst the TAM-based evaluation provided in- sights into user acceptance, the study lacks quantiﬁed performance im- provements (cost savings, schedule adherence, safety incident reduc- tion) that could validate the platform’s core beneﬁts. The short eval- uation period may not capture long-term usability issues or eﬀective- ness throughout complete renovation lifecycles. The TAM sample ex- hibited bias toward early-career professionals (72.5% with less than 5 years experience), potentially limiting applicability to experienced prac- titioners with diﬀerent adoption patterns. Technical limitations include absence of AI capabilities, with the simulation service supporting only historical KPI visualisation without predictive analytics for proactive is- sue identiﬁcation. Reliance on manual data entry through mobile appli- cations may introduce inconsistencies compared to automated sensor- based collection systems. The evaluation relied on self-reported percep- tions rather than observed usage behaviours, which may not accurately reﬂect actual adoption patterns in real-world scenarios [56]. The sus- tainability outcomes from the French demonstration site (Section 4.3, Table 5) represent a single case study with speciﬁc economic and regu- latory characteristics that may limit generalisability of the cost-beneﬁt ﬁndings. Future research should address these considerations through multi- site validation across diverse renovation projects (residential versus commercial, varying scales and types) in diﬀerent climate zones and regulatory environments to establish broader applicability. Longitudi- nal performance studies tracking actual project metrics before, dur- ing, and after RDT implementation would provide empirical evidence of platform eﬀectiveness. Research involving experienced profession- als and comparative analysis between RDT-supported projects and tra- ditional approaches would quantify relative beneﬁts and identify opti- mal implementation strategies. Technical enhancements should priori- tise automated data integration through IoT sensors alongside predictive analytics using deep learning for proactive project management sup- port [57]. Integration of predictive monitoring methods into the RDT platform to assess the impact of renovation works on indoor conditions could inform occupants and stakeholders before retroﬁtting processes begin [58]. Likewise, incorporation of material circularity metrics into the RDT platform to monitor reuse rates, recycled content percentages, and end-of-life recoverability would align renovation tracking with EU Circular Economy Action Plan targets [59]. The current RDT framework provides foundation for developing cognitive digital twin systems capa- ble of learning from project patterns, providing intelligent recommen- dations, and supporting autonomous decision-making processes [60]. 6. Conclusions This paper presents the development, deployment, and evaluation of an RDT platform designed to support multi-domain performance monitoring in building retroﬁt projects. The research addresses iden- tiﬁed gaps in DT applications for renovation contexts, where exist- ing frameworks predominantly focus on operational phase monitoring rather than onsite construction performance across quality, cost, sched- ule, safety, and environmental domains. The RDT was developed using DSR methodology, incorporating stakeholder input from RINNO consor- tium partners to deﬁne 13 KPIs across ﬁve performance domains. Tech- nical validation on the French demonstration site conﬁrmed functional operation across all seven system components, successful BIM integra- tion, multi-stakeholder access control, and GDPR compliance through mobile-based data collection. User acceptance evaluation with 102 construction professionals re- vealed strong positive perceptions across all TAM constructs. Attitude toward use emerged as the strongest adoption driver (RII = 0.894), with Purpose Conﬁdence and Needs Alignment as highest-rated items (both RII = 0.92), demonstrating strong conceptual acceptance of the plat- form’s value and relevance. System Integration (RII = 0.91) emerged as the most valued ease of use factor, whilst Independent Operation (RII = 0.82) identiﬁed training and support as priority enhancement areas. These ﬁndings conﬁrm that interoperability with established BIM work- ﬂows critically facilitates adoption [55]. Theoretically, the research extends TAM to multi-stakeholder con- struction environments, revealing that attitude-driven acceptance may be as important as perceived usefulness in complex renovation con- texts. This ﬁnding provides empirical support for the RINNO barrier framework [8], demonstrating that human barriers (trust, alignment) can equal technological barriers (functionality, usability) in determining adoption outcomes. The study advances socio-technical systems theory by demonstrating successful coordination of multiple performance do- mains through GDPR-compliant architecture with stakeholder-speciﬁc access control. Practically, the stakeholder-informed design process positioned users as active co-creators, embodying the ‘human-in-the-loop’ prin- ciple essential for eﬀective DT implementation [61]. Strong perfor- mance of integration-related factors provides empirical guidance em- phasising that seamless workﬂow integration represents a critical suc- cess factor. The platform demonstrates potential for establishing new project management routines supporting sustainability’s three pillars: enhanced collaboration (social), waste reduction (environmental), and improved cost and schedule management (economic). Deployment on the French demonstration site illustrates this multi-domain capability: whilst achieving substantial environmental performance (73% emis- sions reduction), the project faced signiﬁcant economic challenges (39% cost increase, NPV: -e326,000), exemplifying the complex trade-oﬀs the RDT’s KPI tracking framework is designed to make transparent to stake- holders. Successful DT implementation requires not only technical capa- bility but also systematic attention to human factors, training provision, and organisational change management processes that enable the socio- technical transformation necessary for deep renovation acceleration. Introduction Underground pipeline networks form the backbone of modern urban infrastructure, enabling the efficient transportation of water, gas, sewage, and various industrial fluids [1] . The underground pipeline systems are essential to economic growth, public health, and environ - mental protection. However, with the progression of time, underground pipelines become increasingly susceptible to deterioration due to ma - terial degradation, external loads, corrosion, and soil-structure in - teractions [2] . The failure of underground pipelines can result in severe economic losses, environmental contamination, service disruptions, and even loss of life. Consequently, ensuring the long-term structural integrity of underground pipelines has become a critical challenge in civil and infrastructure applications. Conventional methods, such as replacing deteriorated pipelines, often result in substantial inconvenience to local residents and industrial facilities due to service interruption. In contrast to conventional methods, various trenchless technologies have been developed to restore the function of pipelines while minimizing excavation and associated disruptions [3] . Among them, the cured-in-place pipe (CIPP) lining method has gained wide - spread recognition for its adaptability to different pipe materials and sizes, cost-effectiveness, and capacity to restore the structural integrity of deteriorated pipes [4] . The CIPP lining method involves inserting a resin-based liner mate - rial into an underground pipe. After being cured in place appropriately, the liner effectively forms a new structural layer within the host pipe [5] . Enabled by the enhanced structural integrity provided by the CIPP liner, this method enhances pipeline durability, improves hydraulic ef - ficiency, and extends the service life of the liner-rehabilitated pipe sys - tem. Despite these advantages, the liner-rehabilitated pipe system can * Corresponding author. E-mail address: tao133@purdue.edu (C. Tao). Common damage mechanisms include cracking, delamination, and deformation due to internal or external interferences, with buckling being one of the most critical failure modes [6–8] . Buckling in underground pipelines, whether orig - inal or rehabilitated with a liner, can be caused by soil loads, ground - water pressure, traffic-induced stresses, or vacuum-induced forces [9–12] . Since such failures often occur suddenly and can lead to critical structural instability, early detection and mitigation are essential for ensuring the pipeline’s reliability. Prior studies have evaluated the structural performance of CIPP liners through experimental testing and analytical or numerical modeling, including investigations of ovaliza - tion, local buckling instability, liner–host pipe interaction, and critical capacity [13–16] . These studies have improved the understanding of the progression of buckling from initial imperfection to post-buckling deformation in liner-rehabilitated pipes. However, existing in - vestigations generally rely on visual inspection or discrete sensors, which are unable to capture the distributed strain evolution that governs buckling initiation. Therefore, real-time tracking of buckling behavior and deterioration in liner-rehabilitated systems remains an open chal - lenge, due to the inaccessibility of pipelines and the complexity of their operating environment. Structural health monitoring (SHM) is an approach to assess the condition of critical infrastructure, providing valuable insights that can help detect early signs of damage and prevent catastrophic failures [17–19] . However, traditional SHM techniques face significant chal - lenges when applied to underground pipelines. Conventional point-based sensors, such as strain gauges or fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors, are difficult to deploy in underground environments and offer limited spatial coverage. Therefore, these sensors are not suitable for monitoring underground pipelines, where the locations of potential failures are unpredictable. To address these challenges, distributed fiber optic sensing (DFOS) has emerged as a promising solution to monitor the structural health condition of underground pipelines in real-time [20] . Unlike conventional sensors, DFOS enables spatially continuous strain measurements along the entire length of the optic fiber. This continuous coverage allows for real-time strain monitoring over a large area. Anomalous strain signals are indicative of structural irregularities, enabling both damage identification and localization. As a result, DFOS facilitates the timely detection of localized damage, enables the assess - ment of overall strain distribution, and monitors structural performance with higher resolution and accuracy. Additionally, DFOS offers several advantages, including its ability to work in harsh environments, its high sensitivity to strain variations, and its long-term stability, making it an ideal choice for underground applications. Recent studies have employed DFOS as a strain monitoring tool for various underground applications, including fractured steel pipelines subjected to operational pressure [21] , polyvinyl chloride liners under grouting pressure [22] , and corrugated steel culverts under traffic loading [23] . These previous studies demonstrate the capability of DFOS to provide continuous strain monitoring in underground structures, establishing a foundation for its effective integration into real-time SHM applications. The effectiveness of DFOS relies on its underlying principle of analyzing backscattered light along the optical fiber. In an optical distributed sensor interrogator, a laser source with a continuously varying frequency is injected into the fiber, and the backscattered Rayleigh signal is analyzed. Since the Rayleigh scattering pattern is unique to each segment of the fiber, any mechanical strain changes the local optical path, resulting in a shift in the backscattered signal. By comparing these shifts with the reference state, highly precise distributed strain measurements can be obtained at sub-millimeter-scale spatial resolution (SR). This technique makes Rayleigh-based DFOS particularly suitable for detecting fine-scale strain variations and tracking minor defect initiation and propagation. As a result, it is highly promising for SHM applications of underground pipes, where direct visual detection is often infeasible. DFOS provides a powerful tool for monitoring the structural health condition of underground pipelines. However, it is unrealistic to have DFOS fully cover the entire pipe due to the high cost of fiber optic sensors. Additionally, raw sensing data alone may not fully capture the complexities of pipeline behavior under real-world conditions. There - fore, in practical deployment, DFOS is installed on instrumented seg - ments that terminate at accessible interrogation points. Since pipeline distress typically occurs locally, the sensing data from these instru - mented segments are used to update finite element models of the broader system. By combining localized measurements with numerical predictions, the integration of DFOS and finite element analysis (FEA) provides a comprehensive assessment of the structural performance of the underground pipeline system. FEA-based numerical modeling en - ables the simulation of various loading conditions, damage mechanisms, and failure modes, allowing for the prediction of how a pipeline will behave under different scenarios [24,25] . This approach shows a promising approach to conduct virtual testing on pipelines and generate a dynamic, real-time digital replica through digital twin technology. In the context of infrastructure, the digital twin framework can continu - ously update and provide a virtual representation that offers direct insight into the structural condition [26] . Wang et al. [27] applied polynomial fitting to process experimental and numerical strain data and reconstruct the structural deformation of a pipe-liner system in a three-dimensional digital twin model. Wang et al. [28] developed a digital twin system for pipeline fatigue crack monitoring through the internet of things, advanced analytics, cloud computing, and virtual reality. However, there is a lack of efficient and timely digital twin modeling methods for SHM in pipeline systems, as current practices require substantial computational time for data post-processing. To address this issue, the incorporation of machine learning (ML) algo - rithms represents a further step toward real-time data processing be - tween the physical twin and the digital twin [29] . Limited studies have advanced digital twin-based SHM using ML approaches in infrastructure domains, most of which focus on bridge components [30–32] , moni - toring deflection, moment, and crack propagation. Similar advance - ments have not yet been fully realized for underground pipeline infrastructure applications. Building on these established practices, extending the use of ML toward continuous condition monitoring of pipeline structures, enabling more effective and preventive SHM stra - tegies, is necessary. In order to enhance the effectiveness of SHM for underground pipeline infrastructure, it is important to enable preventive damage identification and localization. Hoult et al. [33] created artificial dam - ages in steel plate samples and detected subsequent deterioration using DFOS by measuring strain variations. Lan et al. [34] installed DFOS on a perforated steel pipeline to measure the longitudinal strain distribution under ground movement. They found that while the global behavior of the perforated pipeline remained unchanged, the strain variation around the perforation was significant, highlighting the capability of DFOS for damage identification in underground pipeline applications. However, these studies were based on predefined, visually identifiable damages. To bridge this gap, DFOS needs to be utilized to continuously monitor the full structural damage evolution, from initially intact con - ditions through progressive deterioration leading to failure, enabling comprehensive SHM of damage propagation and facilitating early-stage damage assessment. One key component of structural damage originates from the plastic deformation of steel, which induces permanent defor - mation. Once the localized strain exceeds the yield strain, the affected region enters plasticity. This plastic region of deformation cannot restore its original shape after the external load is removed, resulting in permanent deformation. This irreversible deformation compromises the strength, bearing capacity, and transmission efficiency of the under - ground pipe [35] . Another key focus is the investigation of crack initi - ation at critical structural interfaces, such as the adhesive layer. The adhesive layer is the intermediate layer between the pipe and CIPP liner, used to bond the liner firmly to the host pipe [36] . This layer has been largely overlooked in previous studies despite its impact on the overall rehabilitation performance of the CIPP liner. Large structural J. Duan et al. Therefore, it is essential to identify the conditions and lo - cations of such cracks. In this context, integrating DFOS with the CIPP liner is suggested as a potential solution to monitor the condition of the entire liner-rehabilitated pipe. While the installation of DFOS directly on the underground pipe remains challenging, embedding DFOS within the liner prior to installation offers a promising alternative. Since DFOS is not directly attached to the pipe surface, the strain monitoring results may be affected, making it critical to investigate the performance of DFOS-integrated liners in effectively monitoring pipe conditions. The objective of this research is to develop a digital twin framework for real-time monitoring and damage identification in underground pipelines. In terms of the strain field correlation method introduced in Section 2.4 , the DFOS strain fields are input into the CNN model and set as the ground truth. The FEA strain fields are organized into a database, which includes all FEA strain fields at the displacement levels from 0 to 7 mm. By extracting the features from DFOS strain fields and iteratively comparing the cosine similarities within the FEA strain field database, the most similar pairs are identified. Fig. 12 shows the results of strain field correlation. The corresponding displacements of the matched strain field pairs are compared, showing an average displacement difference of 0.754 mm. This difference is minor and remains within an acceptable sub-millimeter-level range, making it suitable for practical applications in the underground pipeline industry [54] . Based on strain correlation results, this study reconstructs the deformation and develops the digital twin framework of the buckled single steel substrate. Fig. 13 (a) shows the 3D digital twin framework for visualizing the deformation of the single steel substrate subjected to eccentric compression. The left figure illustrates that as the vertical displacement of the top grip increases to 7 mm, the buckling-induced deformation of the steel substrate progressively intensifies. The four figures on the right provide separate visual representations of the deformation at different displacement levels, further demonstrating that the highest deformation is consistently observed at the center of the free edge between the grips. Additionally, for typical structural steel, the yield strain is commonly around 0.002 [37] . When the estimated strain in the steel substrate exceeds the yield strain, the corresponding region is classified as the plastic region. Fig. 13 (b) illustrates the developed 3D digital-twin framework for visualizing plastic regions of deformation in the single steel substrate. These regions originate at the edges of grip areas and expand to the high deformation regions as eccentric compression increases. The above method leverages the continuous measurement capacity of DFOS, enabling the detection and mapping of the potential damage. It enhances the efficiency of real-time structural damage monitoring and can be employed to identify potential damages in liner-rehabilitated underground pipeline infrastructure, ultimately extending the structural service life.
