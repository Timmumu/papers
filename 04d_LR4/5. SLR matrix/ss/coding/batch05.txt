ABSTRACT Earthquakes pose severe threats to urban safety. Traditional prediction methods fail to fully quantify seismic uncertainties and address low-probability, high-impact ‘‘long-tail risks’’. This paper proposes a partially validated probabilistic urban seismic damage prediction framework integrating large- scale scenario ensembles and digital twins. The framework is conceptually designed with four layers: data fusion, digital twin modeling, scenario ensemble simulation, and probabilistic assessment. Due to current data and computational constraints, empirical validation is limited to two core modules—the PI-GAN ground motion generation module and the probabilistic damage cloud module—using the 2021 Y angbi Ms6.4 earthquake (single event, single region). The remaining two modules (infrastructure cascading failure and social behavior modeling) are presented as conceptual designs requiring future validation. The validation results show that the Physics-Informed Generative Adversarial Network (PI-GAN) generates ground motions with ≤5% PGA error and ≤10% response spectrum error, while the probabilistic damage cloud module achieves 77.7% overall classification accuracy (with 79.5% recall, 87.3% precision, and 83.2% F1-score) and 89.3% structure-damage matching degree. The framework enables a shift from empirical judgment to data-driven pre-simulation, providing a partially validated and technically feasible solution for improving the accuracy and comprehensiveness of urban seismic risk assessment, while clearly acknowledging that full- chain validation—particularly for extreme M≥7.0 scenarios and unvalidated modules—remains as future work. DIGITAL TWIN AND PROBABILISTIC SEISMIC RISK ASSESSMENT As a core enabling technology for deep integration of cyber-physical systems, digital twin technology provides an unprecedented platform for refined and dynamic disaster sim- ulation at the urban scale [4]. It has been applied to construct seismic response models of buildings [5], visualize seismic damage simulation results [6], and even evaluate the seismic resilience of urban lifeline systems [7]. Recent advances in probabilistic seismic risk assessment have increasingly incorporated stochastic rupture simulations within PSHA frameworks, aiming to better characterize source and path uncertainties [8], [9]. However, existing DT-based earth- quake risk frameworks often lack integration with large-scale, physics-consistent scenario ensembles and empirical valida- tion against real seismic events. How to combine large-scale seismic scenario ensembles with digital twin technology for probabilistic and forward-looking urban-scale seismic damage prediction remains a challenge requiring in-depth research [10]. C. SCOPE AND VALIDATION STATUS OF THIS STUDY It is important to clarify at the outset that the proposed framework is presented as a conceptual architecture with partial empirical validation. Specifically, the current study validates two of the four framework modules: (1) the PI-GAN-based ground motion generation module, and (2) the probabilistic damage cloud module. The infrastructure cascading failure module and the Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) social behavior module are described in their concep- tual design but have not been empirically tested against real earthquake data. The validation is further limited to a single moderate-magnitude event (2021 Y angbi Ms6.4) and a single village (Huaian Village, 112 buildings). Generalizability to other tectonic settings, urban typologies, or large-magnitude (M≥7.0) scenarios remains unvalidated and is a direction for future research. All claims of framework performance in this paper should be interpreted strictly within these defined boundaries. D. MAIN CONTRIBUTIONS AND PAPER ORGANIZATION The main contributions of this study are threefold: (1) a sys- tematic integration of high-fidelity digital twin modeling with physics-informed generative AI (PI-GAN) for ground motion synthesis—with validation limited to moderate-magnitude scenarios; (2) a full-chain probabilistic simulation workflow that couples seismic source generation, site response, struc- tural analysis, and infrastructure cascading failure within a unified digital twin environment—where the infrastructure and social modules remain conceptual at this stage; and (3) an empirical validation of the core modules (PI-GAN and damage cloud) using a real earthquake case (2021 Y angbi Ms6.4 earthquake), demonstrating the framework’s practical applicability and accuracy within the validated scope. Its core idea is to abandon the pursuit of predicting a ‘‘most likely’’ future, but instead systematically explore thousands of ‘‘possible’’ futures through computational experiments and statistically analyze their risk distribution. This study will elaborate on the framework’s architecture, key technologies, and implementation process, validate its core modules with empirical data, and discuss how it can provide revolutionary decision support for urban planning, resilience improvement, and precise emergency response—while clearly distinguish- ing demonstrated capabilities from conceptual extensions. INTEGRATION FRAMEWORK OF LARGE-SCALE SCENARIO ENSEMBLES AND DIGITAL TWINS The proposed prediction framework is essentially a complex system integrating advanced sensing, refined modeling, high- performance simulation, and intelligent decision-making. Its core lies in establishing a closed-loop, bidirectional inter- action between the physical city and its digital counterpart, where real-time or near-real-time data from the physical world (e.g., IoT sensor readings, InSAR deformation) are continuously ingested to update and calibrate the digital models, while simulation outputs from the digital twin (e.g., predicted damage states, cascading failure paths) are fed back to inform monitoring, planning, and emergency response in the physical city. This dynamic interaction enables a panoramic, probabilistic exploration of seismic risks via com- putational experiments. Logically, the framework is divided into four closely coupled layers, collectively forming a closed loop from real-world sensing to decision support. The overall architecture, which explicitly illustrates the data and inter- action flows between the physical city and the digital twin, is shown in Fig. 1. The figure highlights the bidirectional syn- chronization loops (physical→virtual and virtual→physical) that are characteristic of a true digital twin system, moving beyond a static conceptual diagram. Railway systems are using large-scale asset substitutio n, preventative repairs, and data-based dependability techniques to solve these problems. The recent introduction of solid-state interlocking (SSI), light-emitting diode (LED) signals that are and constantly track circuiting has made things more durable and less likely to break. Data loggers also let you keep an eye on how well your equipment is working all the time so you can do predictive maintenance. Cybersecurity features are also being added to networks more and more to keep them safe from digital attacks (Wang et al., 2024c; García-M éndez et al., 2025; Vieira et al., 2024). The perpetual modification in RSE struct ure is characterised by technological innovation and robotics. Contemporary signalling technology based on electronic interlocking (EI) and automatic train control (ATC) is lowering human participation and cumulative operating efficiency (Drózd and Rosiński, 2023). To address these issues, a physics-informed neural network (PINN)-based edge-cloud collaborative digital twin (D T) architecture (Kazemi Naeini et al., 2025) has been developed. The framework can be broken down into three layers: physical asset layer (IoT sensors for data collection), edge layer (localised devices like FPGAs and microcontrollers running lightweight PINN models for low-latency anomaly detection), and a cloud layer (computati onally intensive PINN models for model refinement and system-level optimisation) (Yang et al., 2024; Zia et al., 2025). The Moulay Abdellah Dam The Moulay Abdellah Dam (Fig. 1) was constructed between 1999 and 2002 and is located approximately 60 km from Agadir, Morocco, in the Souss-Massa region. Designed to support regional water resource management, the dam plays a crucial role in ensuring water supply, irrigation, and ﬂood control for Agadir and surrounding areas. Structurally, the dam is a reinforced concrete double curvature arch designed to eﬃciently transfer external loads to the adjacent rock abut- ments. This design minimizes the amount of reinforced concrete re- quired compared to single-curved arch dams, while still ensuring struc- tural stability. The dam has a thickness varying between 7 meters, at the base, and 2.5 meters, on the top, and extends for 166 meters along the bridge, reaching a maximum height of 65 meters from the founda- tion. The reservoir generates a storage capacity of 110,000 cubic meters and regulates an annual water volume of 27,500 cubic meters. The structural performance of the dam is inﬂuenced by several in- teracting forces, including the hydrostatic pressure exerted by the reser- voir, the uplift water pressure at the foundation, and the self-weight of the dam body. Environmental factors such as temperature variations, chemical reactions, and material aging also aﬀect its behavior over time. Additionally, external loads from seismic activity, ﬂoods, and sediment accumulation contribute to the dam’s overall stability challenges. Ensur- ing long-term structural integrity requires continuous Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) to detect early warning signs of potential failure mechanisms. To achieve this, the Moulay Abdellah Dam is equipped with an ad- vanced monitoring system that collects real-time data on structural and environmental conditions. Three strain gauges, located on the right shoulder, right bank, left bank, and valley bottom, measure deforma- tions in the body of the dam. The displacement monitoring system in- cludes direct and reverse pendulums, installed in three dedicated cham- bers, to track horizontal movements, and a vinchon-based displacement measurement system, based on the use of 17 winches, installed across the dam joints on the crest and on the abutments to monitor the open- ing of the joint. Two accelerographs, located at the crest and left bank, record seismic activity and structural vibrations. Introduction Bridges are fundamental elements of transportation networks, providing critical con- nections that support the movement of pedestrians, vehicles, and rail systems. Over time, exposure to environmental factors—including precipitation, wind, temperature variations, water currents, and wave action—as well as repeated loading from vehicular traffic, leads to progressive structural deterioration. In Australia, bridges are typically designed for a service life of 100 years. To ensure structural performance and user safety throughout this period, regular inspection and maintenance are essential. For reinforced concrete bridges, corrosion of embedded steel reinforcement is a princi- pal mechanism of deterioration, often resulting in concrete cracking and spalling. Other commonly observed forms of degradation include bearing deformation or misalignment, prestress losses, shrinkage, and creep. These issues, if left unaddressed, can substantially impair the structural capacity and serviceability of bridge systems. Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 https://doi.org/10.3390/s25123727 Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 2 of 19 In addition to structural performance considerations, the preservation of heritage bridges plays a vital role in promoting cultural and environmental sustainability. These structures embody the historical and architectural legacy of a community, contributing to its cultural identity, social continuity, and sense of place. By maintaining and rehabilitating heritage bridges rather than opting for demolition and reconstruction, it is possible to achieve significant environmental and economic benefits. Adaptive reuse and preservation strategies reduce material consumption, minimize construction-related emissions, and extend the life cycle of existing infrastructure. Furthermore, the conservation of such assets allows future generations to engage with and appreciate their historical significance, thereby reinforcing the societal value of sustainable infrastructure management. 1.1. Assessment of Historical Structures in Literature There exists a substantial body of research dedicated to the structural assessment of historical bridges and buildings, employing a range of sensing technologies and analytical methodologies. Valente et al. [ 1] deployed accelerometers to validate a finite-element (FE) model of a historical steel truss bridge spanning the Tua River in Portugal. Batar et al. [2] applied terrestrial laser scanning to generate a high-resolution point cloud model of the Ayvalıkemer (Sillyon) masonry arch bridge in Turkey, enabling accurate geometric representation. Alpaslan et al. [3] conducted dynamic testing and finite element modeling to evaluate the structural behavior of a historical stone arch bridge, also located in Turkey. Granata et al. [4] utilized ground-penetrating radar (geo-radar) to investigate the internal reinforcement configuration of a cantilever reinforced concrete bridge in Italy. Ademovic [5] conducted a comprehensive structural assessment of a reinforced concrete arch bridge in Bosnia and Herzegovina, incorporating thermal imaging, non-destructive testing (NDT), and static load testing using heavy trucks. Bayane and Brühwiler [6] employed acoustic emission monitoring and strain gauge instrumentation to assess a steel–concrete composite bridge in Switzerland. On the other hand, an accurate measurement of vertical displacement in bridges re- mains a significant challenge, particularly for heritage structures where invasive techniques are not appropriate. In response, researchers have developed various non-contact monitor- ing systems, which are especially well-suited for heritage bridge assessment. Zhao et al. [7] proposed a laser spot recognition system for long-term displacement monitoring. Brown et al. [8] introduced a hybrid laser–video displacement sensor. Waqas et al. [9] developed a response-based method using laser displacement sensors to detect malfunctioning steel bridge bearings. Pan et al. [10] applied an off-axis digital image correlation (DIC) technique to measure vertical bridge deflections with high precision. The DIC technique was also used to monitor deformation and crack propagation in concrete structures [11]. Recent de- velopments in image recognition and artificial intelligence have found applications in using computer vision to detect defects in pavements and bridges [12–14]. To assess the dynamic properties of bridges, innovative methods include using test vehicles [15], parked cars [16], mobility vehicles and bicycles [17], and scooters [18]. Recently, the extreme function theory for damage detection has been applied to a case study of structural health monitoring of the well-known I-40 bridge [19]. In addition, recent development and popularity of using drone-facilitated robotic bridge inspections [20,21]. Common strategies for the assessment of historical bridges include: (i) static load testing using heavy vehicles combined with instrumentation, such as strain gauges and displacement sensors to capture structural response; (ii) dynamic testing to extract natural frequencies and mode shapes for finite-element model calibration; (iii) point cloud tech- nologies for capturing the complex geometry of structures, such as masonry arch bridges; (iv) non-destructive testing methods for evaluating material properties and identifying Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 3 of 19 signs of deterioration; and (v) non-contact displacement monitoring techniques, including laser-based systems and digital image correlation, for tracking structural deformation over time. 1.2. Current Practices of Bridge Assessments in Australia The structural integrity of bridges in Australia is maintained through routine inspec- tions and continuous maintenance activities, which are essential for the early detection and remediation of deterioration. At present, the majority of inspections rely heavily on visual assessment techniques. According to AS5100 Part 7 [22], bridge inspections in Australia are categorized into three hierarchical levels: • Level 1 inspections involve routine visual assessments aimed at identifying irregu- larities or anomalies that may warrant further investigation. Common issues noted during these inspections include excessive cracking, corrosion of steel components, degradation of expansion joints, and bearing deformations. In the state of Victoria, Level 1 inspections are typically conducted biannually by trained inspectors, not necessarily professional engineers. Level 1 inspections are also conducted after major events, such as flooding or passage of overweight, overmass vehicles. • Level 2 inspections provide more detailed and quantified information to support asset management and maintenance planning. These visual assessments evaluate the outcomes of past rehabilitation efforts, identify current maintenance requirements, and support the forecasting of future condition deterioration and budgetary needs. In Victoria, Level 2 inspections are generally conducted every 3–5 years by more experienced bridge inspectors, often under engineers’ supervision. • Level 3 inspections consist of comprehensive structural investigations undertaken by qualified engineers. These may involve advanced techniques such as structural modelling, load testing, and both destructive and non-destructive testing methods. Level 3 inspections are typically initiated in response to findings from Level 1 or 2 assessments and must be conducted by structural engineers or technical specialists. Inspections and maintenance of vehicular bridges are the responsibilities of the state road agencies. State agencies published the traffic volume (Annual Average Daily Traffic, AADT) and bridge ratings in the public domain. The maintenance and repair of heritage bridges in Victoria are governed by detailed technical guidelines aimed at preserving both structural integrity and historical significance. The Bridge Maintenance and Repair Manual [23] serves as a key reference, outlining appropriate interventions based on the type and severity of deterioration observed during inspections. Emphasis is placed on using materials and techniques that are sympathetic to the original construction, ensuring that essential repairs do not compromise heritage values. To optimize resource allocation, maintenance activities are prioritized using a structured decision-making framework that considers factors such as the bridge’s physical condition, heritage classification, functional importance within the transport network, and available funding. Furthermore, compliance with the Heritage Act 2017 [ 24] is mandated for all state-registered structures, obligat- ing asset owners to uphold minimum standards for maintenance and repair. This legal framework ensures that neglect or inappropriate interventions do not lead to irreversible damage or loss of cultural value, and it provides a mechanism for enforcement through repair orders or penalties where necessary. Together, these measures support a balanced approach to infrastructure management, integrating technical performance requirements with heritage conservation objectives. Despite these structured practices, incidents of bridge failures globally have high- lighted limitations in conventional inspection regimes. As a result, there is increasing Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 4 of 19 interest in Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) as a complementary or alternative strategy. SHM involves the deployment of sensor networks to monitor key structural parameters, such as vibrational responses, crack widths, and deflections, in real time. This continuous data stream enables prompt identification of structural anomalies, thereby enhancing the responsiveness and reliability of bridge maintenance programs. 2. Descriptions of Heritage RC Bridge in This Study The St Kilda Street Bridge (previously known as the Elwood Canal Bridge) is located approximately 10 km south of the Melbourne central business district, in the state of Victoria, Australia. According to the Heritage Council of Victoria Australia, the bridge possesses significant heritage value as it was the first reinforced concrete girder bridge in Victoria and, possibly, in Australia. It demonstrates the earliest stage in the development of reinforced concrete girders in the construction of bridges, a significant technological development of its time. It spans over a canal, which was a part of the drainage system in the low-lying swampy land in the Elsternwick and Elwood districts. The bridge was designed by renowned Australian engineer and military leader General Sir John Monash. Construction began in July 1905 and was completed in September of the same year. The construction of the bridge used the patented “Monier reinforced concrete method”. It utilized “compressive strength of concrete and tensile strength of iron”, which is essentially the modern-day principle of reinforced concrete. Phase 4: Eradication This phase focuses on a comprehensive analysis of data obtained from the Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) system to identify the root cause of the structural anomaly. A detailed evaluation of the recorded data facilitates a deeper understanding of the factors Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 17 of 19 that contributed to the incident and supports the development of targeted mitigation strategies to prevent recurrence. This process involves correlating SHM data with incident-specific observations to determine underlying failure mechanisms or operational deficiencies. Root cause identifi- cation is critical to inform the design of evidence-based interventions, ranging from policy updates to physical modifications, that enhance the long-term resilience of the structure. Example: If the SHM system records indicate that microcracking occurred due to repeated instances of multiple overloaded trucks crossing the bridge simultaneously, regu- latory measures can be introduced to control such loading scenarios. These may include imposing stricter load limits, restricting certain vehicle classes, or installing weight moni- toring systems and warning signage at approach roads to deter excessive loading. The principal objective of the eradication phase is to eliminate the underlying condi- tions that resulted in the incident and to implement corrective and preventative measures that ensure continued structural integrity and public safety. 5.5. Phase 5—Documentation The final phase involves systematic documentation of all significant incidents that have occurred over the life cycle of the structure. This includes detailed records of the root causes, containment and eradication measures implemented, and any secondary defects that emerged in other structural components as a consequence of the initial incident. The primary objective is to establish a comprehensive repository of lessons learned, which serves as a reference for future incident response strategies. Thorough documentation enables more efficient resolution of similar issues in other structures by reducing the time required to identify effective solutions. Furthermore, it contributes to the broader knowledge base of potential structural vulnerabilities and failure modes, thereby enhancing hazard awareness and risk mitigation efforts. 6. Conclusions This study presents a comprehensive approach to the assessment, monitoring, and management of a heritage-listed reinforced concrete bridge in Australia. Through detailed field investigations and the deployment of non-invasive sensing technologies, including accelerometers for ambient vibration testing, non-contact laser displacement sensors, and elastic displacement gauges for crack width monitoring, a robust dataset was obtained without compromising the structure’s integrity. These measurements supported the de- velopment and validation of a digital twin model, verified through comparison with experimentally identified natural frequencies and mode shapes. The digital twin was subsequently employed to simulate various “what-if” risk scenar- ios, enabling proactive evaluation of structural vulnerabilities under different loading and damage conditions. The simulation scenarios considered both the passage of overweight vehicles and the occurrence of scour damage affecting the foundations of one or two bridge piers. The results indicate that the passage of overweight vehicles significantly increases the force demand on the bridge girders, potentially leading to structural damage. These find- ings reinforce the necessity of strict enforcement of load restrictions on this heritage bridge. Furthermore, simulations of scour-induced foundation degradation revealed notable shifts in natural frequencies and mode shapes, suggesting that such damage can alter the bridge’s dynamic characteristics and may be detectable through ambient vibration monitoring. Building on these insights, a structured Incident Response Framework (IRF) was proposed to enhance the integration of Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) systems into routine asset management practices. This framework facilitates the timely detection, Sensors 2025, 25, 3727 18 of 19 classification, and mitigation of structural anomalies, thereby improving resilience and extending service life while minimizing intervention costs. The methodology outlined in this paper demonstrates the practical benefits of combin- ing digital technologies and SHM for the preservation of heritage infrastructure, offering a replicable model for similar bridges worldwide. Introduction The construction industry is experiencing thought-provoking hazard statistics compared to other industries [ 1 ]. The high number of hazards highlighted by these statistics is due to the production environment constantly changing, which makes safety planning labor-intensive. Automated safety analysis and prevention strategies such as [ 2 – 6 ] can identify and plan mitigation to prevent hazards, with greatly increased spatial and temporal resolution beyond what is achieved through the current manual practices of safety analysis. Knowledge representation in construction Different knowledge representations have been proposed with the increasing interest and research of Digital Twins for construction. Several large research projects in Europe are ongoing (e.g., COGITO [ 40 ] and BIM2TWIN [ 41 ]), each developing an ontology to represent their captured knowledge and needs. While the different representations share the same objective (i.e., to capture the historical, planned, and future state of the construction situation to facilitate an improved con - struction industry), the approaches still vary slightly. Some strategies invite unprocessed data, and some prefer information and knowledge (i. e., processing data elevates it to information and combinations if in - formation creates knowledge [ 23 ]). Ontologies capture the relevant classes and relations that are specific enough to represent the data, in - formation, and knowledge but general enough for similar use cases. Many ontologies exist for different purposes, and reuse of both ontol - ogies and schemas should be considered when creating new ones, as is the case with the Digital Twin Construction Ontology (DTCO) [ 42 ], which relies on, e.g., Building Topology Ontology [ 43 ] and Building Element Ontology [ 44 ]. However, DTCO does not represent safety- related classes and relationships. Within the domain of safety in construction, previous work has been investigating ontologies to capture and represent safety-related classes and their relationships to other classes within and outside of the safety domain. Among others, [ 4 ] presented an ontology that connected the potential hazards (i.e., the new class of the safety ontology) to building elements through job steps, activities, and tasks (i.e., classes of existing ontologies). The safety ontology also captured the potential hazards and their relation to mitigation and safety regulation. In [ 45 ], the safety ontology presented in [ 4 ] was extended with the capabilities to repre - sent potential hazards to define hazard spaces, represented as spatial artifacts. [ 34 ] extended and detailed the spatial artifacts to cover falls- from-heights and struck-by-falling objects hazards in 4D. While ontologies are available for capturing construction project information and others for safety information, they must be merged to exploit interdisciplinary knowledge. This also means that additional classes and relations are needed to connect the domains. Thus, this study will reuse and extend the existing ontologies to incorporate these con - nections and additional safety-related perspectives. There is a growing body of research that investigates the integration of knowledge graphs, ontologies and LLMs [ 46 , 47 ]. Key areas of inte - gration include using knowledge graphs as a form of structure training data [ 48 ], and using LLMs for constructing knowledge graphs [ 49 ]. Towards exploiting such an integration in the construction safety domain, the research presented in this paper focuses on establishing an conceptually-rich ontology and data model of construction safety that can complement the growing role of LLMs in construction site analysis. 2.2. Introduction, Gaps in Knowledge, and Significance Road networks are susceptible to natural disasters such as floods that impact both the structural integrity of the roadways and their long-term serviceability. Considering the short-term implications for road usability is essential because debris from disasters and flooding can lead to road blockages ( Diakakis et al. 2020 ). During these disasters, the increased traffic due to mainline lane closures or people trying to escape the affected area can further affect the road network’s serviceability unfavorably ( Mitsakis et al. 2014 ). Typi- cally, following such an event, specific locations may become less accessible, which is especially critical for first responders in some cases and for people who are dispatched for rescue operations and emergent support. Effective monitoring and assessment of roadway conditions dur- ing extreme flooding events are essential to safeguarding public safety, supporting timely emergency response, and enhancing the resilience of transportation infrastructure. Current flood-monitoring approaches predominantly utilize physical sensor systems deployed along rivers and waterways to track rising water levels and potential overflow events. However, these systems offer valuable insights regarding local inundation; their sparse geographical distribution re- sults in incomplete and often fragmented data regarding the broader impact of flooding across extensive roadway networks. Further- more, these systems do not provide accurate, real-time flooding sta- tus updates for individual roadway segments, significantly limiting their effectiveness in critical decision-making scenarios during disasters (Y uan et al. 2022). Urban and infrastructure planning processes traditionally lev- erage floodplain mapping, historical flooding data, and estab- lished hydrological models to anticipate and mitigate flooding risks. Nevertheless, unprecedented climate phenomena, rapid urbanization, altered land-use patterns, and evolving topographic conditions have introduced substantial uncertainties into these conventional flood-risk assessments. Consequently, the reliability of existing floodplain-based planning and management frame- works has diminished, highlighting the urgent need for dynamic monitoring solutions that integrate real-time and historical data streams to accurately reflect evolving environmental and infra- structural conditions. A critical operational challenge arising from this data inad- equacy is the significant disruption to first responders ’ capabilities during flood events. The absence of integrated real-time monitoring and predictive tools results in uncertainty regarding roadway acces- sibility, closures, and congestion, complicating rescue operations, delaying response activities, and increasing safety risks for both responders and affected populations (Helderop and Grubesic 2019). Current methodologies for evaluating roadway serviceability have primarily focused on surface conditions and structural integrity, offering essential yet limited insights into infrastructure performance and maintenance needs ( Aleadelat et al. 2018 ; Fuentes et al. 2021 ). Despite recent significant advancements in modeling flooding im- pacts on transportation networks, most existing studies emphasized flood propagation modeling, highlighting inundation severity and spatial extent, but seldom comprehensively addressed the indirect impacts of flooding on roadway segments not directly submerged but still adversely influenced by nearby inundation at a network level (Y uan et al. 2022). As a result, prevailing assessment practices significantly underestimate the true scope of network disruption and fail to capture the complexity of roadway serviceability resto- ration efforts following severe flooding events. Considering the existing challenges in the current methodolo- gies, this study is guided by the following research question: How can a predictive, segment-level assessment of roadway serviceabil- ity under flood conditions be achieved in near real time by integrat- ing traffic patterns, flood simulation, and roadway attributes? This question drives the development and validation of an integrated framework that goes beyond binary inundation mapping to forecast both direct and indirect service impacts across a dynamic road network. To bridge this critical research gap, a broader and more holistic analytical approach is required to accurately define and evaluate roadway serviceability under flooding conditions at a macroscopic system network level. Specifically, this study defines serviceability as a multifaceted measure that integrates operational capacity, in- undation severity, and flood duration. Leveraging extensive histori- cal data sets, including pavement performance records, traffic flow data, infrastructure conditions, and historical flooding events, this research proposes an advanced, network-level evaluation frame- work. This methodology revolves around the creation and imple- mentation of a digital twin framework seamlessly integrated with flood simulation capabilities and graph neural network (GNN) mod- els. This innovative integration approach facilitates holistic, real- time simulations, precise visualizations, and predictive analyses of flooding impacts across entire roadway networks. These analyses encompass both directly inundated and indirectly affected roadway segments. In this study, we adopt the concept of a digital twin (DT) as a dynamic, virtual replica of a physical system that integrates real-time data streams, supports predictive analytics, and provides an interac- tive interface for operational decision-making. Unlike conventional simulation models, which often operate in isolation or with static inputs, digital twins are characterized by their ability to receive con- tinuous updates from physical assets and simulate system behavior under evolving conditions. Although some digital twin implemen- tations also include bidirectional control (e.g., sending actions back to physical systems), our focus lies on enabling real-time monitoring and forecasting based on live and historical data, within a responsive GIS-based environment. This qualifies the current system as an op- erational digital twin designed for flood event response. By addressing these fundamental limitations and questions in existing research and operational practice, this study aims to provide transportation agencies and emergency responders with robust, actionable insights for improved decision-making during ex- treme flooding scenarios. Ultimately, the anticipated outcomes in- clude better-informed evacuation strategies, optimized emergency response, more effective maintenance prioritization, and significantly enhanced resilience, safety, and operational reliability of transpor- tation infrastructure in the face of increasing flooding risks. The model predicts future traffic speed conditions on roadway segments using historical traffic patterns and spatial flood intensity as input features. Each road segment is represented as a node in the graph, with edges weighted based on physical proximity. We used a distance- based exponential weighting function with σ2 ¼ 0.1 and ε ¼ 0.5 to promote sparsity. The “USE_GA T_WEIGHTS” flag was set to “True”, which binarizes the adjacency matrix and includes self- loops, following the original GA T formulation. Edge attributes in- clude a 12-dimensional time series of flood exposure levels across multiple time stamps. The data set was constructed using a sliding time window where each graph snapshot included 12 historical time steps ( x) and 9 pre- diction time steps ( y), covering the 45-min future speed prediction. Data were standardized using z-score normalization. We split the data set using Temporal_Signal_Split with an 80∶20 time-based ra- tio, ensuring temporal continuity between training and testing sets. Model training was conducted over 15 epochs using minibatches of 2,000 graph snapshots per epoch. Training was performed on a CPU device using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01. The model optimized MSE between predicted and observed traffic speeds. Evaluation was conducted on a held-out 20% time-based test split, where the lowest test MSE was 0.2602. This demonstrates the model’s ability to generalize to new conditions and accurately esti- mate short-term traffic performance during flood events. Methodological Contributions and Novelty This study presents several key methodological contributions that ex- tend the current state of the art in flood-related serviceability modeling:  Coupling of hydrodynamic simulation with GA T-based predic- tion: Unlike prior models that treat flood effects in isolation or use fixed closure scenarios, this framework dynamically fuses flood simulation outputs with real-time and historical traffic data using a GA T. This coupling enables predictive modeling of both direct and indirect roadway disruptions.  Serviceability estimation at segment level: Whereas most prior works predicted binary status (flooded versus unflooded), the mo- del proposed in this study forecasts a continuous serviceability score—capturing varying degrees of speed degradation and acces- sibility at the road segment level, including for segments not directly inundated but indirectly impacted by rerouting and congestion.  Use of GA T architecture for indirect impact modeling: Our imple- mentation leverages the GA T’s attention mechanism to dynamically weigh the influence of neighboring segments, allowing the model to capture localized traffic propagation effects during floods — something traditional GNNs or statistical methods cannot do.  Integration with a GIS-based digital twin: The predicted outputs are embedded within an operational digital twin platform that supports real-time visualization, scenario exploration, and emer- gency decision support, bridging the gap between analytical models and end-user deployment. These innovations enable more accurate, spatially aware, and op- erationally actionable insights compared with prior flood disruption models that focused only on inundation status or used rigid traffic closure assumptions. Results and Discussion The proposed method is developed based on real traffic and flood- ing simulation data in conjunction with graph neural network mod- els to assess roadway serviceability from a short-term perspective. It considers two critical factors: roadway inundation and the result- ing traffic. The accuracy of the prediction model is influenced by the fol- lowing key elements:  The predictive accuracy of the flood model, which directly af- fects the overall accuracy of the prediction for the serviceability of the roadways.  The volume and granularity of traffic data used for training and testing the machine learning algorithm impacting the model ’s learning and generalization capabilities. These interdependent components collectively shape the effec- tiveness of the proposed method in forecasting roadway accessibil- ity during and after extreme flooding events. To evaluate the performance of the GA T model, this study used the MSE metric, which assesses the average squared difference be- tween the actual and predicted values. After the 12th interval, however, the MSE increased, which shows a decrease in the model’s accuracy. A primary factor contributing to this decline is that this study focuses on predicting flooded areas and inundated roadways, whereas the duration of inundation remains a subject for future study. Although the flood model successfully identified the locations of flooding, the actual recession of floodwaters occurred sooner than predicted. This discrepancy may be attributed to mitigation efforts by authorities, such as drainage operations and other flood manage- ment measures, which were not explicitly accounted for in the model due to the lack of accurate and detailed input data sets. In addition, to accurately evaluate the impact of flooding, dynamic modeling and simulation are required, representing changing parts and portions of roadways affected by inundation over time. This study, however, employed static flooding data that illustrates inundated areas of Houston city at specific time intervals (6 h). Despite the promising results, the study encountered some lim- itations related to the volume and scope of traffic data in Houston for model training and testing. Due to computational constraints and data handling capacity, only two zones in the Houston area — selected based on zip codes —were analyzed. Expanding the model to incorporate broader geographic coverage and more comprehen- sive data sets is expected to significantly enhance prediction accu- racy. In addition, the model does not account for other factors, such as a temporary event, including a car accident, that can make traffic changes that are not affected by a flooding disaster. These factors introduce uncertainty into the relationship between flooding and traffic behavior, limiting the granularity of analysis. Another important factor influencing the accuracy of the predic- tion model is the spatial and temporal resolution of the input data. Higher resolution flood and traffic data can provide more precise insights into roadway conditions, allowing for finer-grained predic- tions of serviceability. Additionally, integrating real-time sensor data, such as traffic cameras and GPS-based vehicle tracking, could en- hance the model ’s responsiveness to rapidly changing conditions. Even though the current case study was limited to two zones in Houston due to computational constraints and data-handling complex- ity, the framework is designed to be generalizable to other regions. Its modular structure allows for the integration of location-specific data such as flood exposure, roadway topology, and historical speed ob- servations. To apply this approach in different contexts (e.g., coastal cities with storm surge, or mountainous areas prone to flash flood- ing), appropriate adjustments to the flood modeling inputs and network features would be required.
