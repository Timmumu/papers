Rapid and reliable forecasts of tunnel behavior support proactive design decisions and risk mitigation. However, most existing surrogate models offer limited predictive resolution and accuracy, as they predict responses only at sparse monitoring points and ignore spatial information, which restricts their applicability in building comprehensive digital twin (DT) systems for full-field simulation. To address these limitations, this study proposes a DT framework for predicting ground settlement and lining deformation in shield tunnels affected by adjacent excavation. A hybrid surrogate model, called the Dual-Fusion Swin-Table Transformer Network (DF-STTN), is developed by combining Swin Transformer and TabTransformer to handle spatial and non-spatial inputs. To enhance multimodal interaction, a dual-stage fusion strategy is applied. The model is trained on high-fidelity finite element data, then updated and validated using results from laboratory-scale tunnel excavation tests. Results demonstrate that the inclusion of spatial information improves prediction accuracy, with notable im - provements achieved when three measurement points of the tunnel are used. DF-STTN outperforms conventional convolution-based and single-fusion models in both ground settlement and lining deformation tasks. The pro - posed framework achieves high predictive accuracy and generalization capability for early-stage tunnel design and excavation risk assessment. 1. Introduction Urban development has led to a significant increase in underground infrastructure projects, particularly in densely populated cities where space is limited. One common engineering challenge in the design phase is planning new tunnel construction near existing operational tunnels, where the impact of excavation-induced ground movements must be carefully assessed in advance. These interactions often lead to ground settlement and structural deformation of the existing tunnel lining, which may compromise structural integrity and operational safety [ 25 , 42 ]. Moreover, due to geological variability, complex soil-structure interaction, and evolving excavation stages, conventional deterministic models and static empirical charts often fail to capture the full range of possible deformation behaviors during adjacent tunneling[ 31 ]. Reliable prediction of such responses during the early design stage is therefore critical to avoid costly remediation, ensure compliance with safety standards, and support decision-making in the planning and design. Recent advancements in computational modeling and sensing tech - nologies have introduced the concept of a digital twin (DT) [ 10 , 18 , 19 , 36 ]. A DT is a virtual replica of a physical system, capable of dynamically utilizing various data sources to simulate structural behavior under different scenarios[ 2 , 6 ]. In tunneling applications, a DT can be used during the design stage to evaluate the potential impact of sequential excavation on nearby structures, allowing engineers to pro - actively assess risk and optimize design strategies. This approach en - hances the ability to test multiple design alternatives virtually, reducing reliance on conservative assumptions and overdesign. Despite its po - tential, the application of DTs in the design of underground infrastruc - ture presents several challenges. High-fidelity numerical simulations, such as finite element (FE) models, are commonly used to represent soil – structure interaction but are computationally intensive and not easily adaptable when new data becomes available[ 3 , 4 , 7 , 34 ]. * Corresponding author. Additionally, the behavior of tunnels under adjacent excavation is highly nonlinear and dependent on spatial–temporal factors, making it difficult for traditional models to generalize across different construc - tion scenarios[ 5 ]. This gap between simulation and reality highlights the need for lightweight and effective models that can combine simulation accuracy with speed to support rapid exploration of design options. Data-driven models, particularly surrogate models, have gained attention in this regard. These models approximate the behavior of more complex simulations by learning from precomputed results or experi - mental data, enabling faster prediction with minimal loss of accuracy [ 8 , 9 , 43 , 44 ]. In tunnel engineering, surrogate modeling approximates the nonlinear mapping between excavation/tunnel parameters and deformation responses, thereby reducing reliance on costly FE simula - tions. This approach follows the fundamental principle of function approximation, where machine learning models serve as universal approximators of complex soil–structure interaction functions that are otherwise captured through high-fidelity but computationally expensive FE simulations. There are various surrogate modeling approaches available, such as response surface methodology [ 24 ], Kriging [ 32 ], regression methods [ 1 , 16 , 46 ], polynomial expansions [ 15 ], and proper orthogonal decomposition [ 13 , 41 ]. In general, these surrogate modeling methods perform effectively when the prediction task involves only a few output variables, such as surface settlements at selected locations or deformations at key points around the tunnel lining [ 22 ]. However, the key challenge in the context of DTs for adjacent tunneling is that reliable design requires capturing full-field responses across many spatial posi - tions, excavation stages, and even different types of outputs (e.g., ground settlement, tunnel lining deformation). Unlike point-based pre - dictions, which only estimate behavior at selected monitoring locations, full-field prediction demands a model that can represent the continuous deformation field of the ground and lining. In this case, traditional surrogate modeling approaches reveal clear shortcomings. First, they typically scale poorly because their formulations (e.g., polynomial ex - pansions, response surfaces, or regression-based methods) become un - stable or computationally prohibitive as the dimensionality of the problem increases. Second, they often rely on simplified or limited input features, which restricts their ability to incorporate diverse information such as soil heterogeneity, construction parameters, and spatial geom - etry. Third, they struggle to generalize beyond the conditions repre - sented in the training data, making them sensitive to changes in geology, tunnel geometry, or construction sequence [ 2 , 3 , 4 , 6 , 22 ]. To address these limitations, more advanced surrogate modeling techniques are needed. Such models would enable the development of comprehensive and adaptive DT frameworks capable of delivering full-field predictions under varying scenarios. Multimodal data fusion has emerged as a promising approach for enhancing surrogate modeling in complex engineering tasks, as it en - ables the integration of diverse data types, such as spatial coordinates, tabular features, and sequential information, into a unified learning framework [ 11 ]. By combining different modalities, models can better capture the intricate relationships between excavation parameters, geological conditions, and system responses, ultimately improving pre - dictive performance and generalization. For example, Luo et al. [ 26 ] proposed a visual navigation framework combining Multimodal Graph Learning and Action Boost Memory to help agents navigate using only visual input. By dynamically modeling spatial, semantic, and historical information, the method significantly improves navigation success and efficiency in unseen environments. Yang et al. [ 38 , 39 ] proposes a Multimodal Knowledge-Guided Model (MKGM) for the joint recognition of bridge defects and structural information by integrating visual fea - tures, knowledge graphs, and label co-occurrence through a dual-graph convolutional network and MambaVision. By capturing hierarchical semantic associations among defects, components, and structural re - gions, the model achieves superior performance. These examples collectively highlight the growing potential of multimodal data fusion to improve model robustness, accuracy, and applicability in a wide range of engineering contexts. In this paper, the DT framework is designed to provide a lightweight yet accurate virtual replica of adjacent tunneling processes, with the specific goal of predicting full-field deformation of an existing tunnel during new excavation. The Dual-Fusion Swin-Table Transformer Network (DF-STTN) serves as the methodological core of the proposed DT framework. This capability is realized through a hybrid Transformer- based surrogate modeling architecture that integrates both spatial and tabular modalities [ 20 , 33 ]. Transformers, originally developed for nat - ural language processing, are chosen in this work because of their theoretical strength in capturing long-range dependencies through self- attention mechanisms. This property is particularly relevant for tunneling problems, where localized excavation can induce deformation at distant parts of the tunnel and ground surface. In contrast to convolution-based networks that emphasize local features, attention- based models dynamically assign importance weights across the entire input domain, making them well-suited for geotechnical systems char - acterized by both local variability and global interactions[ 21 , 35 , 45 ]. Spatio-temporal models (e.g., recurrent networks, ConvLSTMs) are effective at learning sequential dynamics but often face challenges in scalability and capturing long-range spatial dependencies, which are essential in adjacent tunneling where excavation at one location can influence deformation far away [ 40 ]. Graph-based models can represent irregular geometries and local connectivity, yet they typically require complex graph construction and incur high computational costs when extended to dense, full-field predictions [ 14 ]. Moreover, adjacent tunneling problems involve two distinct types of data: (1) non-spatial/ tabular data, such as soil stiffness, tunnel burial depth, and lining thickness, which describe global system properties, and (2) spatial data, such as settlement and deformation fields, which capture location- dependent responses. By explicitly designing a dual-fusion Trans - former architecture, this study ensures that both modalities are pro - cessed appropriately: TabTransformer for tabular features and Swin Transformer for spatial grids, before being integrated in a complemen - tary way. This design reflects the principle that predictive accuracy improves when models account for both global system descriptors and local spatial patterns. This makes them especially well-suited for ap - plications involving high-dimensional, structured, or sequential data. This architecture forms the core of the proposed DT framework, sup - porting high-resolution, multimodal prediction of tunnel behavior. In summary, this study proposes a DT framework tailored for the design phase of tunnel projects involving adjacent excavation. The proposed approach integrates deep learning-based surrogate modeling, feature encoding, and model updating to construct a lightweight and adaptive DT capable of simulating the deformation behavior of an existing tunnel. The proposed framework provides practical guidance for adjacent tunnel design by enabling efficient full-field prediction of deformation and supporting risk-informed engineering decision- making. Key innovations of the proposed framework include: (1) Spatial and non-spatial information are obtained from a para - metric FE model, where simulation outputs are converted into structured inputs for the surrogate model. Including spatial fea - tures, compared to counterparts using only non-spatial data, en - hances the ability to capture spatial positional relationships and improves prediction accuracy. (2) A surrogate model, i.e., Dual-Fusion Swin-Table Transformer Network (DF-STTN), is developed to integrate spatial and tabular modalities. It employs a two-stage structure to separately predict ground settlement and lining deformation, improving interpret - ability and accuracy. Spatial and non-spatial features are extracted using a Swin Transformer and TabTransformer, then fused via dual-stage fusion (early and late) for effective multi - modal interaction. (3) A data-driven model updating method is used to iteratively best estimate soil parameters by minimizing prediction errors against X. Chang et al. Methodology 2.1. Overview of the method This study proposes a DT framework for tunnel behavior prediction and evaluation. The DT framework is structured in four layers (see Fig. 1 ): (1) Physical system layer, where a scaled tunnel test system represents the real-world twin; (2) Data acquisition layer, which collects measurements such as settlement, lining deformation, and acceleration; (3) Digital modeling and data fusion layers, where numerical simula - tions and multimodal features are used to train the surrogate model (DF- STTN) and iteratively update soil parameters; and (4) Service and interaction layer, which enables prediction, assessment, and test setup adjustments. Firstly, the physical twin is established using a laboratory-scale testbed designed to replicate tunnel excavation scenarios. The system includes a physical tunnel model, a data acquisition system, and a sensor network that captures tunnel responses including settlement, lining deformation, strain, soil pressure, and acceleration. These experimental measurements form the test dataset, which is used both for model validation and for updating the virtual model. It should be clarified that, due to the data availability and setup flexibilities, a laboratory-scale setup is adopted in this study to establish the DT framework. In particular, compared to the real tunnel physical twin, the designed system offers more flexibilities of setup configura - tions, e.g., tunnel diameters and inter-tunnel distances, and provides more comprehensive data collection schemes in the lab. Model updating and analysis To enhance the reliability of the baseline surrogate model, a model updating framework was established based on back analysis. In this study, soil parameters were chosen as the focus of the update process because they have a significant impact on ground deformation and are often highly uncertain in practice. Due to the limited availability of test data, the back analysis was conducted on a subset of specific monitoring points, as shown in Fig. 6 . GA was employed as the optimization method due to its global search capabilities and robustness against local minima. Other parameters, such as design variables (e.g., tunnel geometry and tunnel location) and spatial information, were considered known and kept constant during the optimization process to isolate the influence of soil properties. The GA settings used in this study included a population size of 50, a crossover rate of 0.8, a mutation rate of 0.1, and a maximum of 100 generations. These parameters were selected based on empirical testing to balance convergence speed and global search performance. The fitness function was defined as MSE between the predicted and observed settlement at selected monitoring points. As shown in Fig. 12 , both the best and mean fitness values rapidly decreased over generations, demonstrating effective convergence of the GA. The best fitness value stabilized after approximately 20 generations, indicating that the model successfully identified a set of soil parameters yielding high predictive accuracy. The resulting predictions and corresponding true values are summarized in Fig. 12 . The best individual identified by the GA corre - sponds to an MSE of 0.9319. The predicted values closely match the measured settlements at all selected positions, validating the effective - ness of the model updating procedure. This approach allows for the refinement of soil parameters in data-scarce conditions, thereby improving the fidelity of numerical simulations based on the trained DL model. To evaluate the reliability of the back-analyzed soil parameters, the estimated values were compared with laboratory test results for the sand used in the experiment. The main parameters of soil properties are summarized in Table 9 . The estimated parameters were derived through model updating using the surrogate model and field measurements. Building renovation promotes circular design by extending the structural lifespan of a building and minimizing resource consumption. However, pollution created by building renovation activities often poses a hazard to human health and the proximate environment. Among the pollutants, particulate matter (PM) is widely recognized as the most critical (Yan et al. 2023 ), alongside gaseous contaminants (Nazif et al. 2025 ), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and harmful chemicals (Ismaeel et al. 2023 ). Collectively, these impacts contribute to unacceptable indoor environmental quality (IEQ) concerns. Environmental laws of many countries regulate the installation of pollution monitoring systems on construction sites to detect and report pollutant exposure for building occupants and surrounding com - munities. Yet traditional environmental measures like proper ventilation, gap sealing, and noise barriers cannot adequately control the impact of construction pollution. Traditional hierarchical stakeholder relationship structures cause decision-making bottlenecks, leading to delays and poor decision-making (Pan and Zhang 2021 ). Building information modelling (BIM) has emerged as a pivotal enabler to mitigate these issues by enhancing direct stakeholder communication and empowering workers to make informed decisions (Wijeratne et al. 2024 ). When strategically integrated and combined with real-time data streams from Internet of Things (IoT) devices, organized and managed within a Common Data Environment (CDE), the BIM model evolves into a foundation for a BIM-enhanced Digital Twin (DT) platform that pro - motes dynamic, bidirectional interaction between physical and virtual systems (Revolti et al. 2024 ). Despite its potential, establishing a BIM-enhanced DT for pollution management in building renova - tion projects remains technically challenging due to fragmented building data, heterogeneous informa - tion sources, and complex synchronization requirements (Eneyew et al. 2022 ; Pauwels et al. 2024 ). It requires a rigorous alignment with international standards (e.g. ISO 19650), accurate calibration and deployment of IoT sensors, consistent data structures for real-time exchange, and seamless interoper - ability across heterogeneous digital platforms (Madubuike et al. 2022 ). The synchronization of dynamic sensor data with BIM-enhanced semantic databases introduces substantial complexity in ensuring model fidelity, data granularity, and contextual interpretation (Oliveira et al. 2024 ). Moreover, information management in the CDE remains persistent challenges such as information overload and insufficient data curation in the CDE (Du et al. 2020 ), which are exacerbated when pro - ject owners or facility managers fail to identify and manage their specific information needs (Werbrouck et al. 2019 ). To achieve this, project owners must carefully identify relevant BIM data aligned with their objectives by establishing clear information requirements for data production and storage, ensuring an efficient information flow (Oliveira et al. 2024 ). Exchange information require - ments (EIR), under ISO 19650, outlines necessary information, timing, and format for the information delivery among the parties; clarifies stakeholders’ roles; minimizes ambiguity, and standardizes data exchange protocols (Montoya and Roberto 2022 ). These are supported by the Level of Information Need (LOIN) and the Level of Development (LOD) framework defined in the BIM Execution Plan (BEP), which ensure consistent geometric and semantic information delivery aligns closely with the pre - defined metadata structures necessary for DT development (Brumana et al. 2022 ). Because advanced DT applications require highly specific alphanumeric data and documentation rather than just detailed 3D geometry, a systematic strategy LOIN concept enables project teams to define the precise information requirements for the DT without over-modelling the geometry (El Sibaii et al. 2025 ). Nevertheless, there is still a lack of formally defined BIM modelling and metadata struc - tures in the CDE’s information containers that enable the systematic integration of static BIM datasets with real-time IoT data for DT applications in building renovation projects. The conceptual framework for LOIN, which directly defines virtual models of a BIM-enhanced DT remains underexplored. To fill this research gap, we propose a BIM-enhanced DT architecture tailored for managing con - struction pollution during building renovation projects. Defining the LOIN is the first and essential step toward developing a fit-for-purpose BIM model for a BIM-enhanced DT. The proposed architec - ture identifies essential information required for structuring BIM models that can process both static and dynamic data. We explore the relationships among the five dimensions in the CDE of a BIM-enhanced DT framework in building renovation. To support the analysis, we conducted a compre - hensive literature review and semi-structured interviews to examine the evolution from conventional construction practices to BIM-enhanced DT systems. These findings helped us delineate the architecture of the BIM-enhanced DT and the LOIN frame - work, which were subsequently implemented in an auditorium renovation project as a proof of concept. 2 T.-A. The LOIN provides stakeholders with an understanding of information flow and collaboration workflow for pollution monitoring and man - agement during renovation. This approach equips asset owners and contractors with actionable insights to implement BIM-enhanced DT solutions, which can safeguard neighbouring communities, mitigate health risks, and improve safety for construction workers during building renovation. Background BIM-enhanced DT in construction pollution monitoring Low-cost sensor networks (LCSNs) and low-cost IoT sensors (LCSs) were increasingly prominent in environmental sciences for their capacity to produce extensive real-time monitoring data (Silva et al. 2026 ). However, conventional dashboard solutions often cannot represent spatial relationships or pin - point the exact locations of monitored hotspots. Operating independently from advanced CDEs and construction workflows, these systems offered limited spatial context and data integration (Zhou and El-Gohary 2021 ; Nadeem et al. 2023 ). Although the integration of IoT and geographic information sys - tems (GIS) significantly enhanced construction monitoring through real-time location tracking and spa - tial visualization (Liu et al. 2025 ; Valinejadshoubi et al. 2025 ), these technologies often operated in silos and lacked seamless integration with construction-specific digital ecosystems. Recent studies have advanced IoT-BIM integration for real-time data acquisition on construction sites and automated systems for data transmission and storage through interactive DT platforms (Boltsi et al. 2025 ; Valinejadshoubi et al. 2025 ). Modular IEQ sensing approaches have also been developed, including DT-based KPI-driven smart building platforms (Piras et al. 2025 ), IoT-ML-BIM-integrated DT frameworks for indoor air quality (IAQ) monitoring (Qian et al. 2024 ), and HBIM-XR-enabled DT platform for IEQ analysis in heritage building (Banfi et al. 2025 ). Despite these advances in sensing infrastructures and digital monitoring concepts, methodologies for aligning these platforms with regula - tory frameworks and BIM systems remain underdeveloped. Managing environmental pollution in renovation sites calls for a cost-effective, portable, and easily deployable solution, which can adapt to changing spatial arrangements and unpredictable pollution sources (Francisco et al. 2020 ). Most current research on BIM and DT enablers focused on new con - struction projects (Lu et al. 2020 ; Qian et al. 2024 ). Other studies relied on proprietary hardware and complex deployment processes (Seabra et al. 2021 ; Hong et al. 2022 ), which were impractical for the transient nature of renovation projects. A DT for construction was defined through the harmonious integration of static data from BIM models and dynamic data from cognitive technologies into a CDE (Sacks et al. 2020 ). While defining LOIN facilitates this physical-to-digital data exchange (Liu et al. 2021 ; Godager et al. 2022 ), excessive information requirements can burden project management. Despite prior studies having examined scan-to-BIM processes and defined BIM information requirements for specific use cases (Banfi et al. 2022 ; Nguyen et al. 2023 ), limited research has explicitly specified how such BIM models should be structured for DT implementation in the CDE. Although previous studies clarified the flows of static and dynamic information within CDE solutions and linked them to stakeholders’ roles in the CDE workflow (Pham et al. 2025 ), a systematic process for developing and managing different information categories in the architectural framework remains missing. From this perspective, the value of a clearly structured BIM-enhanced DT architecture supported by well-defined LOIN specifications is evident. Such an approach ensures the consistent integration of BIM, IoT, and CDE workflows, while improving alignment between project services and stakeholder needs. From non-BIM to BIM ecosystems BIM offers a structured data environment that ensures consistency between graphical and non- graphical information throughout the project life cycle, thereby reducing information fragmentation and mitigating data exchange inefficiencies (BSI 2020 ; Ali et al. 2021 ). F: OPEN Scientific Reports | (2026) 16:27367 1| https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58063-3 Multi-line LiDAR 3D construction environment modeling and BIM consistency update method for digital twins Chen Liu1, Xin Xu1, Xiaofeng Ding1 & Guangmei He2 Construction digital twins aim to maintain an up-to-date, BIM-consistent as-built state from streaming multi-line LiDAR to enable progress verification, dimensional compliance control, and proactive safety management on construction sites. However, converting sequential LiDAR observations into a temporally stable, element-centric BIM state is difficult in realistic AEC environments. Existing approaches mainly fall into geometry-driven Scan-to-BIM registration frameworks and learning-based point cloud perception models, with some system-level digital-twin frameworks built on BIM-centric reasoning. Purely geometric alignment is brittle under occlusion, clutter, and repetitive structures, and registration uncertainty can propagate to downstream progress and deviation conclusions. Meanwhile, generic perception networks are often trained on static frames, lack temporal stabilization, and do not explicitly optimize BIM-consistent element association or geometry-aware alignment cues. In addition, many digital-twin systems expose limited, loosely coupled state representations that hinder causal, reliable site updates. To address these gaps, we propose LiBiDT (LiDAR–BIM Digital Twin), a causal LiDAR–BIM digital-twin model—where “causal” refers to computational online updating in which only past observations enter the current state—that integrates spatio-temporal mapping, geometric– semantic perception, and scan-to-BIM association for consistent state evolution. LiBiDT consists of Spatio-Temporal Global Registration (STGR) with LiDAR–IMU–GNSS priors, Multi-task Perception (MTP) using a PointNet++ backbone with semantic/instance/normal heads, and Scan-to-BIM Association and State Updating (S2B) via normal-guided point-to-plane refinement, bipartite matching with fused overlap/deviation/semantic evidence, and exponential moving average (EMA) stabilization. The Decision and Risk Analytics (DRA) layer turns the BIM-aligned state into auditable construction- management indicators—weighted completion, deviation summaries, and three forward-evaluable safety/compliance hazard flags. Experiments on three public benchmarks (Hilti SLAM, CV4AEC 2024 Scan-to-BIM, and S3DIS Area-5) cover trajectory, perception, and association evaluation. On CV4AEC 2024 Scan-to-BIM, LiBiDT reaches association F1 = 0.837 and Chamfer Distance (CD) / surface Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 3.2/3.5 cm; relative to the perception-replacement baseline (PointNet++ + S2B, F1 = 0.758) the gain is large, while relative to the strongest competing baseline—CoFiNet (Reg) + S2B at F1 = 0.822—the absolute association gain narrows to 0.015 F1 points, with the bigger gains coming from temporal identity consistency (IDF1 0.854 → 0.861) and ID-switch reduction. A held-out tile-stream case study visualises BIM-element state evolution, alignment refinement, EMA-stabilised evidence streams, and hazard-flag firing as the scan progressively covers the floor; a strict temporal validation under active construction will require time-stamped multi-acquisition data and is left to future work. Ablation results confirm that evidence stabilization and multi-cue matching contribute complementary gains in accuracy and temporal consistency, yielding a favorable efficiency–accuracy trade-off. Building Information Modeling (BIM) provides a design-time, element-centric representation, but the as-built scene evolves continuously and often deviates from the planned model. Construction digital twins aim to close this loop by fusing sensing data with BIM to maintain an up-to-date, operationally interpretable state of the site1–3. Among available sensing modalities, multi-line LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) offers dense 3D geometry with robust depth measurements, enabling frequent capture of complex construction scenes; however, turning streaming LiDAR into a temporally consistent, BIM-aligned, element-level state remains challenging due to clutter and occlusion, repetitive structures, coordinate-frame drift, heterogeneous supervision for perception, and the need for causal, on-site operation. A first family of approaches is geometry-driven Scan-to-BIM alignment and model-based comparison, typically centered on coarse-to-fine registration and handcrafted geometric cues. Representative Scan-to-BIM frameworks exploit planar primitives or congruent sets to initialize the alignment and then refine it 4. While effective in controlled settings, these methods are brittle under partial observations, non-model objects, and symmetries that induce ambiguous alignments; moreover, uncertainty in geometric matching can propagate to downstream “progress/quality” conclusions when the digital twin is built from imperfect registrations 4. More broadly, systematic reviews note that fully automated, robust Scan-to-BIM remains difficult in realistic Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (AEC) scenarios, especially when the environment is incomplete, cluttered, or only partially scanned5. A second family focuses on learning-based point cloud understanding. Deep networks for 3D segmentation and feature learning have significantly advanced geometric–semantic perception 6–8. Construction-oriented studies further demonstrate that learned scene understanding can support Scan-to-BIM automation and downstream reconstruction9. Nonetheless, these models are commonly trained on static frames and do not, by themselves, enforce temporally stable predictions across a LiDAR stream, nor do they guarantee BIM-consistent element associations and geometry-aware alignment cues needed by Scan-vs-BIM workflows. In addition, instance-level consistency and robust clustering remain non-trivial in cluttered scenes even with discriminative embedding objectives10. A third family concerns digital-twin systems and construction management analytics (progress, quality, and safety) built on BIM-centric reasoning. Prior work highlights the promise of digital twins for cyber-physical integration but also emphasizes interoperability, real-time updating, and reliable data-to-model synchronization as persistent gaps 2,3. On the safety side, BIM-based hazard identification and prevention can encode design- time priors and rules, yet its operational effectiveness hinges on accurate as-built localization of hazards and protections, which is difficult without a robust, continuously updated scan-to-BIM state 11. Consequently, construction-management indicators often remain loosely coupled to the sensing and alignment stack, limiting reliability when scene observations are noisy, intermittent, or partially missing. In summary, existing models provide valuable components, but they do not fully deliver a causal, BIM- consistent digital-twin update that tightly couples spatio-temporal mapping, geometric–semantic perception, element-level association, and construction-management indicators from LiDAR streams. The main problems with these methods are as follows: • Fragmented sensing-to-BIM state updating : Many approaches stop at per-scan registration or per-frame segmentation, without an explicit element-level digital-twin state and a causal update mechanism that is ro- bust to occlusion and intermittent observations2,3. • Brittle scan-to-BIM association under real site conditions: Purely geometric alignment and matching can be ambiguous in symmetric, cluttered, and partially observed scenes, and uncertainty in scan-vs-BIM geom- etry can distort downstream conclusions4. • Limited integrated, geometry-aware state for site management : Many systems do not expose a unified, BIM-aligned geometric–semantic state that can be updated online and used consistently across progress, dimensional-compliance, and deviation monitoring11. To address these limitations, we propose LiBiDT (LiDAR–BIM Digital Twin), a four-stage digital-twin model that performs Spatio-Temporal Global Registration (STGR), Multi-task Perception (MTP), Scan-to-BIM Association and State Updating (S2B), and Decision and Risk Analytics (DRA) in a strict causal cascade. The key idea is to treat globally registered LiDAR as a scene-level backbone, attach geometric–semantic predictions to it, and update BIM element states through robust association and temporally filtered evidence, producing an auditable interface of progress and deviation indicators6,12. Throughout the paper, “causal” refers strictly to computational online updating—only past observations enter the current state—rather than structural causality between physical processes; the term is preferred over “online” or “incremental” because it makes the no-look-ahead constraint explicit at the level of the system equations (Eqs. (6)–(10)). The main contributions of our model are as follows: • Causal digital-twin state and windowed spatio-temporal mapping: LiBiDT formalizes an element-centric twin state and updates it causally from a fixed-length LiDAR observation window, leveraging tightly coupled LiDAR–IMU smoothing and mapping to provide a stable scene backbone for downstream tasks2,12. • Geometry–semantic perception coupled with robust scan-to-BIM association : LiBiDT combines mul - ti-task point cloud perception with geometry-aware alignment refinement and bipartite matching using bounded overlap/deviation cues, improving robustness in cluttered and partially observed construction scenes4,6. • Unified BIM-aligned indicators with auditable safety/compliance hazards : LiBiDT derives weighted completion, deviation summaries, and three forward-evaluable hazard flags (compliance breach, clearance Scientific Reports | (2026) 16:27367 3| https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58063-3 breach, status hazard) directly from the updated BIM-aligned state, providing a single auditable interface for progress, dimensional-compliance, and safety monitoring whose thresholds can be re-tuned by site managers without retraining the perception or association stack. • End-to-end visualisation of the digital-twin pipeline : a held-out tile-stream case study (Sec. 4.5) visual- ises BIM-element state evolution, scan-to-BIM associations, alignment refinement, EMA-stabilised evidence streams, and hazard firing as the scan progressively covers the floor, complementing the numerical evalua - tion; a strict temporal validation under active construction requires time-stamped multi-acquisition data and is left to future work. The novelty of LiBiDT is not in re-inventing the individual components (LiDAR–IMU SLAM, PointNet++ perception, point-to-plane ICP , bipartite matching, EMA filtering)—each of which is well established in its respective sub-field—but in introducing (i) a single causal digital-twin state vector S(t) that explicitly carries per-element evidence and discrete element status as part of the network output, (ii) a forward-evaluable safety/ compliance hazard layer (Sec. 4.5) whose thresholds can be re-tuned by site managers without retraining, and (iii) an end-to-end end-to-end visualisation pipeline that ties the abstract’ s three operational goals (BIM- consistency, progress, safety) back to observable outputs on a held-out floor. These three elements are either absent or only loosely specified in prior digital-twin frameworks. Related work Construction digital twins and BIM-centric site monitoring Digital twins have evolved from BIM-centric information management toward cyber–physical representations that fuse design intent with continuously updated as-built observations. Early construction digital-twin research emphasized semantic interoperability, data fusion across heterogeneous sources, and lifecycle integration to support decision-making1,2. Recent surveys further consolidate the view that practical construction twins require (i) reliable sensing-to-model alignment, (ii) scalable state representations, and (iii) robust update mechanisms under incomplete and noisy observations 3. However, many existing digital-twin frameworks remain system- level blueprints, where the online update is either loosely specified or relies on manual/heuristic associations between sensed data and BIM elements 1,3. This gap motivates digital-twin models that explicitly formalize the causal update from spatio-temporally consistent sensing, to geometric–semantic perception, to element-level state evolution and monitoring indicators. LiDAR-based spatio-temporal mapping and point-cloud perception A prerequisite for any scan-driven construction twin is a globally consistent scene backbone. LiDAR odometry and mapping methods, ranging from lightweight LOAM variants to tightly coupled LiDAR–inertial smoothing and mapping, provide accurate ego-motion estimation and drift reduction under rapid motion and sparse features12,13. Y et, construction scenes are highly dynamic (equipment, staged materials, partial structures), which can degrade mapping stability and complicate downstream BIM alignment if the perception stage is not jointly considered. On top of registered point clouds, modern 3D perception increasingly relies on deep point-cloud networks rather than hand-crafted geometry descriptors. Hierarchical set abstraction models (e.g., PointNet++) learn multi-scale local geometry, while large-scale segmentation architectures prioritize efficiency and robustness under non-uniform density and massive scenes6. Convolutional kernels defined directly on point neighborhoods (e.g., KPConv) further improve geometric fidelity for outdoor/structural scenes 7. Despite strong recognition performance, generic 3D perception models are typically trained for semantic tasks alone, assume static scenes, and do not explicitly optimize for downstream scan-to-BIM alignment and element-level state updating. Moreover, instance-level grouping often requires additional objectives; discriminative embedding losses provide a practical route but can be brittle under sparse or partial instance supervision10. These limitations suggest that construction twins benefit from multi-task perception designed to output alignment-relevant cues (e.g., normals and instance clusters) together with semantics. Scan-to-BIM registration, association, and construction analytics Scan-to-BIM research has long studied how to align and interpret as-built point clouds with as-designed BIM geometry. Classical workflows combine global registration and local refinement: global alignment via correspondence sampling or invariant sets14, followed by iterative closest point (ICP) variants for fine alignment, and sometimes fast global registration to reduce dependence on initialization 15. Comprehensive reviews highlight persistent challenges: occlusion, clutter, varying completeness across time, and semantic ambiguity when multiple BIM elements share similar geometry 16. Beyond alignment, construction monitoring methods map scans to BIM to infer progress and deviations, typically through geometry-based matching and rule- based status assignment 17,18. Hyperparameter sensitivity and stability analysis We conduct a comprehensive sensitivity analysis to understand how temporal stabilization and multi-cue fusion affect the robustness of the Scan-to-BIM digital twin state (Fig. 10). Regarding the temporal stabilization, the EMA smoothing factor ρ in Eq. (37) governs the trade-off between system responsiveness and stability. W e vary ρ from 0.1 to 0.9 and observe two distinct failure modes at the extremes. Low smoothing values ( ρ< 0.5) make the element evidence excessively sensitive to transient noise and partial occlusions, resulting in state flickering and a sharp increase in Identity Switches (IDSW). Conversely, overly high values (ρ> 0.7) introduce significant inertia, causing a ’ ghosting’ effect where the updated state lags behind physical reality; this latency prevents the system from promptly transitioning elements from ‘Undetected’ to ‘Detected’ , thereby degrading Recall. Limitations and sensitivity considerations For balance and to guide further research, we acknowledge five limitations of the current evaluation and outline how each will be addressed. (L1) GNSS-dropout sensitivity is not quantified. Our STGR component augments LIO-SAM with GNSS priors on Hilti SLAM, where the dataset provides GNSS messages, and is evaluated only there. We have not yet quantified how the trajectory and downstream BIM-aligned indicators degrade under partial or full GNSS dropout (typical for indoor construction stages or shielded sites). Conceptually, the LiDAR–IMU residual term in Eq. (14) continues to provide a state estimate without GNSS, but the absolute drift can be larger; an explicit GNSS-dropout ablation is required before the system is deployed on outdoor-to-indoor transitions. (L2) Sensitivity to BIM-modelling errors. All deviation, completion, and hazard outputs are evaluated against the supplied BIM elements. If the as-designed BIM contains positional errors (mis-placed walls), missing elements, or coarse abstractions of complex furniture/MEP , the S2B associations will inherit those errors and the DRA outputs may flag them as deviations. The current paper does not characterise this propagation; a controlled BIM-perturbation study (random per-element shifts and class swaps) is a necessary follow-up to provide actionable error budgets to site managers. (L3) Forward-evaluable hazards rather than learned safety models. The three hazard rules of Sec. 4.5 are deliberately simple and auditable; they are not learned safety classifiers. From an engineering infor - matics perspective, a core challenge is achieving semantic interopera - bility between Building Information Modeling (BIM) representations and real-time sensing streams so that computational models and routing algorithms can adapt continuously to evolving indoor conditions. Emergency evacuation provides a particularly demanding context: route feasibility depends jointly on building topology, hazard dynamics, and crowd behavior, and must be recomputed under strict temporal constraints. Mobile Crowd Sensing (MCS) has emerged as a scalable paradigm for collecting occupant-level information during emergencies. By exploiting the sensing and communication capabilities of smartphones and wear - able devices, MCS enables decentralized acquisition of location and contextual data and supports dynamic situational awareness [1 – 4] . It enables information-rich modeling of indoor spaces and supports advanced analysis for design, construction, and facility operations. Background Emergency evacuation systems are essential for safeguarding human life during crises such as fires, structural failures, and natural disasters. However, conventional evacuation strategies are predominantly static and exhibit limited responsiveness to real-time conditions. They often fail to accommodate evolving hazards or human behavior, which can result in congestion, confusion, and delayed evacuations [25] . Inade - quate situational awareness, stemming from insufficient real-time data further, hampers the ability of emergency managers to make informed decisions [26,27] . Although numerous technological interventions have been explored, a significant disconnect remains between research efforts and their application in operational building environments [28,29] . This section reviews the current body of literature in three main areas: BIM- enhanced emergency evacuation systems, mobile crowd sensing and crowdsourced systems, and integrated BIM–MCS approaches. 2.1. Bim-enhanced emergency evacuation systems BIM has emerged as a vital tool in emergency management due to its ability to provide semantically rich and geometrically accurate digital models of the built environment [30] . Recent work also explores BIM integration with emerging technol - ogies such as Bluetooth [12] , VR/AR [35–39] , and digital twins [6,7] , demonstrating its potential for improving evacuation awareness and interactivity. Kanangkaew et al. [40] developed a real-time BIM–AR fire evacuation system, while Ahn et al. [38] demonstrated AR navigation for indoor emergencies. Semantic coupling of BIM and MCS A key contribution of this work is the operational alignment between IFC-based building semantics and real-time crowd-sensed data. Con - ventional BIM-based evacuation models typically rely on precomputed routes or static hazard assumptions, whereas the proposed framework converts the IFC building model into a dynamic graph structure whose nodes and edges are continuously updated using live MCS inputs. This transformation enables indoor spaces, circulation elements, and exits to function as computational entities rather than passive geometric representations. Through semantic mapping, occupant locations and environmental conditions are bound directly to IFC entities, allowing spatial reasoning and route computation to operate within a building-aware context. This mechanism establishes a persistent link between the physical environ - ment and its digital representation and conforms to the core digital twin requirement of state synchronization between physical and virtual systems. 5.2. Routing behavior under multi-criteria optimization The evacuation routing formulation extends conventional shortest- path methods by introducing a multi-criteria cost function that ac - counts for proximity to hazard, travel time, exit availability, and route safety. Unlike distance-only routing strategies, this design treats evac - uation as a dynamic optimization problem governed by both spatial and environmental constraints. The incorporation of a route safety index allows environmental data to influence routing decisions directly. Smoke intensity and crowd density are not handled as secondary indicators but are embedded as control variables within the routing computation. The Analytical Hier - archy Process (AHP) provides a structured approach to weighting parameter selection and reduces subjectivity in cost formulation. Observed improvements in routing stability indicate that perfor - mance gains result from structured multi-criterion integration rather than from dominance of any single factor. Sensitivity analysis confirms that the algorithm adapts to varying hazard profiles without requiring manual retuning, which supports its applicability across multiple emergency contexts. 5.3. Implications for digital twin systems in emergency management From a digital twin perspective, the proposed system demonstrates how BIM models can evolve from static design artifacts into real-time operational platforms. The integration of live occupancy and environ - mental sensing enables continuous state updating and route re- computation, forming a closed-loop system between sensing, modeling, and decision execution. This approach supports the development of smart building systems capable of active intervention rather than passive monitoring. The framework establishes an architectural pattern for BIM-centered digital twins consisting of semantic modeling, sensor integration, dynamic computation, and decision output. This pattern is transferable to other application domains, including energy control, facility automation, and resilience analysis. For emergency management, the system delivers prescriptive guid - ance rather than descriptive status updates. Instead of visualizing haz - ards alone, the system computes and distributes optimized evacuation routes in real time, contributing directly to operational decision-making. 5.4. Reliability and system behavior under constraints Evaluation results show that evacuation times exhibit lower variance under restrictive conditions. In scenarios with limited exits, routing convergence was more stable due to reduced solution paths. This behavior suggests that constraint-limited environments may exhibit higher algorithmic determinism, leading to predictable outcomes during navigation. The two-stage crowdsensing localization strategy contributed to maintaining situational awareness even when part of the user popula - tion was unable to transmit data. From a system reliability standpoint, this indicates resilience against partial data loss and supports continued operation under degraded communication conditions. While Wi-Fi–based localization accuracy remains bounded by envi - ronmental factors, the confinement of user positions to IFC-defined spaces mitigates uncertainty by constraining feasible locations to architecturally valid zones. 5.5. Limitations and future implications Several limitations warrant attention. The case study involved a single mid-sized academic building, and further validation across diverse building types is required. Although the experimental validation was conducted in a single academic building, the architecture is inde - pendent of building type and relies exclusively on IFC and standard wireless protocols, enabling direct transferability. Wi-Fi localization error remains non-negligible and should be addressed through hybrid positioning strategies incorporating BLE, inertial sensors, or Ultra- Wideband (UWB). Ethical and privacy challenges associated with mobile crowd sensing must also be acknowledged. Real-time occupant monitoring requires strict data governance mechanisms including anonymization, encryp - tion, and transparency of data usage policies. These considerations are central to sustainable deployment of digital twin technologies in occu - pied environments. In buildings with highly articulated or irregular circulation geometries, increased spatial complexity primarily results in a larger number of spatial partitions and transitions, yielding a denser evacuation graph rather than requiring methodological changes. Future work will focus on extending the framework through pre - dictive hazard modeling, sensor fusion strategies, and privacy- preserving data architectures. These developments are necessary to support scalable digital twin deployment in highly occupied and infrastructure-intensive facilities. 6. Conclusion This paper presented an IFC-based integration framework that tightly couples Building Information Modeling (BIM) with mobile crowd sensing (MCS) to enable dynamic, context-aware evacuation routing in complex buildings. By transforming IFC models into a semantically enriched decision graph continuously updated with crowd-sensed in - formation, the proposed approach moves beyond static BIM-based evacuation models toward operational, real-time decision support. The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows. First, a semantic interoperability mechanism was developed to system - atically link IFC building entities with live sensing data, enabling dy - namic annotation of spaces, exits, and sensors. Second, a graph-based evacuation model was formulated in which routing decisions adapt to evolving hazards, crowd conditions, and exit availability using a multi- criteria shortest-path strategy. Third, a two-stage crowdsensing approach was incorporated to estimate occupant positions by account - ing for both active and silent users. Finally, the framework was imple - mented and evaluated in a real multi-story academic building under multiple emergency scenarios, demonstrating improved evacuation effectiveness under scenario-specific temporal constraints. Compared to existing BIM-based evacuation approaches, which often rely on static assumptions or precomputed routes, the proposed frame - work emphasizes continuous data integration and adaptive routing behavior. This positions the framework closer to digital-twin–like operation, where building models are not only descriptive but also actively support real-time operational decision-making during emergencies. Introduction With the continuous development of the socioeconomic, the total volume of road transportation is increasingly being climbed, and the issue of overloading vehicles is becoming more prominent (Gomez and Vassallo, 2015; Li et al., 2020; Lv et al., 2014). In addition to design and construction defects, improper operation and maintenance, as well as material performance deterioration and many other factors, a large number of bridges in service are in a state of “working with disease,” which poses serious safety haz - ards (Feng et al., 2023). The increasingly critical situation of the operational safety of bridges has led the international community to recognize the importance of adopting more systematic and tar - geted technical measures during the service life of bridges in order to respond more effectively to the growing challenges of bridge safety (Han et al ., 2018 ; Jeon et al., 2023; Somodikova et al., 2016 ; Zacchei et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2020). Therefore, the safe service of the existing bridges will be the main direction of bridge engineering in the future, and at the same time, it is urgent to vigo- rously develop new bridge intelligent operation and maintenance technologies such as intelligent detection, monitoring, evaluation, and maintenance. At present, researchers at domestic and overseas have established bridge construction monitoring and operational maintenance systems, which have initially realized the digital collection and management of data at various stages. Wu et al. (201 1) combined GNSS-RTK (global navigation satellite system real-time kine- matic) and GIS to develop a set of cable hoisting construction safety monitoring system, which can carry out real-time position- ing of cable hoisting tanks with high accuracy and assist com- mand. Cheng (2021) developed a steel bridge construction assembly simulation system based on Unity3D, which is able to guide the process of construction based on the progress informa- tion or component location. Sun et al. (2020) established an auto- matic wireless monitoring system by means of a sensor system, data acquisition and communication system, transmission system, and monitoring center. Chen and Zhang (2020) built a multichan- nel monitoring system of vibrating string strain sensors to solve the problems existing in traditional data collection and transmis- sion. Mei et al. (2020) introduced PTDMS (pressure-transmitter- based communicating pipe deflection monitoring system), which is commonly used in bridge health monitoring, into the deflection monitoring of the main girder of Yangzi River Bridge during the construction period from the perspective of whole-life monitoring. Given this context, digital twin technology emerges as a promising digital solution for bridge structural monitoring and risk analysis. Bridge digital twin model construction and simultaneous simulation 3.1 Bridge digital twin model construction To establish a bridge digital twin system platform, it is essential to construct a digital bridge model to provide virtual digital assets that serve as the central carrier for data integration, presentation, and interaction. This study utilizes BIM and 3D modeling soft - ware to initially create the digital bridge model based on funda- mental bridge design data. The established model encompasses the main structure of the bridge, deck slabs, piers, abutments, and other components. The modeling logic for the primary bridge structures and components is as follows: (1) Overall modeling sequence: a) bridge deck system; b) superstructure; c) substruc - ture; (2) Modeling sequence of bridge deck system parts: a) bridge deck pavement, expansion joints; b) parapets, crash walls; c) light- ing, signs; (3) Modeling sequence of superstructure parts: a) main beam; b) hinge joints, wet joints; c) beams, diaphragms; d) bear- ings; (4) Modeling sequence of substructure parts: a) cover beams, table caps; b) pier body or columns, platform and other abutment appurtenances; c) pier foundations, bearing platforms and pile foundations. After completing the creation of all structure and component mod- els, web-BIM model conversion technology is used to convert the bridge and its associated models into a standard 3D model format (e.g. .glb, .gltf) and model asset files (e.g. JSON/XML data files). This process enables the separation of the model from its numeri - cal data and ensures lightweight processing. Based on this, a web- based 3D model rendering framework (e.g. three.js, babyon.js) is utilized to load and display the bridge model on the web.
