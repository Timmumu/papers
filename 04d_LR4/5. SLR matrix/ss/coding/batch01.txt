This paper introduces the Right-time Analysis and Mitigation (RAM) module as part of the Digital Twin for Construction Safety (DTCS) concept. RAM (a) leverages safety rule-based compliance checking of construction layout plans in Building In - formation Modeling (called safeBIM) and (b) records and analyzes resource trajectory data involved in potential struck-by incidents using wearable and mobile Real-Time Kinematic Global Navigation Satellite Systems (RTK- GNSS) technology. RAM further (c) provides new access to unprecedented information and gives detailed in - sights into human-machine interactions that (d) permit rapid implementation of change by targeted control. Results from real-life validations create an in-depth understanding of the potential root causes that often lead to harmful struck-by incidents. While the proposed DTCS technology contributes data input and information output, the change in safety management processes leads to safer work practices. Both prevent SIF that plague the construction sector. 1. Introduction Construction sites are widely recognized as some of the most haz - ardous workplaces, with higher rates of fatalities and injuries compared to any other industrial sector. In the European Union, for example, construction is responsible for over one-fifth of all workplace fatalities, with up to 15 % resulting from incidents where workers are struck by equipment or other objects [ 1 ]. Similarly, in the United States, the close proximity of pedestrian workers — those who are on foot at off-road worksites working alongside vehicles and equipment — to equipment or its components increases the risk of struck-by injuries, accounting for 48 % fatal and 20 % nonfatal injuries in construction sites [ 2 , 3 ]. The inherent safety risks on construction sites arise from the simultaneous execution of multiple tasks and the presence of essential resources within the constrained workspaces, such as partially completed struc - tures, temporary installations of supportive equipment, and cluttered areas filled with building materials. In tasks involving heavy equipment, workers are particularly at risk of being run over, crushed, or struck by moving equipment or falling objects. Unless controlled, many of these risks can evolve into serious injuries and fatalities (SIF). The potential root causes and control methods of many SIF are well studied. Consequentially, as one of many examples around the world, the German Workplace Ordinance [ 4 ] sets (minimum) requirements for employee safety and health protection. Such a law primarily intends to establish and operate safe workplaces in the construction sector. It re - quires employers to identify and assess the risks associated with work tasks and design hazards out before work starts. While various measures exist to mitigate risks upon detection, such as installing barricades that clearly separate pedestrian workers from onsite vehicle traffic, safety plans are still predominantly generated manually and are rarely updated along dynamic construction schedules. Likewise, safety responsibilities are often outsourced to consultants managing multiple projects, making it difficult to frequently update safety plans. Often, construction site safety supervision is left to site managers and superintendents. Unless a construction site reaches a decent size to warrant dedicated safety personnel on site, safety tasks stay within broad field monitoring. Furthermore, unplanned work or changes in activities often lead to non- compliant safety practices, necessitating continuous monitoring. Traditional safety management tools rely heavily on manual methods, such as identifying hazard zones on printed layouts and con - ducting manual site observations [ 5 ]. Nevertheless, labor-intensive methods often lack sufficient frequency and accuracy to keep up with the actual site condition. All these observed challenges highlight the need for improved tools that integrate safety planning and continuous moni - toring of workforce safety status, enabling the automation of hazard assessment and dynamic modification of worksite layout plans. The emergence of advanced technologies as part of digitalizing construction safety processes has significantly transformed safety man - agement tools. For instance, Building Information Modeling (BIM) method has enhanced the geometric and semantic representation of construction schedules, enabling more precise planning. BIM has facil - itated the adoption of the Prevention through Design and Planning (PtD/ P) concept by enabling the proactive identification and elimination of hazards during the early stages of construction planning. Recent ad - vancements include preemptive hazard zone identification algorithms that automate the creation of a digital construction safety plan [ 6 – 9 ]. Sensing technology has also advanced, enabling continuous and accu - rate monitoring of safety status. Despite varying location errors, Real- Time Location Tracking Systems (RTLS), such as Ultra-Wideband (UWB) and Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS), allow resource trajectory monitoring in outdoor construction environments in runtime [ 10 , 11 ]. The integration of magnetic-field technology on construction equipment and wearable radio-frequency tags on pedestrian workers enhances situational awareness around construction equipment [ 12 ]. Such advancement in technologies can enable the detection of incidents where workers enter hazard zones, providing the involved operator and worker/s with critical real-time alerts. These alerts help prevent injuries or equipment damage. Additionally, incident data logging and analysis have demonstrated value for safety managers and trainers, supporting the development of virtual training environments as part of serious gaming [ 13 – 18 ]. However, a research gap remains in the method of holistically inte - grating the output from these technologies, resulting in incomplete hazard identification and incident detection. (Semi-)Static hazard zones — temporary zones with hazards that are not mitigated effectively or immediately and are often pre-identified on the site layout plan — and dynamic hazard zones - areas surrounding moving and operating equipment that constantly change in location and size along with con - struction activities - cannot be effectively merged for incident detection. This limitation significantly impedes the ability of safety personnel to accurately assess the on-site safety situation. Even with the availability of digital safe site layout plans, merely visualizing RTLS data on a simplified or outdated site layout model in BIM would still require sig - nificant human effort to detect or monitor deviations over time before a safety responsible person could react upon worthwhile findings [ 5 , 19 ]. Recent studies towards this direction have proposed a Digital Twin for Construction Safety (DFCS) as a method to enhance safety engi - neering and management processes throughout the planning and execution phases [ 7 ]. DTCS not only automates data flows between physical construction sites and their digital counterparts but also en - ables the analysis, prediction, and prevention of next or future hazard - ous incidents. Additionally, DTCS serves as a comprehensive platform and user interface for exchanging data, information, and knowledge related to safe site layout planning, monitoring, and control [ 7 ]. As such, a digital twin that integrates models and resource trajectories has the great potential to make construction safety more efficient, proactive, and adaptive. This paper focuses on the process of the automation and integration of incident detection, assessment, and proactive mitigation within the DTCS framework. The process is achieved by connecting the modules of safety layout planning and sensor-based tracking, utilizing statistical methods to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of hazards and in - cidents, and providing site layout adaption recommendations, which are eventually integrated into the site model. Additionally, this study eval - uates the applicability and performance of Real-Time Kinematic-Global Navigation Satellite System (RTK-GNSS), a GNSS-based tracking solu - tion with improved accuracy and reliability [ 20 , 21 ], for construction resource location tracking in live outdoor construction work environments. In summary, this study aims to address the following research questions (RQ): • RQ1: Is RTK-GNSS a viable and accurate location tracking technol - ogy for resource tracking in live outdoor construction work environments? • RQ2: How can digital worksite layout plans and location tracking technologies be integrated to enhance safety planning, monitoring, and incident prevention? • RQ3: What level of detail is required to identify and understand the root causes contributing to too-close proximity or struck-by equip - ment incidents? • RQ4: Accordingly, what are suitable mitigative measures for the different types of incidents, and how can these measures be effec - tively communicated and implemented in the DTCS? This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the related work in BIM-based safety planning, sensing technologies for resource location tracking, and the most recent works on digital twins with respect to construction safety. Section 3 presents the proposed framework and the developed computational methods. Section 4 details the field experi - mental setups and presents results from two realistic case study imple - mentations. Lastly, the method ’ s strengths and limitations are reviewed and discussed, followed by future research directions. 2. Related work This section presents an overview of existing researches that focus on digital site layout planning, real-time location sensing, and digital twin applications for construction safety. 2.1. From manual to digital safety site layout planning BIM has made significant contributions to the digitalization of con - struction management processes, incorporating multiple domains crit - ical to construction projects, such as design, cost, and scheduling. However, the integration of safety into BIM in the project design and planning phases, which would enable Health, Safety, and Environment (HSE) professionals to eliminate or reduce hazards at the earliest possible moment or more frequently, has only recently begun to gain recognition [ 9 ]. So far, digital safety planning using BIM methods aligns with the existing PtD/P strategy, which proactively identifies and eliminates hazards during the early design stages of a project, continuing up until the start of execution [ 8 ]. While PtD/P is still applied iteratively in the operations phase, it primarily involves the identification and mitigation of known yet uncontrolled hazards before they escalate active risks [ 22 ]. In the context of safe construction equipment operations, Teizer and Cheng [ 23 ] classified the hazard types, while Johansen et al. [ 9 ] further expanded this classification, distinguishing between semi-static and dynamic hazard zones. Traditionally, PtD/P requires substantial manual effort from the architectural and engineering disciplines [ 24 ]. For instance, in con - ventional risk assessment, the safety responsible must manually identify unprotected leading edges and floor openings on printed construction drawings to determine where fall protection equipment is required. As project sizes grow, this task becomes increasingly labor-intensive and prone to errors [ 25 ]. Even with the adoption of BIM models, a safety responsible knowledgeable in BIM must examine three-dimensional (3D) building information models and schedules to identify hazards embedded in the spatial-temporal context of the design intent. To assist this process, automated hazard identification has been proposed, uti - lizing BIM object information (e.g., geometry, spatial-temporal re - lationships, and associated work tasks) to reduce manual efforts and detect hazard zones that might otherwise be overlooked [ 6 , 26 ]. Zhang et al. [ 6 ] first came up with a rule-checking algorithm to K. Hong and J. Further ad - vancements were made by Johansen et al. With the creation of PtD/P knowledge bases and safety ontologies, which encapsulate safety re - quirements and best practices, rule-based algorithms have demonstrated significant adaptability to various hazard types and safety regulations [ 29 ]. These safety rule-checking algorithms evaluate construction schedules against safety regulations and recommend mitigation mea - sures, enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of safety planning. 2.2. Real-time resource location tracking Real-time location tracking using sensor-based systems is an essen - tial method for monitoring the locations of workers, equipment, and other resources on construction sites. The research community has extensively tested various location tracking systems to evaluate their effectiveness on construction sites. Cheng et al. [ 30 ] assessed UWB systems for real-time tracking of mobile resources in challenging construction environments and performed an - alyses of worksite operations through case studies. Their findings demonstrated UWB’s applicability for the design of construction man - agement tools. Costin and Teizer [ 31 ] used Radio Frequency Identifi - cation (RFID) to monitor worker locations in indoor environments, visualizing these locations within BIM models. Park et al. [ 5 ] presented a safety monitoring framework that integrates Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)-based location tracking technology with BIM. For outdoor tracking in construction sites, Global Positioning Sys - tems (GPS) have been widely applied due to their simplicity and wide coverage [ 32 ]. Zhang et al. [ 33 ] utilized GPS data loggers attached to workers’ hardhats to monitor their movements during the construction of cast-in-place concrete columns. RTK-GNSS implementation An RTK-GNSS system [ 62 ], based on a Long Range (LoRa) network [ 63 ], was deployed to monitor the movement of workers and equipment throughout a three-day-long field experimental period. The system, sourced from simpleRTK2B, consisted of a base station and multiple rovers. As shown in Fig. 4 , the base station was positioned on top of a two-story office container, while rovers were embedded into existing pockets on pedestrian workers ’ safety vests. For mobile equipment, rover antennas were mounted through existing strong magnets outside and on top of the equipment cabin, centered close to the equipment operator ’ s seat. All relevant dimensions and equipment specifications were recorded. Location data collection and streaming began once the base station was fully operational and the rovers started receiving correction signals. Workers ’ locations were recorded at a frequency of 1 Hz, the system ’ s default setting, while equipment locations were recorded at 5 Hz to better capture motion. All data were stored locally on smartphones connected to the rovers. During data collection, a precision test was conducted for the RTK- GNSS system at the implementation site. According to the RTK board and device manufacturer ’ s specifications, the system supports three tracking modes: RTK Fix, RTK Float, and Single [ 64 ]. RTK Fix offers centimeter-level precision, RTK Float provides accuracy within 0.5 to 1 m, and Single mode has the lowest precision, with accuracy ranging from 1 to 5 m. To assess the system ’ s precision, a rover was placed at a clear, un - obstructed location in the staging area, and its position was recorded continuously for three hours. The system operated in RTK Fix mode, and the deviation from the mean position was used to evaluate precision. Approximately 10,800 location points were collected. The 50th percentile precision was 1.0 cm, and the precision at the 95th percentile reached 2.3 cm. Fig. 5 presents a visualization of the precision results, demonstrating the RTK-GNSS system ’ s suitability for high-accuracy tracking in field applications. Hazard zone identification A site layout model of the staging area was created using commer - cially available BIM software for manual modeling objects and geome - tries. Self-taken photographs and satellite images provided valuable input to designate zones for the identified work area, such as existing building structures, rail tracks, temporary material storage areas, and office containers. Based on this site layout model, the researchers, guided by responsible safety engineers, developed a safe baseline model to delineate distinct movement zones for the pedestrian workers and equipment. All of this work was done independently from the RTK-GNSS data collection and analysis to avoid potential bias. As shown in Fig. 6 , pedestrian worker walkways, mobile equipment driveways, and rail track zones were color-coded within the model. Given that the experimental site was confined to a single ground level, the coordinates were simplified to two dimensions (2D) for clarity and ease of use. In the baseline model, concrete barriers were recommended for installation along the boundaries of the pedestrian walkways to protect them from nearby moving equipment. These barriers were intended to create a physical separation between workers and vehicles, ensuring that pedestrian workers remained within designated safe zones and reducing the likelihood of inadvertent entry into equipment driveways. However, due to operational requirements, full separation of pedestrian walkways and equipment driveways could not be achieved. Specifically, vehicles moving across these (traversable) zones were necessary to allow equipment, such as telehandlers and wheeled loaders, to access key areas for material transportation to and from the railcars. These had to be loaded and unloaded with new or recyclable material, respectively. This inability to fully separate worker and equipment zones resulted in the declaration of one of the research objectives: the identification of these intersections as semi-static hazard zones. Dynamic hazards were observed during the experiments, particularly due to the movement and operation of a telehandler tasked with transporting materials between storage zones and railcars. The tele - handler ’ s continuous operation created a hazardous environment for nearby pedestrian workers. Yet, they often needed to guide the tele - handler during material handling tasks, such as positioning the forks into the handles of the ballast bags. This close coordination required workers to stand in very close proximity to the telehandler, often within its 1-m boundary and several blind spaces. The combination of workers standing near the equipment and the operator ’ s limited visibility in these critical zones significantly increased the risk of struck-by incidents or close calls. Section 6 concludes with limitations and future research directions. 2 Related work 2.1 Digital twin and sensing for construction safety Construction sites are dynamic, hazard-rich environments, making them a natural target for DT-enabled monitoring. Prior work has linked IoT and DT platforms to track the state and location of workers in real time via RFID/BLE infrastructures [64]. Wearable DT systems using IMUs have captured posture and movement and delivered immediate feedback (e.g., via AR) to reduce unsafe kinematics [ 4]. DT-driven behavioral simulations have been used to study how workers respond to safety interventions and to inform training design [ 17]. Closed-loop DT frameworks [ 47] that integrate machine learning and case-based reasoning have been explored for proactive safety control during live projects. Task-specific DT applications, such as for crane/ hoisting operations, have modeled spatiotemporal risk and visualized evolving hazards on site [37]. Together, these ini- tiatives show that DTs have great potential for safety man - agement; however, many systems target narrow use cases, operate at limited scale, or rely on centralized processing that raises privacy and latency concerns in outdoor, multi- worker contexts. Our study complements this line of work by focusing on on-device safety-index prediction under fed- erated training, and by proposing an FL-HPO mechanism that respects privacy, bandwidth, and heterogeneity con - straints common to construction deployments. 2.2 Classic hyperparameter optimization techniques in centralized settings HPO is indispensable for system enhancement when objec- tive functions are non-analytical, non-convex, or non-dif - ferentiable. It involves tuning hyperparameters, set prior to training, that significantly impact model performance. In centralized learning, common HPO techniques include grid search [ 5], random search (RS) [ 18], Bayesian Opti - mization (BO) [ 55], and evolutionary optimization [ 29]. These methods, while effective, rely on iterative trial-and- error processes to strike a balance between exploration and exploitation, making them computationally expensive. Model-free approaches, such as grid search and RS, explore predefined configurations without relying on parameter models, offering simplicity but often with lower efficiency. In contrast, model-based methods like BO leverage surro - gate models to approximate the objective function, resulting in more efficient search processes [48]. The primary distinc- tions among these approaches lie in the choice of surrogate models and their strategies for selecting subsequent evalua- tion points [22, 36]. Evolutionary algorithms (EAs), includ- ing differential EAs [2, 52], iteratively refine populations to achieve optimal performance. The high computational costs associated with training deep neural networks on large datasets make full-fidelity BO evaluations impractical. In AutoML, comparative studies have simplified the assessment of HPO methods and improved reproducibility [ 20]. Tools like HPO-B [9] facilitate the evaluation of transfer-HPO tech - niques, while HPOBench [15] addresses the need for multi- fidelity HPO benchmarks, offering a robust framework for method comparison. Although FL is becoming important in improving worker safety in outdoor construction situations, most HPO benchmarks center on centralized models, there- fore ignoring the challenges of distributed settings. 2.3 Hyperparameter optimization in federated learning At a high level, prior FL-HPO work falls into four buckets: (A) NAS/weight sharing (e.g., FedEx and derivatives), (B) adaptive/learning-based HPO (RL/meta-learning, per-client tuning, clustering/offloading such as FedCust), (C) single- shot/server-side selection (e.g., FLoRa), and (D) other decentralized or proxy schemes. While centralized machine learning has extensively explored HPO, FL presents unique challenges for HPO due to the decentralized nature of the data. The limitations of conventional HPO approaches in FL settings stem from the need for more access to the complete dataset and the high communication and training costs associated with training numerous models for various hyper-parameter configurations. One-shot local Bayesian Optimization is used to identify optimal hyperparameters for each client using a fraction of the dataset. Initially, FL clients determine and transmit their optimal hyperparameters to a central server, which uses a weighted consolidation strategy to cre- ate and distribute a global hyperparameter set. This method improves model performance and convergence, ensuring scalable federated learning deployments in worker safety- critical applications. Across different datasets, client distri - butions, and evaluation metrics (accuracy for classification and MAE for regression), the weighted hyperparameter consolidation method demonstrates an overall improvement of approximately 6.44% across all datasets and client counts (increased accuracy for classification; decreased MAE for regression, each normalized and averaged) compared to the simple averaging approach. Despite the significance of HPO in FL, there remains ample opportunity for further explo - ration and application across diverse domains. Our study, which mainly focused on worker safety data, underscores the importance of addressing these challenges to maximize the benefits of FLHPO in real-world scenarios. The proposed mechanism enhances FL by optimizing hyperparameters through a weighted consolidation strategy, improving model performance and scalability in worker safety applications. However, privacy risks arising from exchanging unprotected hyperparameters highlight the need for approaches that safeguard information while enabling collaborative tuning. Additionally, addressing communica - tion overhead in large-scale networks and benchmarking against advanced HPO methods remain key areas for future research to maximize FLHPO’s real-world impact. Limitations and future work. Our evaluation involves up to 15 clients on Raspberry Pi–class devices; behavior at larger scales still needs investigation. The loss-weighted aggregation assumes honest, fairly calibrated client valida - tion losses, and although only hyperparameters (not model weights) are shared, some basic information leakage is pos- sible. We present results from a single seed per setting and focus on non-visual, tabular, and temporal signals. Vision tasks (e.g., PPE detection) and additional baselines beyond Simple-A VG and a server single-shot proxy are planned for future work. Moving forward, we will explore larger client populations (such as partial participation and lightweight multi-fidelity search), improve robustness against noisy or misreported losses (e.g., with robust weighting and secure aggregation), and combine the method with lightweight vision models. The framework is designed to support the development and utilization of simulation components by an interdisciplinary team of researchers and developers. Integrating existing simulator systems, each with its own user interfaces, simulation control, and communica- tion facilities poses a major challenge. The framework is designed to be scalable and flexible, possessing the following key characteristics: (i) simplicity of use and extensibility, (ii) capability for the development of complex simulation scenarios, and (iii) support for the execution of such scenarios on local machines, cloud-based infrastructures, or hybrid environments. The initial considerations have been based on existing approaches such as serverless (stateful functions), event- driven, and service-based architectures. A central objective is to completely separate application and platform function- ality. Core data processing functionality is to be separated from the following aspects. • Control flow: simulators are required to surrender, for instance, time control facilities, so that they could be controlled from the outside. • Data flow: simulators are to relinquish control of data exchange to facilitate their seamless integration into flex- ible data processing pipelines. • Communication technology: a simulator’s algorithmic component needs to be separated from any integrated communication mechanisms, to facilitate its deployment in diverse runtime environments. • Deployment specifics: a simulator’s functionality needs to enable cross-platform execution, allowing it to run on various environments, including local machines, cloud services, and hybrid configurations. 2.2 Framework components The framework comprises four major types of components: (i) processing units, (ii) services, (iii) applications, and (iv) orchestrator(s). Processing units implement simulation algorithms or other data processing tasks as passive and independent building blocks providing a common Application Program- ming Interface (API) to facility controlling their execution from the outside. Processing units can be written in diverse programming languages. Services encapsulate processing units and provide life cycle management and inter-process communication inter - faces. They offer mechanisms for creating unit instances, initializing them for a simulation run, and invoking their functions to apply external data, execute subsequent steps, or query their state. This enables running multiple simulation algorithms in a coordinated manner in distributed environ- ments and allows processing units to participate in multiple concurrent simulations for various users, objectives, and parameter sets. Applications describe simulation scenarios. In a typi- cal co-simulation scenario, services are interdepend- ent, i.e., the output of one service serves as the input of another. Orchestrators accept application descriptions and initiate and execute corresponding simulation runs. Studying simulations under varying conditions enables a better understanding and forecasting of the crisis situation. In the present research, an approach is developed that enables the site- and building-specific quantification of flood damages and building vulnerabilities. This is accomplished using categorizable, generalizable, and scalable physical damage models tailored for extensive urban structures but applied at the building level. A key aspect of the solution is the development of generic building models that reflect typi- cal characteristics related to structure, materials, and con- struction methods, thus eliminating the need for specific data and calculations for each individual building. This facili- tates a more efficient assessment of flood impacts on urban environments. Vulnerability is assessed for a representative building drawn of a portfolio of structures with similar char- acteristics. The urban mixed-use pattern is categorized into various buildings classes, such as typical 19th/20th century masonry residential buildings or reinforced concrete admin- istrative structures built after 1945 (Vetter et al. ( 2024)). Variability in geometry, material strength, and other relevant parameters within each class is incorporated into the analy- sis through probabilistic distributions (Neuhaeuser et al. (2024)). The assessment framework comprises three sequential modules presented in figure 5. The first module utilizes open geodata to categorize buildings and develop geomet- ric models, while literature research addresses data gaps. A parametric modeling approach is employed for simula- tion-based probabilistic analyses, starting with automated identification of building types and the generation of para- metric base models which are then modified for structural analyses (Vetter et al. (2024)). The second module prepares structural models for probabilistic analyses and conducts the analyses (Neuhaeuser et al. (2024)). The module allows for various loading scenarios, including standard loads and those resulting from hazardous events, such as flooding, by varying flood height and flow velocity. Structural models representing specific building classes are derived by con- sidering uncertainty and adjusting input parameters accord- ing to statistical distributions. The third module collects and evaluates results to derive fragility curves, describing the probability of reaching or exceeding certain damage levels based on intensity measures (Neuhaeuser et al. (2024)). All developed software tools are fully integrated, enabling auto- matic execution of modules for seamless integration into the co-simulation framework. This semi-automated procedure facilitates the development of fragility curves for assessing building vulnerability in urban contexts, as exemplified in Rosin et al. (2024). To integrate into the co-simulation framework, georef- erenced classifications of buildings and respective fragil- ity curves for the building types are submitted. The latter are then overlaid with water levels within the co-simulation framework. As a result, the damage probability is deter - mined spatially and event-dependently for each building type using the corresponding fragility curve. 3.1.7 Integration of situation data via spontaneous volunteers The here-presented co-simulation requires external input data: the state of the simulated infrastructure at the begin- ning of the simulation serves as the initial conditions and further information at later times can be used to update and recalibrate the co-simulation. Architecture of the decision support system The architecture is presented in blocks with detailed information. Decision-making based on events requires an architecture (Fig. 4) con- sisting of two modules. The ﬁrst module is a “small digital twin” of a single station that is intended for the operator. The operator monitors safety at the subway station. Information for the operator is obtained from the digital twin. The digital twin analyzes incoming and previous information, and an event is generated for the operator. In Module 1 of the digital twin the situation at various stations is assessed. When new data is obtained a digital twin is rebuilt and an event is generated on the digital twin. All these steps are interconnected. Then, the event history is analyzed, and the best response to the event is determined. All these steps are inseparably linked. Module 2 houses a “large digital twin” of the entire subway. The server processes data generating summary information on the events. The quality of the decision-making process depends on interconnected modules 1-2, exchanging information on the events and decisions made in response to them. Computational costs are minimal, since all the most complex operations are processed on the fog server. The proposed architecture is distinguished by deep detailed pro- cessing of occurring events in real-time in combination with general processing of events that occur across all subway stations. Decision- making is based on the information received from monitored and at other neighboring stations. The architecture of the system is modular and block-based. Modules are decomposed into individual blocks and interconnections are deﬁned for the blocks. Overall, the architecture is designed to generate well founded decisions and ultimately respond to events. 5.3. However, the multi-level architecture of BIM+GIS relies heavily on the integration of heterogeneous data from multiple sources. In actual construction environments, data collection errors and model update delays can easily lead to deviations between 3D scenes and real working conditions. At the same time, the fine-grained risk discrimination rule library does not fully cover the risk types in complex dynamic scenes, and some unconventional or coupled risks are difficult to accurately identify, resulting in high false alarms or omissions and reducing the overall perception accuracy. Fan proposed a safety risk per ception method for the entire construction process based on the multi class Adab oost algorithm (Fan, 2025). The K-means clustering algorithm is used to pre classify the original construction data to determine the feature indicators for safety risk perception. Subsequently, the Adaboost ensemble learning strategy was integrated with the BP neural network to construct a multi class Adaboost algorithm. This algorithm trains multiple BP neural networks in parallel as base classifiers and combines them into multiple strong classifiers. The introduction of positive and negative ideal solutions of TOPSIS method makes the results of factor ranking more objective and interpretable.
