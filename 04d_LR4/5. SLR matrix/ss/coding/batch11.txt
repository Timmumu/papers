Subsequently, a methodology for extracting one-dimensional geometric models from point cloud data is described. The proposed safety analysis method is then applied to the retractable roof of the Shenzhen Gymnasium, with a comparison made between two approaches: the LiDAR-based method and the consistent imperfection mode method, in terms of their consideration of the impact of initial geometric imperfections on structural mechanical properties. The research findings reveal that the one-dimensional geometric models extracted from airborne LiDAR measurements can accurately reflect the real-world structural imperfections and can be regarded as geometric digital twins of the structures. In comparison with the consistent imperfection mode method, finite element models based on airborne LiDAR measurement technology offer a superior representation of the real performance of the structures. 1. Introduction The use of large-span spatial structures in large-scale public buildings is increasing, due to the advantages of light weight, excellent mechanical performance, a variety of structural forms, innovative shapes and substantial available space. Examples of such structures include the Shenzhen Gymnasium, Shenzhen Financial and Cultural Centre, Shenzhen Natural History Museum and Shenzhen Reform and Opening Exhibition Hall, which are currently under construction and are about to start construction. However, the large-span spatial structure mostly adopts steel structure, which is susceptible to instability and failure of components or structures. The initial defects of the components during the production and transportation processes, the asymmetrical docking of components, and the deviation of joints caused by the on-site installation process can exacerbate the instability and failure of the steel structure. Consequently, the impact of initial geometric imperfections must be considered during the stability analysis of large-span spatial structures. * Corresponding author. Regarding the influence of initial geometric imperfections on the stability performance of large-span spatial structures, Ma et al. [ 1 ] investigated the effect of different defect shapes and amplitudes on the stability bearing capacity of a stadium roof structure by considering the first three buckling modes and their combinations as the defect distribution. Du [ 2 ] investigated the effect of different defect modes on the overall stability of a roof structure by considering the first six buckling modes as the defect distribution. Wang [ 3 ] investigated the overall stability performance of a portal frame structure system by taking the first elastic buckling mode as the defect distribution. Jiang et al. [ 4 ] investigated the influence of different defect sizes on the overall stability performance of a retractable roof structure using the first buckling mode as the defect distribution. Díaz et al. [ 5 ] investigated the overall stability performance of large-span metal roofs by considering the first mode of buckling analysis as the defect distribution. Liu et al. [ 6 ] proposed a random defect mode superposition method for the stability analysis of single layer reticulated shell structures. This method identifies the most unfavourable defect mode for structural instability. Xu et al. [ 7 ] introduced a reliability-based method for calculating the critical stability load-bearing capacity of single-layer reticulated shell structures and validated its effectiveness through two case studies. Borri et al. [ 8 ] used the random defect mode method to study the influence of the distribution pattern and size of initial geometric im - perfections on the structural stability bearing capacity. Chen et al. [ 9 ] proposed a modelling method for initial geometric imperfections in reticulated shell structures based on random field theory and used it to investigate the effect of these imperfections on the structural stability performance. Furthermore, Cui et al. [ 10 ] proposed a simultaneous autoregressive model capable of simulating spatially varying initial geometric imperfections, including those in irregular reticulated shell structures. As can be seen from the above studies, the main methods currently used to account for initial geometric imperfections are the random defect mode method and the consistent defect mode method. However, it should be noted that the defect modes assumed in these methods are random, whereas in constructed structures the defect mode is deterministic. Therefore, when performing a safety analysis on an existing structure, calculations should be based on the actual defect shape. Digital twins, which serve as digital replicas of real-world assets, can effectively reflect the actual state of structures, including stresses, deformations and geometric imperfections. Many scholars have used three-dimensional (3D) laser scanning technology and reverse modelling techniques to capture geometric imperfections of structures and create geometric digital twins. Sangani et al. [ 11 ] used 3D digital image correlation equipment to scan tubular steel columns with initial geometric defects, and evaluated the residual capacity of tubular steel columns according to the scanned model. Chacon et al. [ 12 ] measured the initial geometric defects of planar steel frames using a terrestrial laser scanner. Selvaraj [ 13 ] and Zhang [ 14 ] demonstrated that 3D laser scanning can be used to measure the initial geometric defects of steel members. Xu et al. [ 15 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to detect the full-field geometric defects of circular steel pipes and investigated the influence of geometric defects on the compressive and flexural strength of circular steel pipes. Tzortzinis et al. [ 16 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to measure the corrosion of bridges and combined 3D laser scanning with finite element to evaluate the residual capacity of steel beams. Ban et al. [ 17 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to measure the size and geometric defects of square hollow section short columns, and investigated the local buckling behaviour of square hollow section short columns. Lazzari et al. [ 18 ] proposed a method for measuring geometric defects and dimensional deviations in GFRP profiles using a 3D contact coordinate measuring machine. Feng et al. [ 19 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to obtain and evaluate initial geometric defects such as prestressed CFRP reinforced columns. Zou et al. [ 20 ] attempted to use 3D laser scanning technology to obtain the initial geometric defects of steel structural components, and proposed a method to build a digital model of the components based on 3D laser point cloud data. Xu et al. [ 21 ] proposed a method to determine the initial geometric defects of steel members and establish a geometric model by using 3D scanning, and proved that this method can better predict the initial geometric defects of steel members. Aktepe et al. [ 22 , 23 ] used a 3D laser scanner to measure the geometric defects of cold-formed thin-walled steel in detail to propose a predictive expression for the initial geometric defects. The experimental results show that the finite element results based on the scanning model are in good agreement with the experimental results. Ma et al. The above studies demonstrate that 3D laser scanning technology can accurately capture the true initial geometric imperfections of components and create geometric digital twins of structures. However, most current research uses terrestrial 3D laser scanners to acquire point cloud data of structures, and the geometric digital twins produced are mostly in the form of 3D solid models. Terrestrial 3D laser scanners face challenges in measuring spatial structures with large spans, and it is not economically feasible to use 3D solid models for computational analysis of such structures. Therefore, in order to accurately obtain the real geometric defects of the large-span spatial structure and reduce the computational effort of the finite element model, this paper proposes a method to obtain the real geometric defects of the structure by using the airborne LiDAR measurement technology. At the same time, the method of extracting a one-dimensional geometric model from point cloud data is described. Taking the movable roof of Shenzhen Gymnasium as an example, a finite element model based on LiDAR is established, and the difference of finite element results under different initial geometric defect modes is explored. Engineering overview The Shenzhen Gymnasium has a total of 6 floors, with a total floor area of 57,600 square metres and a building height of 45.00 m. It consists of a steel roof structure and a lower grandstand. The gymnasium is shown in Fig. 2 . The lower grandstand adopts the reinforced concrete frame-shear wall structural system, and the lower grandstand is the supporting structure of the roof steel structure canopy. The roof steel structure canopy is a radial retractable roof composed of one fixed roof and two movable roofs. Each movable roof is supported by eight trolleys above the fixed roof. The fixed roof and the movable roof are mainly composed of orthogonal plane truss system, where the span of the movable roof is 65 m and the width is 25 m. Fig. 2. Schematic diagram of gymnasium. Airborne LiDAR measurement principle and equipment introduction 3.1. Measurement principle Airborne LiDAR measurement system is an active measurement system composed of aircraft, dynamic differential global posi - tioning system, inertial navigation system and laser rangefinder. It takes the aircraft as the carrying platform of the whole mea - surement system, uses the dynamic differential global positioning system to record the coordinate position of the aircraft, uses the inertial navigation system to record the navigation parameters such as the attitude, speed and position of the aircraft, and uses the laser ranging system to measure the distance and orientation between the aircraft and the measurement object. The principle of determining the coordinates of the target by the airborne LiDAR system is shown in Fig. 3 . The points O and P on the map are the position of the aircraft and the position of the target, respectively. The position coordinates O ( x 0 , y 0 , z 0 ) of the aircraft are recorded in real time by the dynamic differential global positioning system. The pitch angle σ , roll angle ω and yaw angle k of the aircraft are recorded in real time by the inertial navigation system. The distance R and azimuth β between the aircraft and the measurement point are recorded in real time by the laser rangefinder. Safety analysis by FEM To explore the differences between point cloud model and theoretical model, and to compare the differences between the two methods (airborne LiDAR method and consistent defect mode method) in considering the impact of initial geometric defects on structural performance, this paper established theoretical model, LiDAR model, and consistent defect model, respectively. The three finite element models are shown in Fig. 11 . Among them, the theoretical model is the model used in structural design; the LiDAR model is a model that modifies the coordinate values of some nodes according to the LiDAR scanning results; the consistent defect model refers to a model where the defect size is 1/300 of the span of the movable roof, and the defect form is the first buckling mode under gravity. 5.1. Finite element modelling method When the finite element model is established, the movable roof adopts BEAM189 element with a length of 1 m. All the members are connected by common nodes, and the translational displacement of the node XYZ in three directions at the trolley position is con - strained. Q355B steel is used in the movable roof, and the steel in the finite element is bilinear elastic-plastic constitutive. The material parameters are shown in Table 2 . During the analysis, the node load is applied on the nodes on the top of the roof, and the bearing capacity of the movable roof is determined by gradually increasing the size of the node load. 5.2. To ensure safe excavation in LDSS projects, this paper proposed a digital twin (DT) platform integrated with knowledge-data driven method to achieve prediction and control of tunnel face stability (TFS) during LDSS construction. The DT platform enables acquisition of physical data and expert knowledge, facilitating bidirectional synchronization between physical entities and virtual counterparts. DT platform utilizes Bayesian Optimization (BO), Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), driven by physical data, together with expert knowledge, to achieve knowledge-data driven prediction and control of Tunnel Face Stability (TFS) during LDSS construction. A case study of Wuhan Metro Line 12 construction demonstrates the platform's effectiveness, with key findings revealing: (1) The DT platform achieves accurate TFS prediction across six Geological types, showing average R 2 values of 0.935 and root mean square error (RMSE) of 0.239. (2) Key construction parameters are identified through the knowledge-data driven method, including air chamber pressure, grouting pressure, advance rate, cutterhead rotation speed, and cut - terhead torque. (3) The DT platform enables control of the TFS during LDSS construction by maintaining the slurry pressure (SP) within an optimal range. The DT platform developed in this study fills TFS research gaps in LDSS construction and provides valuable references for similar complex projects. 1. Introduction With the rapid advancement of tunneling engineering technology, large-diameter slurry shield (LDSS) technology has emerged and gained widespread adoption ( Chen et al., 2024 , 2025 ; Wu et al., 2023 ). it has been applied in significant projects such as the Slender West Lake Tunnel in Yangzhou ( Chenn kui, 2014 ) and the Sanyang Road Tunnel in Wuhan ( Wang, 2015 ). However, the diversity of the geological environments and the uncertainty of the construction scenarios have significantly increased the safety risks associated with LDSS operations ( Wu et al., 2024 ), particularly the precise prediction and control of tunnel face stability (TFS) ( Xu et al., 2021 ). As the diameter of the shield increases, the geological structures encountered by the tunnel become more complex ( Li et al., 2017 ; Yu et al., 2023 ), and the frequency of encountering multilayered strata and composite strata with varying mechanical properties ( Chen et al., 2025 ), such as intermixed soft and hard clay and sand, significantly increases ( Cong et al., 2023 ). These challenges not only markedly increase the complexity of the excavation environment but also greatly increase the difficulty and intricacy of maintaining TFS. Therefore, the TFS is crucial for ensuring the safe construction of an LDSS. Traditional research on TFS issues has been mostly centered around theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and experimental studies. This analysis highlights significant advancements over previous studies in the field but also notes certain limitations: (1) Theoretical analysis simplifies complex situations for easier un - derstanding and computation, but this may reduce prediction accuracy by omitting some influencing factors. (2) Numerical simulations need fine meshing and many iterations to meet high accuracy demands, which increases computational costs and makes large-scale simulations time-consuming, affecting their application efficiency in construction. (3) Experimental studies in complex environments require substan - tial investment in equipment and manpower, and they interrupt the construction process, leading to higher costs and safety risks. (4) Traditional data-driven methods can build predictive models using field data but lack interpretability and struggle with highly complex parameter interactions and dynamic changes. As a branch of data-driven methods, deep learning has been exten - sively utilized by many scholars owing to its powerful data processing and analysis capabilities, as well as its capacity to reveal and capture complex nonlinear relationships within data ( Liu et al., 2021 ; Phoon and Zhang, 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2020 ).。However, existing research indicates that the construction parameters in SM construction exhibit spatiotem - poral relationships; these relationships are difficult for conventional data-driven methods to capture and analyze, and their predictive per - formances are reduced ( Fu et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2024b ). Moreover, some existing studies mainly focus on the accurate prediction of TFS under traditional SM conditions; however, there is limited research on establishing suitable data-driven methods for precise prediction and control of TFS under LDSS conditions. Among the various data-driven methods, LSTM is commonly used for long time series data but can only predict future moments based on past information ( Li et al., 2019 ). This is insufficient for LDSS construction parameters, which may also depend on future moments ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Siami-Namini et al., 2019 ). To address this, BiLSTM, combining forward and backward LSTMs, is introduced to process LDSS tunnel construction data ( Graves and Schmidhuber, 2005 ). Additionally, TFS in LDSS construction is influenced by both past construction parameters and spatial relationships among parameters ( Fu et al., 2024 ). For example, different parameters are emphasized in soft soil and hard rock ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Zhao et al., 2019 ). To capture these spatial re - lationships, GCNs are introduced, representing data as graph structures and using the Laplacian matrix for convolution operations. During the development of data-driven method ( Wu et al., 2026 ), hyperparameter optimization is a critical preliminary step aimed at identifying the hyperparameter configurations that achieve optimal model performance during both the training and testing phases ( Xu et al., 2025 ). While traditional optimization methods, such as manual operation (MO), grid search (GS), and random search (RS), provide re - searchers flexibility in model tuning, they suffer from inefficiency, subjective bias, wasted computational resources, and inadequate opti - mization ( Victoria and Maragatham, 2021 ). In contrast, Bayesian opti - mization (BO) introduces a probabilistic acquisition function to efficiently explore the parameter space which significantly increases the likelihood of discovering the global optimum ( Chen et al., 2025 ). Therefore, to improve the prediction and control performance of data-driven methods, a hybrid BO-GCN-BiLSTM method is proposed. Additionally, to address the research gap in key construction pa - rameters for TFS during LDSS construction, this paper employs the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify and analyze these key parameters. By utilizing this method, it is possible to accurately deter - mine the construction parameters that significantly impact TFS ( Xu et al., 2026 ), thereby enabling precise control of TFS in LDSS construction. Data-driven methods can improve prediction and control perfor - mance but may lack reliability for practical engineering due to the absence of practical experience and expert knowledge ( Zhang et al., 2024a ). Therefore, a knowledge-data driven method integrating expert knowledge is more suitable for guiding TFS during LDSS construction. This approach can select input parameters and identify key influencing factors based on expert knowledge and literature ( Su et al., 2025 ). However, most existing research focuses on developing prediction and control algorithms, with limited efforts on creating a unified platform for integrating knowledge-driven and data-driven methods with phys - ical systems to enhance LDSS control and monitoring capabilities. Digital twin (DT) technology integrates construction site data with virtual dynamic models to create a digital simulation platform, showing superior capabilities in tunnel operation and management ( Boje et al., 2020 ; Yu et al., 2021 , 2023 ). Recent studies have applied the concept of DT to general shield tunnel excavation, enabling efficient remote interaction between physical and virtual entities by transforming physical equipment into digital models ( Pan and Zhang, 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2024b ). However, research on using DT platforms for data collection and TFS prediction and control in LDSS construction is limited. This paper proposes a DT platform that integrates data-driven methods (BO-GCN-BiLSTM and SHAP) with expert knowledge to form a knowledge-data driven method, enabling data acquisition and sup - porting TFS prediction and control in LDSS construction. In summary, the following issues motivate this paper: (1) how to analyze the spatiotemporal relationships among the various construc - tion parameters affecting the TFS during LDSS construction; (2) what construction parameters are the key factors affecting the TFS during LDSS construction; and (3) how to implement prediction and control of TFS in LDSS construction. Methodology To enable prediction and control of the TFS during LDSS construc - tion, a DT platform has been developed. This platform includes a virtual twin layer with a virtual LDSS model, a data-driven module, and a re - ality service layer. Specifically, the data-driven module integrates BO, GCN, and BiLSTM to capture the spatiotemporal relationships of shield construction parameters. Additionally, the reality service layer facili - tates prediction, SHAP for key construction parameters, and control of TFS during LDSS construction using the knowledge-data driven method. Fig. 1 illustrates the framework of the DT platform. 2.1. Virtual twin layer The establishment of the virtual twin layer is a core component of the DT platform. It achieves digital mapping and real-time interaction with the LDSS physical entity through high-fidelity modeling of the LDSS virtual model and multidimensional construction data fusion. This layer includes a data acquisition module and a virtual model. During the LDSS tunnel construction process, the digital twin (DT) platform utilizes two types of data sources: physical data and expert knowledge ( Li et al., 2025 ). The former consists of real-time and his - torical data collected from the construction site and the shield machine, including ring numbers, cutter head speed, cutter head torque, and tunneling speed; the latter primarily relies on experts who, based on their expert knowledge and relevant literature ( Chen et al., 2024 ), guide the selection of key parameters, thereby enabling knowledge-data-driven methods for TFS prediction and control during LDSS construction. These two data categories jointly support moni - toring, prediction, and control functionalities. Model interpretability analysis As a powerful tool, SHAP has gained attention for its ability to pro - vide interpretability of model predictions. Through this method, the reality service layer of the DT platform can not only identify the LDSS construction parameters that most significantly impact TFS but also understand how these parameters should be adjusted according to different geological types. This provides deeper insights into ensuring safety during LDSS construction. The SHAP results for each geological type are shown in Figs. 16 – 18 . As shown in Figs. 16 – 18 , the ranking of construction parameters significantly impacting TFS varies across different geological types. Specifically, the advance pressures A-E (x 13 -x 17 ) have a notably signif - icant impact on TFS in Geological Types 5 and 6. Particularly in Geological Type 5, construction parameters x 16 and x 13 both rank within the top five most influential construction parameters affecting TFS. Conversely, in Geological Types 1 and 2, the influence of these con - struction parameters is relatively minor, with lower rankings. Moreover, the in and out slurry pressure difference (x 6 ) exhibits higher influence rankings in Geological Types 1 and 3. However, in Geological Types 2, 4, 5, and 6, x 6 is identified as the most significant construction parameters impacting TFS. Meanwhile, some construction parameters maintain consistent rankings across all six geological types; for instance, Air cushion chamber pressure (x 1 ) consistently ranks within the top three in terms of its influence on TFS across all geological types, underscoring its consistent importance. Although SHAP can identify important construction parameters affecting TFS under different geological types, it is essential to distin - guish between active and passive parameters in LDSS construction. Active parameters can be directly adjusted by operators, while passive parameters, though identified as important, cannot be controlled. Additionally, some active parameters may rank lower but still require attention during operations. Therefore, based on SHAP and expert knowledge, the final selection of the top five parameters influencing TFS across various geological types are: x 1 , x 7 , x 9 , x 10 , and x 11 . The above analysis results also can be verified in similar studies in the past ( Chen et al., 2013 ; Shang et al., 2023 ; Weng et al., 2020 ). In this study, the interpretability of the BO-GCN-BiLSTM model is enhanced through the adoption of SHAP, revealing the complexity and geological dependency of how LDSS construction parameters influence TFS. This provides guidance for identifying key construction parameters affecting the entire well section. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpreta - tion of the data-driven method establishes a theoretical foundation for adaptive control strategies in LDSS operations under varying geological conditions. During construction, LDSS operators can leverage SHAP analysis results from the DT platform combined with integrated expert knowledge to adjust TFS control strategies specifically for different geological types, thereby achieving an adaptive closed-loop TFS control encompassing interpretation, decision-making, and execution. 4. This approach is being implemented within smart cities, focusing on intelligent circular resilience. The process begins in the physical world by recording data through strategically placed sensors distributed throughout the evaluated structures. Afterwards, the virtual world refers to the development of numerical models that capture the structural per- formance of the building while considering specific features to simulate the original one. The interconnection of both sides requires exhaustive and detailed cross-validation to ensure an established level of reliability, often based on the expected accuracy and limitations . Nevertheless, extending this process to heritage buildings de- mands a more sophisticated approach, designed to identify and classify discrete damage levels, and involves establishing proper threshold limits for an early warning/alarm system. This paper presents a methodology focused on the virtual implementation of these processes, aiming to integrate such technologies effectively. The case study is a religious monument that has been standing for over a century, located in a high seismic hazard zone in Lima, Peru. Numerical models were developed using 3DEC and SAP2000 software to capture the building ’ s dynamic response, including modal and time-history analyses for a set of 34 seismic ground motion records. The results were used to identify susceptible zones and the location of sensors . Further recommendations address the challenges encountered in implementing this approach and outline the next steps, including sensor deployment and validation against experimental models. 1. Introduction In modern engineering, the characterization and estimation of both the seismic response and the performance of structural and non- structural systems have evolved through the incorporation of innova- tive technologies. These tools allow users to capture and integrate all building components into a virtual model through numerical models, which are meant to approximate the behavior of structures and on un- certainties on the level and frequency of extreme loadings. However, these representations are constrained by the scope of work, the reli- ability required for each particular project, the level of detailing of the model, and the capabilities/limitations of the software for the simula- tion. In addition, access to specific building features related to geometry, material properties, damage reports from past events, and interventions plays a key role when determining the model ’ s adequacy and accuracy in properly capturing the seismic response of the construction. Never- theless, this practice has nowadays been widely standardized, to some extent, in national and international codes and standards for common practice [1,2] , under what has been cataloged as seismic vulnerability assessment for existing buildings. Special attention has been given to buildings classified as heritage buildings, which present a unique structural behavior when subjected to external loadings. In fact, many seismic vulnerability studies have demonstrated that, despite having similar features from a geometric and material standpoint, their seismic response cannot be grouped into a generic category [3 – 7] . As a result to overcome this challenge, a joint effort has been made by professionals and academia to outline guidance on how to address these kinds of challenges [6,10,11] , and the limitations that arise when characterizing construction materials due to restrictions on performing destructive tests [12 – 17] . Despite all these challenges faced by structural engineers in char- acterizing the seismic response of heritage buildings, major research projects have been developed worldwide to preserve their historical integrity [18,19] , leading to the development of alternative techniques integrating technologies that feed traditional numerical modelling. Nowadays, Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) is one of them, focused on capturing the building ’ s state by installing sensors to measure real-time parameters [20 – 24] . The sensor locations are based on expert judgement and results from Finite-Element Method (FEM) simulations, which focus on components highly susceptible to seismic actions [21, 25 – 27] . One of the main goals of SHM is the creation of systems capable of predicting damage levels. In this context, the digital revolution 4.0 offers a new solution called digital twin (DT) models [28 – 32] , meant to connect the real and virtual worlds by creating a digital model that represents the actual asset, while in the physical world, there are sensors placed strategically, streamlining the real-time flow of information [33 – 36] . At this point, the Intelligent Circular Resilience (ICR) is proposed as a novel methodological framework aimed at improving DRR management by extending the concept of dynamic circular seismic resilience through the integration of SHM, real-time data analysis, and active public-private participation. The framework operates across three stages — understanding seismic risk, implementing mitigation strategies, and responding to seismic events — within a continuous, circular process that identifies blind spots in risk perception, institutional coordination, and infrastructure planning. ICR is designed to function on three inter- dependent levels: institutional expertise, infrastructure risk manage- ment, and real-time monitoring, each supported by the adoption of emerging technologies toward the development of intelligent and resilient urban environments [43] . On one hand, this approach addresses the challenges and limitations encountered in heritage buildings by identifying critical features such as geometry configuration, material properties, and available historical records. On the other hand, it deals with the duality of the physical and virtual world through the selection and installation of sensor devices and the devel- opment of a trustworthy DT model for continuous feedback. This research delves into the Building Virtualization and partially on Data Gathering and Analysis from Sensors phases outlined in Fig. 1 (i.e., steps 02 and 03, respectively). The broad development of the methodology will be complete in further publications, incorporating experimental models and sensor deployment, covering phases 3 – 6. In this study, the DT is defined as a dynamic numerical representa- tion of the heritage building that replicates its behavior under seismic loading, continuously updated and cross-validated through real-time data from strategically placed sensors. This virtual replica integrates geometric fidelity, material heterogeneity, historical performance data, and predictive deep learning (DL) algorithms, enabling proactive structural assessment and decision-making. Geometrical accuracy is critical in the development of DT models, especially when dealing with heritage buildings whose non-linear seismic behavior is highly sensitive to local geometric irregularities. Recent studies (e.g., [44,45] ) demon- strate the power of combining 3D scanning technologies and photo- grammetric methods to reconstruct detailed digital geometries suitable for structural analysis. In parallel, recent work by [46] outlines a sys- tematic pipeline for generating discrete element models (DEM) directly from imagery, reinforcing the potential of image-to-DEM workflows for data-driven seismic assessment. The analyzed phases were achieved through three main steps; the first provides guidance on the available and recommended field survey methods to characterize the geometric configuration and material properties using a Building Information Model (BIM). The second fo- cuses on the creation of the virtual model using a finite element method (FEM) model and covers key aspects such as software selection based on its features, capabilities, and limitations; the linking process between the BIM and FEM model, modeling techniques, and seismic hazard defini- tion are also well-thought-out . Finally, the last phase highlights the challenges that arise during the post-processing of results and highlights the available tools to analyze massive data by suggesting a novel idea that incorporates the intensity-duration-frequency plots for the engi- neering demand parameters (EDP). Therefore, all these steps lead to the integration process between the physical and virtual world under the ICR framework (see Fig. 2 ). The process deals with aspects related to the deployment of a reliable SHM while focusing on the Early-warning system (EWS) and its con- nectivity between both worlds in real-time. Specialized sensor devices installed on the building will cover the physical world mapping response, while a FEM model is meant to represent the DT model in the virtual world. Thus, sensor software and hardware requirements, as well as the location selection for installing those, are defined using the performance-based assessment from a non-linear FEM model. Recent developments in artificial intelligence have introduced advanced techniques for crack detection using DL and computer vision, with proven applications in masonry and concrete structures. Studies such as [47] and [48] have demonstrated the potential of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for accurate, real-time crack localization and quantification. Although this study focuses on numerical and sensor-based modeling, these techniques are highly relevant and may be integrated into the early damage identification process or serve as validation tools for physical damage post-event. The case study corresponds to a heritage building located in Lima, Peru, specifically a religious monument that was built a century ago . A field survey was conducted to portray the geometric configuration through a point-cloud survey using a 3D laser scanner (3D LiDAR technology) that was then converted into a BIM, while the definition of the mechanical properties of the materials was based on previous studies and archives. In addition, detailed research into the building ’ s perfor- mance during previous earthquakes was addressed by checking existing damage reports, including the 2007 Pisco earthquake (moment magni- tude, Mw = 8.0). Subsequently, the virtual model was developed using the software 3DEC Version 9.0 [49] , based on an intermediate calibra- tion and validation process, which consisted of approximately 4500 block elements and 161,000 contact zones, and analyzed a total of 5 seismic records through time-history analysis (THA). The results were used to identify the monument ’ s seismic response, compare it with the previous damage report, and define the best spots for installing the field sensors that would be integrated into the physical world via the DT model. The main novelty of this research lies in the development of a methodology integrated with the ICR framework for heritage buildings. This methodology leverages SHM through a DT model, combined with a numerical one that focuses on the virtualization of the building. The technical aspects are addressed using a performance-based assessment, which captures the seismic structural response through a novel way of using the intensity-duration-frequency (IDF) for characterizing EDPs ’ results. This approach condenses the vast data generated from the THA results, allowing the identification of critical distribution patterns for the selection, placement, and calibration of sensors on the building, as well as TF for estimating damage on masonry walls. Finally, the methodology proposes action strategies to overcome the challenges encountered throughout the entire implementation process. Furthermore, it can be extended to other heritage buildings. 2. The results illustrated in Fig. 27 show the evolution of RMSE over training iterations for two input signal scenarios. Four sensor heights were analyzed: 9.38 m, 20.31 m, 36.15 m, and 39.65 m. The continuous lines represent training RMSE, while dashed lines indicate validation RMSE. A general trend observed across both cases is the decreasing RMSE over iterations, confirming successful convergence of the LSTM model. Moreover, the model performs significantly better when the sensor is placed at lower heights (i.e., 9.38 m and 20.31 m), where both the training and validation RMSE remain below 0.1. This suggests that signal quality and information content are richer at these locations, likely due to higher energy response concentrations and better observ- ability of local deformation patterns, which align with the nonlinear FEM-predicted damage zones. Furthermore, Case 2 consistently outperforms Case 1, with reduced RMSE levels across all control points. This confirms the added predictive value of the vertical motion component (UZ) in estimating axial stress — an important insight, as vertical accelerations are typically underestimated in masonry monitoring due to assumptions of high axial stiffness. The results here emphasize that vertical ground motion, while small in magnitude, carries critical information regarding through- thickness cracking and stress redistribution, especially in complex ge- ometries such as towers. Fig. 28 presents a summarized comparison of RMSE values obtained from the 24 LSTM models applied to validation and testing datasets. The lowest RMSEs are again concentrated in sensors located at 9.38 m and 20.31 m, supporting the findings from Fig. 27 . Notably, the RMSE values remain under 0.1 in most test cases, which reflects a high level of generalization and robustness of the LSTM models. However, a slight increase in error variability is seen at the higher elevations (36.15 m and 39.65 m), particularly under Case 1 scenarios. This suggests that elevated regions of the tower present higher complexity in dynamic behavior due to boundary condition interactions, modal coupling, and potentially unmodeled phenomena like torsion or out-of-plane flexure. In summary, the figures provide empirical support for the effec- tiveness of LSTM-based models in predicting nonlinear axial stress re- sponses in heritage masonry walls using real-time acceleration, velocity, and displacement inputs. Conclusions Based on the structural seismic response assessment on an existing heritage building, a methodological approach was proposed by the au- thors to standardize the ideal distribution of sensors devoted to real-time data feeding of digital twins. This methodology involved the identifi- cation of its main characteristics, including materials and geometry, followed by the idealization of the structural response through the development of numerical models. Then, the calibration and validation required a performance-based assessment of the monument, by means of linear and non-linear multi-degree-of-freedom models and a modal- response analysis to identify its dynamic response and behavior under static and dynamic loading conditions. The output allowed the re- searchers to tackle some of the main challenges faced when dealing with Building Virtualization for the integration of the numerical model into the Digital Twin model. Nevertheless, due to variables such as material degradation and human intervention, some architectural historic sites are already compromised [ 2 , 3 ]. Consequently, transitioning conservation efforts from passive salvage restoration to proactive preventive maintenance and intervening prior to the onset of threats is essential [ 6 – 8 ]. Digital twin technology [ 9 ] offers a novel approach to precisely understanding architectural history by creating a virtual model that represents an entity ’ s complete life cycle state using data and model-driven simulation, monitoring, prediction, and optimization [ 10 , 11 ]. It can assess the condition of historical structures in real-time using multi-source heterogeneous data and execute hyper-real-time extrapolation, thereby enhancing building health management and minimizing human intervention [ 12 ]. The digital twin concept was initially introduced by Michael Grieves in 2003, characterized as a three-dimensional representation of entities, voxels, and their informational interconnections [ 13 ]. Subsequently, Negri et al. expanded this concept, highlighting the real-time connection between physical and virtual systems via data synchronization [ 14 ]. As research advances, the three-dimensional model is increasingly inadequate to fulfil complicated requirements. Tao Fei and colleagues developed a five-dimensional architecture encompassing physical entities, virtual entities, twin services, twin data, and connectivity linkages [ 15 ]. This design has significantly advanced the utilization of digital twin technologies across various study domains. Although digital twin technology has shown unique advantages in many fields, its application in architectural heritage conser - vation is still in the exploratory stage, especially in preventive conservation, and the theoretical construction and empirical analysis have not been systematized yet. Vuoto et al. explored its potential in conserving the structural integrity of architectural heritage but lacked the verification of its practical application [ 16 ].Tan et al. combined laser scanning with tilt photogrammetry tools for archi - tectural archaeology, but this approach is limited to specific heritage types [ 17 ]. There is no universal method for building digital twins of architectural heritage. Digital twins, as virtual entities, are digital representations of physical entities for understanding, predicting, optimizing and controlling natural objects. The universality of its construction method directly affects the application effect of the digital twin so - lution. In the face of complex building structures and variable environments, how to construct pervasive digital twins to realize real- time monitoring and early warning is a vital issue in current research [ 18 ]. Bordeleau et al. explored various model-driven engineering techniques used in the model building process, identified as potentially overcoming the challenges associated with digital twin modeling [ 19 ]. Concurrently, Rasheed et al. provide a comprehensive overview of the methods and techniques currently applied in constructing digital twin models [ 20 ]. They discuss the challenges encountered during the construction process in detail, offering specific recommendations and insights from the authors. Digital twin modeling Architectural heritage digital twin construction is the digital modeling of physical entities ’ properties, methods, behaviors, and other characteristics in a digital space to simulate and predict their state and behavior. In order to ensure that the twin is highly consistent with the physical entity, it needs to be modeled in a virtual environment from the fusion of four dimensions: geometry, physics, behavior and rules, as shown in Fig. 2 . (1) Geometric Modeling Geometric modeling ( M G ) is a digital representation of architectural heritage, detailing the geometric attributes of elements such as columns, beams, and sapphires. These define the heritage ’ s intricate internal and external structures and constitute the initial phase in creating a digital twin. Point cloud modeling and building information modeling are commonly utilized. Point cloud modeling uses laser scanning and other techniques to gather dense point cloud data, thereby capturing the intricate aspects of the item. The volume of data remains substantial and necessitates high-performance computing resources. This work employs a hybrid point cloud approach and building information modeling to ensure data consistency to reconstruct physical things ’ geometric form and non-graphical data, thereby establishing a foundation for future model analysis and interactive functions. (2) Physical Modeling Physical modeling ( M P ) is the addition of physical properties and constraints to a geometric model to simulate the dynamic response of built heritage under natural environments (e.g., wind, rain, earthquakes, etc.) or anthropogenic forces, such as stresses and deformations. Mathematical approximations are usually made using finite element modeling techniques to construct models reflecting the physical phenomena of built heritage at macro and micro scales. Training and optimizing the models with real-time data streams can achieve a high-level mapping of the built heritage ’ s physical entities. This strategy operates on a closed-loop optimization model that continuously progresses along a timeline. This strategy optimizes the control objectives within the current time window based on predictive models, outputting the optimal decision for the current time window . This decision is then fed back into the actual construction process, thus advancing the time window to determine the actual construction status of the next time window for further decision optimization ( Sethi and Sor ger , 1991 ). Metaheuristic algorithms are commonly used in optimization processes, where the hyperparameter settings of such algorithms directly influence the optimization performance. Therefore, inspired by the aforementioned studies, this study aimed to establish a closed- loop model for construction processes based on a DT -enabled RHO strategy to address the flexible scheduling problem of multi-objective automated earthwork construction site equipment, ensuring construction progress. Its core principle is the bidirectional mapping between physical assets and their virtual models, enabling real-time sensing, prediction and optimization of the underlying system ( Glaessgen and Star gel, 2012 ). W ithin the AEC sector , DT applications are concentrated in design, construction scheduling and operations and maintenance (O&M). Discussion The proposed DT -based RHO approach has strong potential to improve the efficiency of construction equipment allocation and scheduling. The framework comprises a site-agnostic core and site-specific elements. The site-agnostic core is the perception–optimization–feedback loop coupled with the OIAO solver . The site-specific elements include (i) activity (process) definitions and constraints – resources, precedence relations and time, temperature and quality windows; (ii) sensing infrastructure and mappings from modalities to state variables (e.g. GNSS for vehicle localization and productivity/progress estimation; vision for indoor asset tracking; sensors for quality states); and (iii) boundary and safety constraints. The framework can be deployed without modification in analogous large-scale earthmoving contexts (e.g. highway asphalt paving ( Labban et al., 2013 ) and airfield construction). Extending it to broader civil works – including vertical buildings – requires adapting the site-specific elements (i–iii). The core perception–optimization–feedback loop with the OIAO solver remains unchanged. As an illustrative example in vertical construction with tower-crane operations, the multimodal perception module described in Section 3.1.1 can capture the parameters required to instantiate a discrete-event (DES) model of the hoisting workflow . These parameters include the number and type of cranes, service zones, load-radius envelopes, component weights, the decomposition and durations of the hoisting cycle (hooking, lifting, slewing, trolleying, positioning and releasing), hook travel distances, laydown/receiving queue capacities, floor-level takt times and inter-cr ane interference and safety clearances. This is consistent with established DES-based tower -crane scheduling research ( Al Hattab et al., 2017 ). These perceived variables parameterize the DES of tower -crane-assisted assembly and floor -by-floor production. The perception layer then continuously reconciles time-aligned observations—crane telemetry , queue lengths and floor progress – with synchronized DES predictions (cycle-time distributions, waiting times and resource utilization). When deviations exceed predefined thresholds, the OIAO-based RHO is triggered to revise crane assignments, hoisting sequences, time-slot allocations, priorities for shared resources and crew synchronization. The current best plan is subsequently fed back into field execution. For municipal works, the framework can adopt shorter rolling-horizon intervals. This further shrinks the decision space and runtime and facilitates online re- optimization under tight real-time constraints. In our case study , the maximum runtime was 712 s, which is operationally acceptable within the 3 h rolling interval. Going forward, we will integrate decision-space pruning (constraint presolve and activity/resource windowing), hierarchical decomposition with short horizons, warm starts with early stopping and parallel or batched (optionally surrogate-assisted) evaluation of candidate solutions to further reduce runtime and enable scaling to larg er projects. Additionally , the current evaluation benchmarks OIAO exclusively against AO under matched rolling-horizon settings. Future work will broaden the comparative scope – under identical RHO configurations – to include representative metaheuristics (e.g. PSO, DE and SA) and to integrate faster , lightweight optimizers within the RHO framework. In addition, we will conduct multi-site validation across vertical buildings, transportation infrastructure and small-scale municipal works. For the comprehensive insights into building fire dynamics, the thermometry array constructed from sensors need to be deployed to support real-time temperature monitoring. Resistance temper - ature detector (RTD) works based on a strong linear relationship be - tween resistance and temperature. But response speed and probe size also affect its competitiveness in wireless temperature measurement. Thermistors satisfy the requirements for fire temperature wireless sensing in buildings by virtue of their resistance-based thermometric principle [50] . For example, Negative Temperature Coefficient of Resistance is widely integrated into wireless sensing devices due to their affordability, compact size, high precision, and rapid response. Some optical-based temperature measurement methods such as fibre optics (FBG/DTS) and infrared (IR) cameras are also suitable for temperature measurement in the built environment. However, the integration into smart firefighting is still under-researched due to high overheads. Advances in wireless technology eliminated the limitations of wired, allowing the widespread implementation of IoT. The integration of wireless networks and devices has garnered significant attention in various areas such as object detection, environment monitoring and disaster management [51] . There are already a variety of wireless sensing protocols have been adopted in industry, such as Zigbee, Wi-Fi, and Bluetooth [52] . Considering the low power consumption charac - teristics required for long-term operation of the device, LoRa (Long Range) is an alternative wireless IoT connectivity method. The feature of transmitting limited data over long distances in a short period of time makes LoRa increasingly popular in low-power battery-powered embedded systems [53] . However, the existing IoT communication is limited to simple feedback on monitored fire signals, while the potential for smart firefighting driven by wireless IoT data inputs remains largely unexplored. 2.2. Ai-driven smart firefighting Currently, AI technology represented by deep learning is widely used in construction safety assessment and information management [54] . AI-driven fire forecast is primarily achieved by the multi-dimensional fitting via artificial neural networks (ANN). Database is constructed from large amounts of fire experiments and simulations to assist deep learning in characterizing building fires. Deep learning models can accelerate the solution process by establishing efficient mapping re - lationships [55] . Presently, various deep learning algorithms [55,56] have been developed and migrated to fulfil research on smart fire - fighting. It expedites access to fire-related information for decision- makers, encompassing aspects, such as fire source localization, heat release rate (HRR), and fire development stages [57 – 59] . Leveraging convolutional neural networks (CNN) for image processing, computer video techniques can be utilized to detect visual target in fire scenarios [60,61] . Advanced algorithms like recurrent neural networks (RNN), graph neural networks (GNNs), generative adversarial network (GAN), and Diffusion models can rapidly identify or advance forecast disaster escalations such as flashover and booms [62 – 65] . AIoT-integrated digital twin system for smart firefighting The AIoT-integrated digital twin for SureFire system is constructed based on the above-mentioned technological framework. As shown in Fig. 12 , the interactive Digital Twin necessitates the integration of multiple data types. Based on real full-scale buildings, separate pro - cesses are carried out including BIM construction, on-site sensing system deployment and AI model development. The cloud server assumes the responsibility of data interaction and Unity game engine is utilised as development engine and visualization platform. The system interface of FASA Fire Safety Digital Twin is demonstrated in Video S3 . Users can access and operate the system through the cloud-based user interface (UI) by entering the IP address, username, and password. The main interface of the system comprises five functional modules, namely On- site CCTV , Temperature distribution , 3D digital twin , Temperature field inference , and Hazard risk region . In the event of building fires, data form sensors will be shown on Temperature distribution module as bar chart and synchronised to the cloud server by the IoT system. The AI-integrated system allows for the fire Temperature field inference , include real-time reconstruction and 60- second-advanced forecasting. By setting target information (like tem - perature above 50 ◦ C), Hazard risk region module will display the valu - able regional information to support fire evacuation and rescue planning. In addition, on-site CCTV enables direct monitoring of the actual environment as the realistic basis for smart fighting system. The 3D Digital Twin is designed to visualise the virtual scene of the building fire by rendering flame and smoke data. 5. Conclusions For smart firefighting in buildings, this work proposes an informa - tion fusion framework for AIoT-integrated digital twin. ADLSTM-Fire model is developed to achieve the physical field spatiotemporal infer - ence based the limited on-site data. Moreover, the generalization capability of the proposed approach is demonstrated through the full- scale building fire experiments. The main conclusions are: (1) An AIoT-integrated Digital Twin system is proposed as the data fusion platform, overcoming the issues of data cross-domain collaboration during building fires. The system maps the com - plete building temperature field distribution in virtual space based on the collected discrete environmental data. Digitisation and visualisation of high-dimensional generic information can help decision-makers to fully understand the details of building fire and guide subsequent firefighting measures. (2) The simplified model can reconstruct the complete 2D tempera - ture field by discrete sensor data (Accuracy = 93 %), and its high robustness is well verified by benchmark numerical data. Moreover, ADLSTM-Fire model is developed leveraging the parameter initialization method based on model pre-training. ADLSTM-Fire model could forecast the temperature field distri - bution 60 s in advance based on the historical data (Accuracy = 92 %). Such super-real-time fire physical field information is available for building safety enhancement by fire prognosis and escalation prevention. (3) Without involvement in the system construction, the full-scale building fire experiment is employed to demonstrate the gener - alisation capability of the Digital Twin model. The validation showcases that the constructed system can generates trustworthy predictions with the realistic fire dynamic pattern. Therefore, the proposed AIoT-integrated Digital Twin system could be extended to different fire scenarios and served as the information founda - tion for the development of smart firefighting features. Future research of SureFire will further accommodate the challenges posed by scenario changes in real building fires. The ADLSTM-Fire model will be further refined for smart firefighting in unfamiliar buildings. With the building layout acting as an additional input parameter, a comprehensive multi-modal framework integrating ADLSTM-Fire will be developed to regulate the 2D data generation process of temperature field. Similarly, the dataset will be further enhanced by simulating more different factors in building fire scenarios such as structural type, room arrangement and wind environment.
