However, traditional monitoring methods that rely on inspectors are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and limited in their ability to detect three-dimensional (3D) incidents. Digital twin technology presents a promising solution by integrating real-time monitoring, simulation and interactivity capabilitie s in three dimensional virtual environments. Therefore, we propose a real-time digital twin –driven near-miss detection system to facilitate safety management at construction sites. Using advanced stereovision techniques, the system reproduces the construction site as a digital twin model, enabling the real-time 3D monitoring of near-miss events. To demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed system, a lifting project under various exposure conditions was investigated. The results demonstrate the system ’s ability to detect and visualize near-misses in three- dimensional space and provide proactive warnings of potential hazards. Introduction Safety is a crucial concern in the construction industry, because lack of safety leads to fatalities, injuries, and accidents that result in substantial costs and irreversible damages every year ( Rokooei et al. 2023 ). A comprehensive investigation of numerous construc- tion safety accidents found that almost every occurrence of a safety accident is not a coincidence without premonitory signs ( Shen and Marks 2016 ). Construction sites can be high-risk areas for serious safety accidents due to the accumulation of various incidents influ- enced by hazardous factors associated with building processes. However, in certain situations, these risk factors can be mitigated or eliminated, preventing any actual harm or significant damage (Khakzad et al. 2014 ). Thus, only minor injuries and minimal losses may occur ( Zhang et al. 2019 ), leading to what is known as near-miss events at construction sites ( Raviv et al. 2017 ). The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) defined a near miss as a critical incident that narrowly averted an accident due to timely intervention ( OSHA 2016 ). Heinrich’s law ( Heinrich 1941 ) suggests that for every major accident, there are typically 29 minor accidents and 300 potential hazards. This implies that injuries or fatalities are not isolated in- cidents, but rather the result of a series of events occurring one after another, even if the incident itself happens suddenly at a specific moment (Fig. 1). Near-miss events serve as precursors to accidents, highlighting the importance of paying attention to these near-misses in order to prevent safety incidents (Saleh et al. 2013). Consequently, the real-time and effective detection and analysis of near-miss events can reduce the frequency of safety accidents and enhance the overall safety manageme nt at construction sites ( Duan and Zhou 2023 ). However, the real-time detection of near-miss events at construc- tion sites poses a complex and formidable challenge ( Goldenhar et al. 2003 ; Hajdasz 2015 ; Yang and Cai 2024 ), highlighting the dynamic and unpredictable nature of construction environments. The traditional approach to on-site safety inspections, which is heavily reliant on professional surveyors, demands significant workerpower and material resources. Moreover, manual monitor- ing is prone to errors and is less reliable due to factors such as staff fatigue or prolonged strain on vision ( Cheng et al. 2024 ), resulting in an underestimation of risk factors at the construction sites. To address these issues, various solutions have been proposed in both academia and industrial settings. An innovative safety model employing the Internet of Things (IoT) has been designed to detect near-miss events in real-time through vigilant monitoring of site personnel and environmental conditions ( Chung et al. 2023 ), with the goal of reducing accident rates. Recently, blockchain technol- ogy has emerged as a cutting-edge tool for monitoring near-misses at construction sites ( Li et al. 2023 ) laying the groundwork for an extensive database of incident records and an early warning sys- tem for near-miss detection. Additionally, 2.4-GHz Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) technology has been utilized to develop a real-time location system to accurately gather and tally near-misses at con- struction sites ( Li et al. 2016 ). However, contemporary research on near-misses detection predominantly has focused on two- dimensional analysis, and the transition to more-comprehensive three-dimensional (3D) tracking is still pending. In recent years, the construction industry has begun exploring various new technologies, including digital twin (DT) technology. This technology enables the real-time collection of construction- related information, such as workers, materials, equipment, and so forth, which is displayed intuitively on a general platform. Individ- uals at different phases of the construction process can access this information for strategic planning in their next steps. Moreover, digital twin technology plays a vital role throughout the entire life cycle of a building, encompassing the design, construction, and operation and maintenance phases. To optimize the planning sched- uling and execution of prefabricated components at construction sites, a real-time synchronous digital twin system has been pro- posed (Jiang et al. 2022c ). In addition, digital twins also have been applied to anomaly detection and monitoring during the operation and maintenance phases of construction projects ( Lu et al. 2020 ). Another notable application is the use of a vision-based frame- work for digital twinning and threat assessment, which identifies and evaluates the characteristics and potential impacts of wind- borne debris within a virtual simulation model of a construction site (Kamari and Ham 2022). However, current research has not yet explored the application of digital twins for the detection of near- misses at construction sites. Therefore, this paper proposes a digital twin –driven near- miss detection system at construction sites in a timely manner. Related Works Near-Misses in Construction Although incidents may pose risks of injury or death, they can be prevented with proactive measures ( Saurin et al. 2015 ). Despite the importance of gathering detailed data on near-miss incidents for construction safety management, too few of these events are identified and reported. At present, the existing research on near- misses in the construction field is divided into two categories. One category traces the roots of near-misses at construction sites. Wu et al. ( 2010a) established a systematic approach to ad- dress and prevent precursors and immediate factors (PaIFs) at con- struction sites, enabling the extraction of valuable insights from accident histories to enhance site safety. Zhou et al. ( 2019) intro- duced a human dynamics model using attempt data to uncover the shared dynamic characteristics of accidents stemming from unsafe behaviors among construction workers. Li and Ding ( 2019) proposed a deep learning-based video surveillance method for automatically detecting and analyzing the possible causes of near- miss events at construction sites, forming an exhaustive case base from real-time monitoring for safety assessment, training, and safety management. Another category monitors near-miss events for analysis to guide construction. Wu et al. ( 2010b) proposed an autonomous real-time tracking system of near-miss accidents (ARTTS-NMA) at construction sites to monitor near-misses. Yang et al. ( 2014) introduced an inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based method for detecting attempted falls, which provided quantitative data for fall risk assessment of steel workers. Teizer and Cheng (2015)p r o p o s e d a proximity hazard indicator to automatically identify hazardous areas at construction sites and to collect and analyze spatiotemporal conflicts between walking workers and identified hazards. The evolution of near-miss detection technologies has been marked by advancements in wearable devices, mobile technology, and artificial intelligence. Early efforts utilized wearable inertial measurement units to monitor workers ’ movements and detect po- tential falls ( Aria et al. 2014 ). Subsequent research refined these systems with automated detection methods and threshold-based algorithms ( Yang et al. 2014 , 2015, 2016). Mobile technology introduced a new paradigm, leveraging smartphone sensors and artificial neural networks (ANNs) for event recognition, capitalizing on the ubiquity of smartphones ( Zhang et al. 2019). However, this approach faced limitations in battery life and computational power. Proximity-based systems used Global Positioning System (GPS) and radio-frequency identification (RFID) technologies to assess risks associated with hazardous proximity, providing valuable insights into potential near-miss incidents ( Golovina et al. 2016 ). Real-time location tracking with ultrawideband (UWB) systems further enhanced this approach by offering precise tracking of workers ’ movements in relation to hazardous zones or equipment ( Teizer and Cheng 2015 ). How- ever, these methods are hindered by high costs and installation complexities. Despite progress, current technologies face limitations includ- ing discomfort and usability issues with wearables, battery and processing constraints with mobile devices, and cost and com- plexity challenges with proximity-based systems. To address these limitations, computer vision offers a promising alternative. By an- alyzing video feeds from cameras strategically placed on construc- tion sites, computer vision systems can detect near-miss incidents with high accuracy and minimal user discomfort. This approach not only reduces the reliance on wearable devices and mobile technol- ogy but also provides a more comprehensive view of the construc- tion environment, enhancing the overall safety monitoring system. Discussion This study introduces an image-based digital twin system that, compared with traditional point cloud –based counterparts, demon- strates significant enhancements in real-time capability and light- weight processing. The system has been trained to recognize and track common elements found on construction sites, such as per- sonnel, materials, and machinery, and has established a dynamic element model database. This foundational training ensures that the system is broadly applicable to a wide range of construction envi- ronments. However, it is acknowledged that certain specialized equipment and materials may require additional training before deployment. This pretraining process is designed to be efficient, and does not entail an extensive time commitment, allowing for rapid adaptation to specific site requirements. The system ’s modu- lar design and the use of advanced stereovision techniques facilitate this scalability, making it a robust and flexible tool for enhancing safety management across diverse construction projects. During field trials, the real-time digital twin –driven system for detecting near-misses demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving a perfect score in both precision and recall for Type S1 near-miss detection. This high level of accuracy may be attributed to the relatively uncluttered environment surrounding the Type S1 near-misses during testing, which were characterized by a lower number of workers and less visual confusion. Additionally, the proximity of Type S1 near-misses to the installed binocular camera, resulting in clearer video imagery, likely contributed to the system’s enhanced detection capabilities. Additionally, the impact of lighting on computer vision is a pivotal factor to take into account, because it greatly influences the visual quality of captured images. This paper presents an analysis of the system ’s stability under varying exposure conditions through experimental evaluation. The noticeable decrease in precision for Type S3 near-misses in over- exposure scenarios can be attributed to several factors:  image degradation: overexposed areas within the image can become excessively bright, resulting in a loss of detail;  feature distortion: overexposure often leads to prominent high- light areas that can overshadow critical features such as edges, shapes, textures, and colors; and  Data set limitations: the model was trained on a data set lacking diverse exposure scenarios, which hinders its ability to accu- rately recognize and adapt to real-world exposure variations. To mitigate these issues, the following methodologies could be considered for future improvements:  image preprocessing: implementing automatic exposure correc- tion as part of the preprocessing stage can diminish the influence of inconsistent lighting on the system ’s performance;  dynamic range extension: employing image sensors with a superior dynamic range can help alleviate the issues of overexposure and underexposure, thus preserving more detail in the imagery; and  data augmentation: enhancing the training data set to include a wider array of images with varying exposure levels can enable the model to more effectively learn features across different lighting conditions. The lower recall rates for Type S3 near-misses observed across the three test sets, compared with other types, may be attributable to the densely populated construction machinery present around the S3 areas within the site. The ensuing chaotic and complex environ- ment could result in certain S3 near-misses being undetected by the system, thus contributing to a marginally reduced recall relative to other incident types. Conclusions and Future Work The construction industry is characterized by its complexity and variability, leading to a high frequency of safety incidents. To mit- igate these risks, it is crucial to implement a real-time monitoring of near-misses at construction sites. Although these near-misses may not result in immediate serious harm, they serve as indicators of underlying risks and vulnerabilities that could precipitate severe injuries or damages. Through proactively analysis of these near- misses, construction management teams can pinpoint and rectify unsafe conditions or practices, effectively forestalling the occur- rence of actual accidents. Furthermore, this vigilant monitoring process fosters a culture of safety and heightens the safety con- sciousness among construction workers, contributing to an overall safety climate. Digital twin technology has gained significant attention as a state-of-the-art simulation tool across various industries. Therefore, we introduced the application of digital twin technology to the field of near-misses monitoring at construction sites by presenting a digital twin –driven near-miss detection system which distills im- plicit ontological knowledge within engineering realms. This sys- tem adeptly detects near-misses within a three-dimensional space, providing early warnings that underpin a scientific approach to safety management at construction sites. However, the research in this paper still has some limitations. Constrained by the inherent limitations of computer vision tech- nology, there is an issue of incomplete information capture when personnel and machinery at the construction site overlap. In addi- tion, certain extreme lighting conditions can marginally affect the stability of the digital twin –driven system for detecting near- misses. Furthermore, current vision-based digital twin system op- erates effectively within a camera field of view of up to 20 m, which is not suitable for residential houses, high-rise buildings, and large classroom buildings. Future research will address the occlusion problem by inte- grating multiple perspectives to broaden the system ’s applicabil- ity and enhance its suitability for large-scale complex scenarios. Additionally, enhancements in both hardware and algorithms will be considered to mitigate the impact of lighting conditions, with the overarching goal of continuously refining the system to further the advancement of safety management in the construction industry. Furthermore, future studies will conduct detailed variable analysis of different update frequencies to balance efficiency and cost, enhancing the practical application of DT technology in various construction settings. In recent years, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) have gradually become a powerful tool for solving complex engineering problems as an emerging machine learning technology. PINNs combine physical laws with neural networks to effectively model systems and make efficient predictions and optimizations based on physical equations, especially in situations where data is scarce. The emergence of this technology provides new ideas for the performance analysis of bridge structures [2 ]. On the other hand, Building Information Modeling (BIM), as an integrated digital modeling tool, has been widely used in the construction industry, especially in bridge design and construction management. BIM can provide accurate geometric modeling, construction planning, and material infor - mation, thereby helping designers, engineers, and construction personnel to collaborate and make better decisions. However, BIM itself still has certain limitations in the in-depth analysis of structural performance and multi-physics coupling simulation [3 ].A significant advancement in addressing these limitations is the integration of live data meas- urement systems directly installed on structures, which enables the continuous monitoring of real-time performance data. By incorporating these sensor-based measurements into BIM, it becomes possible to create more trustworthy and dynamic models that reflect the actual behavior of the structures under varying conditions [4 ]. This approach not only enhances the accuracy of BIM models but also strengthens their predictive capabilities. The integration of real-time monitoring data is gaining increasing global recognition as an essential practice for enhancing the reli- ability and functionality of BIM, particularly in the context of structural performance analysis. This practice is gaining traction due to its potential to bridge the gap between theoretical modeling and actual physical performance, thus providing more reliable insights for maintenance, safety, and optimization [5 ].Physics-Informed Neural Networks. Therefore, combining PINN with BIM technology to form a new intelligent design and optimization method can make up for the shortcomings of traditional methods. This can not only enhance the safety and reliability of bridge design, but also provide more scientific and systematic decision support for the construction of prefabricated bridges. Therefore, this article aims to explore the theoretical framework and application path of combining PINN and BIM technology, provide new technical solutions for predicting and opti- mizing the structural performance of prefabricated bridges, and provide theoretical support and methodological guidance for practical problems in engineering practice. Specifically, we focus on several key aspects: ✥ Stress State: The distribution and magnitude of stresses within the bridge components under various loading conditions. ✥ Deformations and Displacements: The geometric changes and movements of the bridge structure, including deflections and rotations, under different operational scenarios. ✥ Operability: The ability of the bridge to function effectively under normal usage conditions, including traffic loads and environmental influences. ✥ Extreme Events: The structural behavior and resilience of the bridge during extreme conditions such as high winds, earthquakes, or other natural disasters. General applications of digital twin technology Digital Twin technologies have gained increasing attention across various industrial domains due to their ability to provide real-time synchronization between physical and digital entities. Across all these sectors, the ability of DTs to integrate real- time sensing, virtual modeling, and predictive analytics has opened new frontiers for intelligent monitoring and control. Within the construction industry, DT adoption is accelerating (Jiang et al., 2021), yet compre- hensive frameworks for real-time safety management remain relatively underexplored. While these efforts demonstrate the potential of DTs for enhancing safety management, many current approaches are limited to single- use cases or small-scale implementations, lacking broader integration across multi-worker, outdoor environments. 2.3. Federated learning and digital twin integration Recent years have seen growing interest in combining Federated Learning (FL) with DT to support privacy-preserving, distributed in- telligence across diverse systems. Pioneering frameworks have been proposed to enable collaborative FL among city-level DTs for public health applications such as pandemic response (Pang et al., 2021). In industrial IoT environments, adaptive DT architectures have been designed to enhance FL by enabling device-specific modeling while reducing communication costs (Sun et al., 2020). Drone-based DT-FL frameworks have been introduced for privacy-aware data aggregation and storage in blockchain networks (Islam and Shin, 2023). Health- care applications have benefited from hierarchical FL-DT models for anomaly detection with improved resilience to data poisoning and communication delays (Gupta et al., 2021). Other studies integrate FL with DT and mobile edge computing (MEC) to overcome latency and scalability challenges in heterogeneous network environments (He et al., 2022; Alhartomi et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2020a; AbdulRahman et al., 2023). Despite these advancements, existing works often focus on static or semi-static environments (smart cities, healthcare, IIoT), with limited attention to highly dynamic, mobile, multi-agent environments such as outdoor construction sites. Furthermore, many studies remain at the simulation stage, without extensive validation using real-world edge hardware. 2.4. As a result, SHM is regarded as an especially promising domain for advancing DT technology in civil engineering. Viviani * HEIG-VD/HES-SO - University of Applied Sciences and Arts Western Switzerland, Route de Cheseaux 1, CH-1401 Yverdon-Les-Bains, Switzerland ARTICLE INFO Keywords: Digital twin models Bridge management Degradation phenomena Maintenance Differential evolution algorithm ABSTRACT Digital twins are evolving to oversee the entire construction life cycle, with a strong emphasis on sustainability across environmental, financial, regulatory, and administrative dimensions. This paper introduces a methodol - ogy for managing existing bridges through an adaptable digital twin. The aim of this research is to develop a framework for constructing digital twins that, by enabling structural analysis and “ what-if ” scenario simulations, supports more reliable maintenance decision-making. Such type of digital twin ensure safety, extend lifespan, and provide a precise database for managing end-of-life processes within a circular “ cradle to cradle ” framework. This methodology also addresses obsolescence issues related to software evolution and the longer lifespan of a bridge compared to its creator. A case study demonstrates the methodology ’ s effectiveness, showing that digital twins can be flexible, cost-effective tools for managing all types of bridges, including small and existing ones. 1. Introduction Failures in bridges and infrastructure result in significant economic losses and, in many cases, loss of life [ 1 – 3 ]. Additionally, these failures often have a profound emotional impact on communities, similar to other types of traumatic events [ 4 , 5 ]. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , except for unpredictable extreme events, all causes of bridge collapse have known causes. Bridges in service are subject to degradation processes influenced by material properties, mechanical loads, and environmental factors. [ 7 – 9 ]. Despite safety factors designed to reduce uncertainties and extensive research aimed at avoiding design errors and ensuring main - tenance, bridges naturally undergo performance degradation over time. In principle, for a well-designed structure, the loss of performance is slow and manageable, but without an appropriate inspection and maintenance plan, the risk of an acceleration in the deterioration of performance leading to service and safety problems is amplified [ 3 , 10 – 12 ]. Moreover, existing structures were designed under different conditions and standards that have evolved over time [ 13 – 15 ]. For decades, tools like Finite Element (FE) software have been used to build digital models of bridges to analyze how performance changes due to degradation or increased loads. These models have improved the safety, serviceability, and lifespan of bridges, but they have limitations. They are software-specific (i.e., they become useless if the software is unavailable) and lack interoperability, meaning a model created for structural analysis cannot be used with other software. Building Information Modeling (BIM) has partly addressed these limitations and promoted the development of new models known as digital twins (DTs). Borrowed from other industries, DTs are digital representations of physical counterparts, integrating all the character - istics of the physical entity into a single platform. This research aimed to develop a methodology for establishing a DT for existing bridges that can evolve alongside its physical counterpart. The DT can then be used to manage maintenance operations following a “ cradle to cradle ” logic. In this context, adopting a “ cradle to cradle ” approach means that, at the end of the bridge ’ s life, the DT will serve as a precise database of its composition, facilitating the efficient reuse or recycling of the bridge. 1.1. Literature review DTs offer potential for effective maintenance management and are increasingly recognized among emerging systems. Generally, a DT refers to a digital version of a system, component, or asset [ 16 ]. Although the definition of DT remains debated, it is commonly described as a “ digital representation of the physical asset that can communicate, coordinate, and cooperate in the manufacturing process to improve productivity and efficiency through knowledge sharing ” [ 17 ]. In general, the models developed for bridges often lack real-time updates and communication with their physical counterparts, preventing two-way interaction and effectively making them digital shadows rather than true digital twins. Nevertheless, the term “ digital twins ” for these “ digital shadows ” has become so common in bridge engineering that it will be used in the following sections. It is important to note that the extension of the term “ digital twins ” to “ digital shadows, ” as observed in the literature, could lead to confusion or misaligned expectations in the future. According to [ 16 ], a DT can be categorized by its level of sophisti - cation/maturity: pre-digital twin, digital twin, adaptive digital twin, and intelligent digital twin. These categories differ based on the existence of a physical twin, data acquisition methods, and the use of machine learning for system/environment interaction. Bridge engineering DTs are classified as ‘digital twin – level 2, ’ which means they include per - formance, health, and maintenance data from the physical twin and receive batch rather than real-time updates. The essential features of a bridge DT, as summarized in [ 18 ], include: a digital replica of the physical structure, data as a core component, near-real-time updates, life-cycle data integration, a common data environment, and its function as a simulation and visualization tool for risk assessment, collaboration, and predictive performance. Additionally, it leverages real measure - ment data to enhance future projects and practices. These characteristics make DTs particularly suitable for managing bridge structures, which require continuous monitoring and adaptive maintenance strategies. [ 19 ]. Regardless of DT classification, DT models are sometimes seen as an unnecessary cost, particularly when the owner lacks the expertise and personnel to use them effectively, and the bridge in question does not face significant challenges or holds reduced relative importance. Nevertheless, whether small or large, a bridge remains a crucial part of the economy and society, with a lifespan likely to outlast the citizens who use it. Small bridges punctuate the landscape of a country, and their management by small towns can become challenging, leading to sub - stantial costs for repairs or strengthening operations necessary to ensure safety and serviceability. Sometimes, societal changes may require an old local bridge to support much heavier loads than anticipated at the time of its construction, as it now falls within a heavy-load transportation route leading to a motorway. In such cases, a DT can provide a better means to ensure necessary public service and keep costs under control. Indeed, a DT enables service life prediction, anomaly detection, seismic collapse assessment, and structural health analysis [ 20 , 21 ]. These functionalities are achieved through the integration of FE methods and data-driven approaches [ 22 ]. Recent efforts on DTs focus primarily on geometric reconstruction and [ 23 ] and physical-digital interaction [ 24 ]. In terms of geometric reconstruction, advancements in laser scanning techniques have enabled scan-to-BIM processes that accurately reproduce the geometry of existing structures in digital form. However, real-world complexity often exceeds model representations, making models based solely on laser scanning generally unsuitable for bridge safety and serviceability analyses or vulnerability assessments, even with AI-assisted defect detection through image analysis. Therefore, once the model obtained from laser scanning is converted into an open format and interoperable DT, it is essential to incorporate the specific characteristics of the structure and inspection results [ 25 , 26 ] to achieve an accurate repre - sentation of the real structure and meaningful prediction of its perfor - mances. Other advancements concern the interaction between the DT and its physical counterpart for structural health monitoring, achieved through the integration of sensors that measure critical metrics [ 27 – 29 ]. However, as stated in [ 20 ], challenges remain, particularly in simulation and the development of “ what-if ” scenarios for assessing asset risks and predicting performance. Addressing these challenges necessitates a comprehensive methodology and appropriate ontology to ensure interoperability and effective knowledge sharing among stake - holders [ 30 ]. A well-constructed DT can have a synergistic effect that enhances long-term sustainability. Open formats and interoperability enable more effective knowledge sharing among professionals working on the same project. They also allow owners greater flexibility in choosing consul - tants for various maintenance operations or collaborating with other owners to optimize life-cycle management costs. Open formats and interoperability help address software obsolescence issues and ensure continued use of the DT even if the original developer is no longer available. Additionally, these features aid owners, especially institu - tional ones, in sharing knowledge and collaborating to reduce opera - tional costs while increasing safety, serviceability, and the lifespan of structures. Knowledge sharing can be maximized through interoperable formats like IFC [ 31 , 32 ], which aim to ensure interoperability among AEC/FM industry software applications. Leveraging semantic modeling at the intersection of IoT and AI, IFC robustly supports the development of DTs [ 33 – 35 ]. In bridge engineering, IFC has been used for two main pur - poses: detecting degradation and real-time monitoring. For example, IFC has been used for spalling detection from point cloud data [ 25 ], damage visualization through image processing [ 26 ] and real-time monitoring using artificial neural networks based on structural analysis [ 36 ]. Despite various efforts to enhance comprehensive knowledge sharing, a methodology that addresses the challenge of software obsolescence, while also enabling the creation of “ what-if ” scenarios, has yet to be found. Structures like bridges require ongoing structural analyses to assess safety and serviceability after exposure to loads and environmental ef - fects. Therefore, an interoperable DT must be open with an ontology able to accommodate the effect of degradation phenomena and the re - sults of monitoring activities [ 31 , 37 , 38 ]. The DT model should also be easily transferable to a FE method software specialized for structural analysis and offer sufficient flexibility to integrate inspection and monitoring data seamlessly. 1.2. Problem statement Bridges are owned by a diverse range of entities, from large orga - nizations like the Swiss Federal Roads Office (FEDRO), which manages hundreds of bridges, to small rural villages overseeing only a few. These owners face a common challenge: the need for DTs that are tailored to the specific characteristics of each bridge and capable of evolving alongside their physical counterparts by integrating data from regular inspections and monitoring [ 20 , 39 ]. While these capabilities are essential for ensuring safety, serviceability, and effective maintenance planning, existing DTs often fall short in meeting another critical need — interoperability across various software platforms. Public entities typically outsource DT development to engineering firms or universities, resulting in multiple models created with different software that often become obsolete within a few years. This fragmen - tation can lead to inefficiencies, particularly when new professionals are unable to use these models, necessitating the development of new ones. To address these challenges, DTs must be interoperable and use open formats, ensuring they remain functional across different software used Fig. 1. Distribution of causes of bridge collapse [ 6 ]. M. Moreover, the longevity of bridges and the evolving nature of their environments demand flexible DTs that can adapt to new scenarios and expand as needs arise without altering their core principles. Rather than merely listing advanced features, the focus should be on developing a robust methodology for constructing DTs that can be easily updated and remain relevant over time. 1.3. Research contribution The overarching goal of this research is to establish a methodology for constructing Digital Twins (DTs) that support structural analysis and maintenance decision-making, with a particular focus on enabling “what-if” scenario simulations. This approach ensures that maintenance strategies can be explored and optimized without altering the core working principles of the DT, while also being flexible enough to accommodate other applications. To be effective, these models must accurately represent reality and integrate all relevant data collected during inspections. Such dynamic models enable professionals to operate the DT using their specialized software. The methodology is demonstrated through a real case study of the Praz bridge, which illustrates how laser scanner technology, combined with an appropriate BIM ontology, contributes to creating an open and interoperable DT. This DT evolves over time by integrating observed degradation effects, serving as a tool for structural analysis and main - tenance. It also permits the evaluation of structural safety and service - ability under multiple hazard scenarios based on the potential evolution of these degradations and facilitates the efficient management of the bridge’s end-of-life in a circular logic. Additionally, a low-cost monitoring methodology applied to the Grandson bridge highlights how software obsolescence and the retire - ment of engineers had previously hindered the use of valuable tech - nology. This example underscores the need for open formats and interoperable DTs and demonstrates how a well-constructed DT can prevent the loss of critical information. 1.4. Although this model respects the dictates of the time, it does not represent faithfully the real stiffness, the geometries, and the materials used. Such differences lead to results that render the predictive model unsuitable for monitoring purposes. This can be compensated by an optimization process. In order to update the FE model, the problem is first simplified, leading to an optimized law while maintaining computational effi - ciency. The proposed approach relies on the static scheme illustrated in Fig. 26 . Displacement values at points P1-P6, indicated in Fig. 26 , were computed assuming the bridge deck as a Timoshenko beam element. The displacement functions S a , S b , and S c have been expressed in function of: the (i) elastic modulus (EM), (ii) the first span length (L a ), and (iii) the middle span length (L b ). These are the key variables to be optimized. According to [ 77 ], optimization parameters might include material characteristics, geometric features, and masses. The EM was chosen for its significant influence on the entire structure, along with L a and L b for taking into account potential asymmetries. L affects deflection with a power of 3, while self-weight, EM and inertia with a power of 1. Notably, not all software allows inertia variation without altering the cross- section. The DE algorithm implemented facilitates escaping local minima, but the optimized solution may deviate significantly from initial values if parameters that strongly impact control parameters (S a , S b and S c ) are not used. However, optimization parameters choice also depends on the model ’ s objectives and the structure type. The DE al - gorithm is implemented in Wolfram Mathematica platform [ 78 ]. The optimization process entails minimizing the objective function, obtained as the sum of squared differences between measured (mean of DIC measurements for all loading conditions for each midspan) the calculated values using the displacement functions S a , S b , and S c . 4.5. Introduction With rapid urbanization, various types of construction take place in different corners of our cities, ranging from tunneling, house devel- opment, and road renovation, to pipeline rehabilitation. Many of these construction activities involve an excavation process, which removes earth materials on the surface either in order to install/ repair utilities underground, or to replace with manmade structures for foundation engineering. Excavation in urban areas has always been a challenge for the underground surface of our cities has already been filled with an extremely complex network system consisting of water mains, power lines, natural gas pipes, sewage, drainage, inter alia . Urban excavation among such a complex but invisible network is like “a man walking on eggshells, ” which, with any carelessness, can cause a collision with the underground utilities, potentially leading to destructive consequences. Over the years, there have been many major accidents in which excavators hit and destroyed underground pipes, resulting in huge loss of life and property ( Li et al. 2015 ; Talmaki et al. 2013 ; Talmaki and Kamat 2012). According to the report of the Underground Pipeline Committee of China Urban Planning Association (UPCC), from 2019 to 2021, 3,450 pipeline accidents occurred in total, of which 26.1% (902 incidents) were due to excavation damage and incorrect operation, and caused 242 fatalities, 648 injuries, and hundreds of millions in property damage. The challenges of UES reside in several aspects. First, the tradi- tional excavation operation depends on the experience of the oper- ator, which will lead to incorrect operation because the position of the bucket tip and the body cannot be accurately grasped. Second, due to the invisibility and uncertainty of the underground pipe, the excavator operator cannot accurately judge the relative posi- tion between the bucket tip and the underground pipe in real-time (Su et al. 2013 ; Tanoli et al. 2019 ). Third, the lack of a convenient and friendly way to accurately and timely transmit the 3D pose of the excavator and the spatial position of the underground pipe to the excavator operator. In light of its challenging nature, research efforts have been made to improve UES. In terms of excavator 3D pose estima- tion, the current research estimates the 3D pose of the excavator from visual and nonvisual perspectives, respectively. Vision-Based Pose Estimation Method The input information of the vision-based excavator pose estima- tion method often comes from the camera on the construction site. Compared with precision instruments such as laser scanners, the camera is widely used in construction sites and has the advantages of low cost and convenient data acquisition ( Liang et al. 2019). The vision-based pose estimation method can directly analyze the po- sition and orientation information from the input data, identify the on-site construction equipment (Golparvar-Fard et al. 2013; Soltani et al. 2016; Zou and Kim 2007), and then estimate the pose of its six degrees of freedom ( Feng et al. 2018 ; Liang et al. 2019 ; Luo et al. 2020; Mahmood et al. 2022 ). According to whether the marker needs to be preinstalled on the device, it can be divided into un- marked pose estimation methods and marked pose estimation methods. The marker-based pose estimation method uses an optical camera to monitor the fiducial markers preinstalled on the exca- vator manipulator and obtains the manipulator ’s pose through computer vision technology. Lundeen et al. ( 2016) developed a marker-based excavator pose estimation system, and the bucket tip depth error can be controlled within 2.5cm, but due to the complexity of the electromechanical system, the implementation on the construction site is very difficult. Feng et al. ( 2018) pro- posed a camera marker network structure in which two cameras and markers can achieve centimeter-level measurement accuracy within a range of 15 m. Zhao et al. ( 2021) proposed a method for estimating the pose of an excavator through a monocular camera and markers. The depth error of this method is less than 2.2cm, but it has a relatively large orientation error. Although the marker- based pose estimation method has achieved relatively good re- search results in terms of accuracy, this method has obvious shortcomings: on the one hand, the marker is prone to damage. On the other hand, it is difficult to keep the cameras and markers free from occlusion in a dynamic construction environment (Lundeen et al. 2016 ). The unmarked pose estimation method omits the step of installing markers in advance and can directly extract pose infor- mation from images, which is convenient for application on construction sites. Soltani et al. ( 2017) estimated the 2D pose of the excavator by training on synthetic images of the excavator components. Traditional image processing and computer vision methods rely on the quality of the dataset, and their pose estima- tion bias may increase due to the constantly changing background of the construction site ( Luo et al. 2020 ). In recent years, the combination of computer vision and convolutional neural net- works (CNN) has achieved good results in estimating the 3D pose of excavators, and the decisive factor for the dramatic im- provement in performance is a large, accurately labeled, and di- verse dataset ( Liang et al. 2019 ; Luo et al. 2020 ; Xu and Y oon 2019). However, in a practical setting, the samples that can be used to make datasets are limited, and it takes a lot of time and effort to prepare datasets with high-quality annotations ( Mostafa and Hegazy 2021). Therefore, Mahmood et al. ( 2022) studied train- ing a CNN on a synthetic dataset to estimate the 3D pose of an excavator, and the 3D position error of the proposed method was 51.42 cm. System Prototype Development Fig. 6 is the architecture diagram of intelligent guidance and safety early warning systems for urban excavation. The system frame- work is divided into four parts, which are the vehicle terminal, data- base and application servers, remote monitoring client, and field managers. The vehicle terminal is divided into a physical model, virtual model, and data transmission module. The physical model consists of an excavator, two GNSS receivers, four IMU sensors, a data con- troller, and a Pad. The data controller has a multifunction port and a built-in data transmission unit (DTU). The multifunction port in- cludes a power port, an RS-232 serial port, a CAN serial port, and a GNSS antenna port. The power port is used to connect the excavator power supply module, and RS-232 serial port is used to connect the Pad. The CAN port is used to connect the IMU sen- sors, and the antenna port is used to connect the GNSS receivers. Considering the real-time performance of subsequent calculations, we use wired transmission protocols for data transmission between various hardware devices of the vehicle terminal, instead of using wireless local area networks (WLANs) or public networks. The data controller obtains tasks from servers through DTU, including work area, work time, and model information, integrates GNSS receivers data and IMU sensors data, and transmits the above data to Pad in real-time through RS-232 serial port to improve the re- sponse speed. The vehicle terminal adopts a Client/Server architec- ture, and the Pad can directly perform calculations and analysis after receiving data. Unity is used as the 3D visualization frame to establish the virtual model on the Pad, which can realize the 3D pose estimation, relative distance calculation, real-time safety warning, and visualization guidance. The Pad visually displays the calculation results and 3D models of the excavator and its sur- rounding scenes to the excavator operator in real-time to guide the excavation operation. The results are transmitted back to the data controller in real-time. The data controller transmits the above data to the server through DTU. The server receives the returned data, which is further stored and analyzed. The remote monitoring client adopts a Browser/Server architecture and implements remote three-dimensional visualiza- tion based on Three.js. The remote monitoring client should be pre- pared for monitoring first, including establishing a BIM model and setting excavation tasks. Meanwhile, it realizes remote real-time monitoring and safety warning for excavation work, helping the system administrator judge the operation situation of the entire work area. After excavation, the system administrator can inquire about the warning information and excavation trajectory and play- back the excavation process on the remote monitoring client to judge the cause of the warning. Personal digital assistants (PDAs) can receive warning messages and task information sent by servers to assist field managers in controlling. System Field Application The system is applied in a sewage pipe network project in Shan Dong, China, which includes three roads to be excavated, with a total excavation length of 1,760 m. Fig. 9(a) is the project over- view interface of the remote monitoring client that can remotely monitor the overall situation of the work area and help the project management personnel master the number of excavator machi- nery, warning times, and the location of each excavator in the work area during the current construction. Fig. 9(b) is the visual display interface of the dynamic mapping of the excavator and the BIM of underground pipes during construction, which can be dis- played on both the vehicle terminal and the remote monitoring client. This interface displays the relative distance between the bucket tip and the underground pipe in real-time. Fig. 9(c) shows the bucket automatic tracking display interface at the vehicle ter- minal. The view is set from the top view and left view of the bucket. When the excavator enters the risk area, the color of the underground pipes model will change. The yellow indicates that the excavator has entered the low-risk area, and the red indicates that the excavator has entered the high-risk area, and the Pad will give a voice warning. At the same time, the server will send a message to the field managers. After the excavation task is completed, the system administra- tor can replay the excavation process and inquire about the bucket tip trajectory and working position trajectory at the remote mon- itoring client. As shown in Fig. 10(a), the system can replay the excavation process in a period before and after the alarm and can assist the field management person to analyze the warning reason. Fig. 10(b) shows a graphical report of the bucket tip trajectory of the excavator for a digging operation. In this report, for every 10 cm movement of the bucket tip coordinate, the system draws a 3D space point with different colors according to the area of the bucket tip. The bucket tip trajectory is displayed in green in the safe area, yellow in the low-risk area, and red in the high-risk area to visually reflect the field excavation. During excavation, the ex- cavator bucket is mostly in a safe area. When the bucket enters the risk area, the system will give a warning in advance, effectively avoiding the collision between the bucket and the underground pipes. Fig. 10(c) is a graphical report of the working position track of an excavator within 40 minutes, which can plot the excavator working track and provide data support for subsequent mechanical efficiency analysis. The project needs to replace the old underground pipes, the de- sign elevation of the trench is 50cm above the original underground pipes. The system counts the minimum relative distance between the bucket tip and the underground pipes every 1 m, and the re- sults of K 2 þ 150-K2 þ 200 and K3 þ 750-K3 þ 800 sections are shown in Fig. 11. It can be seen that during the construction, the average elevation of the trench is 50.44 cm away from the underground pipe, indicating that the construction quality meets the design requirements. Through model visual guidance and voice warning, the minimum distance between the bucket tip and the underground pipe is 23.67cm, ensuring the safety and reliability of field construction. Fig. 9.
