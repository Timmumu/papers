Brillouin fiber optic sensors and mobile augmented reality-based digital twins for quantitative safety assessment of underground pipelines Minghao Li, Xin Feng * , Yang Han Faculty of Infrastructure Engineering, Dalian University of Technology, Dalian, China ARTICLE INFO Keywords: Underground pipelines Distributed fiber optic sensors Digital twin Mobile augmented reality (MAR) Joint data – physics driven model Structural safety assessment ABSTRACT The impossibility of visual inspection and the complexity of combined loads hamper the quantitative assessment, lifetime prediction and control of underground pipelines during their lifecycles. A methodology based on mobile augmented reality (MAR) and Brillouin fiber optic sensors (BFOSs) is presented to build a digital twin (DT) for underground pipelines. Field experiments were carried out to demonstrate that the proposed method can quantitatively assess and predict the structural safety of an underground pipeline from the DT. The results demonstrate that the distributed sensor networks can measure important but unpredictable deformations (i.e., longitudinal bending and axial thermal strain), the joint data-physics driven model can estimate the structural stress state more accurately than the common calculation model, and the MAR-based human – asset interaction interface enables more intuitive, efficient, automated operation and maintenance (O & M). In the future, in-line robotic systems and localized damage models should be further adopted for lifecycle O & M. 1. Introduction Underground pipelines are widely used engineering assets, and their safety management is easily neglected because they are not visible and direct structural damage inspection is difficult [ 1 ]. However, with the rapid growth of underground infrastructure assets and the deterioration of existing structures over time, the increasing number of public emer - gencies caused by underground pipelines have brought safety manage - ment to the forefront [ 2 ]. Underground pipelines have a complex spatial distribution and are controlled by different departments. In addition, there are significant differences in the structural states of pipelines caused by specific operating conditions, such as foundation settlement and internal fluid pressure/temperature. Therefore, the digitalization and automation of safety management for underground pipelines is still a challenging issue. As a next-generation technology that can integrate a series of advanced technologies (e.g., sensors, modeling and visualization), emerging digital twin (DT) technology, which is considered to be an efficient intelligence solution for digitalization and automation, uses digital models to dynamically monitor, describe, simulate, predict and control physical counterparts in a timely manner [ 3 – 5 ]. Recently, DT technology has drawn much attention from both the academic and in - dustrial communities [ 6 ]. Gartner predicted that 50% of large industrial corporations would use DT technology by 2021, corresponding to a 10% improvement in effectiveness [ 7 ]. Since the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) gave the first specific definition in 2012 [ 8 ], the DT concept has gradually been extended from the field of in - dustrial manufacturing to construction engineering and from theoretical research to application practice [ 9 – 13 ]. At the asset scale, Lin et al. [ 14 ] proposed a DT-based collapse fragility assessment method for long-span cable-stayed bridges during strong earthquakes. Mohammadi and Taylor [ 15 ] introduced a smart city DT paradigm that revolutionized human - – infrastructure interactions and urban planning through the Internet of Things (IoT) and virtual reality (VR). Lu et al. [ 10 , 16 ] presented a sys - tem architecture for DT technology that is specifically designed at the building and city levels and developed a DT demonstrator at the West Cambridge site of the University of Cambridge in the UK. Although each of these cases has its own unique definitions and requirements [ 5 ], a successful DT paradigm relies on (1) the mutual interaction between the physical asset and its DT, which is related to high-performance sensor technologies and high-fidelity data communication; (2) DT modeling approaches that should focus on both geometrical appearance and the ability to carry out asset-specific analysis, prediction, optimization and decision-making support; and (3) a human – asset interaction interface presented to the end users, which should improve the ability to under - stand the unique characteristics of an existing physical asset and be * Corresponding author. In pipeline engineering, Siemens proposed the Pipeline 4.0 concept [ 17 ]. Pipeline 4.0 utilizes DT technology to explore innovative ap - proaches to further reduce the operating expenses of pipeline systems with no risk or cost. In the current phase, Pipeline 4.0 mainly focuses on developing DTs for pumping stations rather than pipelines. PetroChina has adopted digital recovery techniques, including aerial photogram - metry, 3D laser scanning, 3D terrain construction, oblique photogram - metry and digital 3D modeling, to build the DT of the China – Myanmar oil and gas pipeline [ 18 ]. Stennikov et al. [ 19 ] presented an object- oriented computer modeling technology to construct the DTs of pipe - line systems for online hydraulic calculations. Lieberman et al. [ 20 ] discussed the potential benefits of using big data analytics and IoT principles to develop functional DTs of underground infrastructures and improve livability and resilience in modern cities. Carvalho [ 21 ] pre - sented a DT framework combining building information modeling (BIM) and asset management (AM) techniques to support the maintenance and operation of water and wastewater utility assets. According to a litera - ture survey, pipeline DT technology is still in its infancy, and there are few reports on using DTs for the structural safety assessment of under - ground pipelines. The application of DTs to underground pipelines faces the following challenges. (1) The sensor network technique underpins the mutual in - teractions between DTs and hidden buried assets. Although nonde - structive test (NDT)-based in-line inspection (ILI) tools, including magnetic flux leakage (MFL), ultrasonic wall measurement (UT) and remote field eddy current (RFEC) tools, are commonly adopted in oil and gas pipelines, they are local inspection techniques rather than real-time sensing techniques. However, these methods cannot measure the stress states along a pipeline. The supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) system uses only sparse low-sampling transducers to monitor the internal pressure, flow rate and temperature parameters of pipelines; however, this system is unable to provide information regarding struc - tural deterioration during a lifecycle. The comprehensive sensing of the structural state, including both stress/deformation and deterioration in the pipeline, is recognized as one of the most critical issues for pipeline DTs. (2) Human – asset interactions and visualization are vital for un - derground pipelines since they are not visible in the buried environ - ment. The state of the art is summarized in Table 1 . Augmented reality (AR) is considered to be a friendly human – asset interaction technique due to its fast communication, 3D visualization and beyond-reality interaction for underground pipelines. However, the current AR-based modeling and visualization methods mainly focus on the geometric attribute information (design and construction phases) rather than the dynamic evolution of structural states (operation and maintenance (O & M) phase). During the O & M phase, which covers > 80% of the lifecycle, pipelines suffer from corrosion and load or operating condition changes, resulting in the dynamic evolution and deterioration of the structural state over time. Therefore, the current studies lack a modeling and interaction approach for the dynamic evolution of the pipeline structural state to guide lifecycle safety management. To address these challenges, a framework for DTs is presented in this study for underground pipelines by using mobile augmented reality (MAR) and Brillouin fiber optic sensors (BFOSs). BFOSs, in addition to IoT-based pressure and temperature sensors, constitute distributed sensor networks, which are used to comprehensively sense the structural stress states of underground pipelines. Then, a joint data-physics driven model is presented to achieve dynamic modeling of the evolution and deterioration of specific asset structures. A MAR-based human – asset interaction interface is proposed to visualize geometric attributes, real- time monitoring data and structural safety assessment results. The feasibility of the proposed methodology for automated safety assessment was validated by experimental investigations in a real pipe network. 2. Methodology development Underground pipelines are not visible and are large-scale. These pipelines are subjected to complex combined loads and inevitably suffer from structural deterioration during long-term service. A framework of a DT system based on MAR and BFOSs is proposed for quantitative safety assessment and O & M decision-making support. As shown in Fig. 1 , the DT of an underground pipeline has three main components: distributed sensor networks oriented toward the physical pipeline, a joint data - – physics driven model oriented toward the DT, and a human – asset interaction interface based on MAR oriented toward safety inspectors. Based on this framework, the methodology is presented in detail as follows. It should be noted that only continuous metallic pipelines are investigated in this study, and jointed pipelines (e.g., bell-and-spigot cast ‑ iron and concrete pipes) are beyond the scope of the proposed approach. 2.1. Circumferential breaks are mainly caused by longitudinal stress due to thermal contraction, soil settlement or voids, and third-party interfer - ence. Longitudinal breaks are the result of excessive hoop stress caused by internal pressure, soil cover, frost load, structural deterioration, etc. Current design codes clearly present a calculation model for hoop stress from external loads (soil overburden and traffic load) and internal pressure [ 34 , 35 ]. The thermal axial stress in a continuous pipeline is also considered in the model. Rajani et al. [ 36 ] developed a pipe – soil inter - action analytical model for the longitudinal stress of jointed pipelines. This model accounts for temperature differentials and explains the frequently observed occurrence of circumferential breaks in pipelines. Notably, the longitudinal bending stress due to foundation defects (settlements and voids) or third-party interference is not considered in the model because these events cannot be predicted in advance. During long-term service, the longitudinal bending actions induced by foun - dation defects and third-party interference may occur with unforeseen magnitudes and at arbitrary times and locations during the lifecycle. However, third-party interference and foundation defects are the second and third greatest contributions to pipeline failures [ 37 – 39 ], respectively. As indicated earlier, longitudinal bending stress may occur arbi - trarily along pipelines. The commonly used discrete point sensors, i.e., strain gauges and fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors, can only measure the structural strain/stress in limited specific locations. This contradicts the fact that the locations of the longitudinal actions on a pipeline cannot be determined in advance. As a cutting-edge sensing technique, Brillouin backscattering-based fiber optic sensors can provide real distributed measurements for strain as well as temperature with a sensing range of up to a few tens of kilometers [ 40 – 43 ]. BFOSs are ideal candidates for monitoring pipelines due to their large spatial range and relatively small spatial resolution [ 44 ]. Inaudi and Glisic [ 45 ] reported several significant field application examples using BFOSs, including leakage detection for brine and gas pipelines and strain and deformation monitoring for gas pipelines. Mobile augmented reality visualization AR is considered to be an effective and fast communication and interaction tool that can visualize virtual objects [ 79 , 80 ]. However, current AR alignment methods, such as quick response (QR) markers [ 81 ], simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) [ 82 , 83 ] and global positioning systems (GPSs) [ 28 ], have two main limitations: (1) the positioning accuracy of currently commercially available SLAM systems and GPSs is far from sufficient for practical engineering requirements, and (2) the application of QR markers is limited to the tabletop level, far from the city level. In addition, the computer vision (CV)-based method [ 84 ] is affected by the environment and is mainly used for the appear - ance of buildings rather than for hidden pipelines. With the rapid development of AR-enabled software (e.g., ARCore, ARKit) and micro - global navigation satellite system (GNSS) surveying receivers with centimeter-level location accuracy (e.g., Lite RTK from Qianxun Spatial Intelligence, Fig. 5 (b)), AR-GIS technology can be used to achieve pre - cise mapping of spatial locations between DTs and physical assets in the field ( Fig. 5 ). This solution features lightweight equipment, good pre - cision, environmental robustness and large-scale applicability. As shown in Fig. 5 , GNSSs (e.g., GPS or BeiDou) are used to send signals for navigation and location. Ground-based augmentation sys - tems are responsible for collecting and processing satellite data and providing calibration data to the GNSS receiver in real time. GNSS receivers are used to receive high-precision positioning coordinates using the global positioning system fix data (GGA) format [ 85 ]. The mobile app obtains precise positioning coordinates from GNSS receivers in real time via a Bluetooth connection so that the Mapbox map layers are perfectly aligned with the physical world. In addition, ARCore/ ARKit's plane anchoring capability (the so-called ARPlaneAnchor object [ 28 ]) can be used to “ seamlessly ” overlay DTs with physical assets. This system continuously searches for feature points to identify the plane of the surroundings and creates ARPlaneAnchor objects to anchor the Mapbox map layers. Alignment of the orientation angle is carried out using a combination of the electronic compass of a mobile device and manual rotation of the map layers [ 24 ]. Location-based human – asset interaction is achieved by the above approach. 3. Sensor networks and instrumentation The modeling parameters of the pipeline networks were collected from the design documents and as-built surveys. The digital models were then built at the system level. As the pilot demonstration at the unit level for structural safety assessment, GasPipe_6 and HeatingPipe_2 (as shown in Fig. 6 and Table 2 ) were selected to have distributed sensor networks installed for real-time monitoring of the structural states. These pipeline segments are located below roads with high pedestrian and vehicular traffic. BFOSs were used to monitor the longitudinal strain along the pipeline segments. The commercially available NZS-DSS-C02 and NZS- DTS-C05 were mounted on the pipeline to measure the strain and tem - perature based on the sensor topology presented in Section 2.1. The sensing fiber core of these sensors is a common SMF-28 single-mode optical fiber with a sensing fiber. Commercially available fiber optic sensors were used in this study. The strain and temperature coefficients were provided by the vender. These coefficients were calibrated by laboratory tests before field application. During sensor installation, the pipe surface was cleaned to remove dirt and grease. Then, high- performance epoxy resin was applied on the top and two sides of the strain sensor to ensure that it was firmly bonded to the surface of the pipe [ 49 ]. During this process, the strain sensor was slightly preten - sioned to keep it straight along the longitudinal axis of the pipe. Thus, good strain transfer was achieved, and the readings of the strain sensor represented the longitudinal strain of the pipeline. Neubrex NBX-6050A was adopted for real-time data acquisition from the BFOSs. The spatial resolution and sampling interval were set to 10 cm and 5 cm, respectively, and the measurement accuracy was ± 10 με . These BFOSs were arranged along HeatingPipe_2 from the fixed support to the compensator, with a total measurement length of 57.7 m. The mea - surement length of GasPipe_6 was 61.3 m, and the BFOSs were arranged from the south fixed support to the north fixed support ( Fig. 6 ). Thus, distributed measurements could be obtained along the sensor path. To monitor the internal pressure and temperature, SITRANS P DS III digital pressure transmitters and DS18B20 digital temperature sensors were placed on the pipeline. The SITRANS P DS III digital pressure trans - mitters were connected to a personal computer (PC) through an analog- to-digital (A/D) converter, while the DS18B20 digital temperature sensors were connected to the PC via an RS-422 data interface. The sampling frequency was 1 Hz. All monitoring data were transmitted to the cloud platform. 3.1.2. Introduction Cable-supported bridges, as crucial transportation infrastructure, face significant threats to their functional and structural safety over their service life due to material degradation and various other influencing factors, including traffic overloads, vehicle or ship collisions, fatigue cracking, steel corrosion, fires, typhoons, earthquakes, and especially the ever-increasing traffic demands and changing weather conditions [1] . Garg et al. [2] analyzed the failure causes of 2010 bridges from 1977 to 2017 and found that natural disasters accounted for 80.30 %, material degradation for 10.10 %, traffic overloads for 3.28 %, and human-induced disasters for 2.19 %. In reality, there are numerous specific cases of bridge damage. For example, the Humber Bridge in the UK, which experienced excessive wear of its bearings due to extreme loads [3] , the Martinus Nijhoff Cable-Stayed Bridge in the Netherlands, which showed fatigue damage much earlier than expected during its design service life [4] , and the Jiangyin Suspension Bridge in China, which suffered from the failure of expansion joints due to traffic loads [5] . Therefore, long-term and effective health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges throughout their service life are crucial. Traditional monitoring and assessment methods for cable-supported bridges have met certain demands but still face chal - lenges or issues [6 – 8] . Currently, digital twin (DT) has become a research hotspot [9 – 11] due to its unique advantages and is widely used for monitoring, pre - dictive maintenance, and analysis of various physical assets. DT can be considered as a virtual model that represents an object throughout its lifecycle, maintaining its state, properties, and operational dynamics. Background 2.1. Cable-supported bridges Cable-supported bridges, primarily consisting of suspension bridges and cable-stayed bridges, represent one of the most significant in - vestments in the national transportation network. The main components include towers, main cable/stay cables, and the deck. Fig. 1 illustrates schematic diagrams of both suspension bridge and cable-stayed bridge. Although cable-supported bridges account for only about 1.5 % of all bridges, they are an effective structural form for large spans and intri - cate environments [13 – 15] . In bridge categories with spans exceeding 200 m, cable-supported bridges account for over 90 % [16] . With a service life exceeding 100 years, cable-supported bridges may undergo material aging, traffic loads [17] , hurricanes or typhoons [14] , earth - quakes [18] , fires [16] , etc., during their lifespan, which can signifi - cantly impact the functionality and structural safety of the bridges. 2.2. Methods of health monitoring and condition assessment Ensuring the safety and uninterrupted operation of cable-supported bridges is a key concern for stakeholders. Health monitoring and con - dition assessment of these bridges are essential processes for addressing the aforementioned issues. There are various traditional methods for health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges. The most basic method, and one adopted by most countries, is visual inspection for bridge condition assessment. However, visual inspection involves extensive manual labor and is prone to neglecting defects [6,8] . Bridge testing can inspect the overall structure or major components of a bridge, providing valuable data support for bridge monitoring and assessment. But this method typically requires bridge closures, which can have certain traffic and economic impacts. Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) is described as the application of sensing technology and analysis of structural characteristics to identify structural damage or degradation [19] . The development of SHM has facilitated the advancement of health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges [20 – 25] , enabling real-time diagnosis of the health status of both local components and the overall structure of Fig. 1. Schematic diagram of the main types of cable-stayed bridges. (a) Suspension bridge, and (b) Cable-stayed bridge. Z. Zhang et al. Structures 79 (2025) 109448 2 bridges [26] . However, SHM still faces challenges such as poor inter - operability, data loss, low integration, low query efficiency and inability of sensor networks to comprehensively monitor the actual structural response [24] . Building Information Modeling (BIM) creates a virtual three- dimensional model and utilizes digital technology to provide a comprehensive, reality-aligned information database for construction projects [27] . BIM plays a crucial role in promoting digitization, visu - alization, and data exchange throughout the entire lifecycle of bridges [28] . However, it faces challenges such as lack of unified standards [29, 30] , poor data interoperability [31] , high hardware requirements for model operation [32] , and high technical barriers [33 – 36] . In recent years, data-driven technologies have been applied to the monitoring and assessment of cable-supported bridges [14,37 – 39] , using data as the center for decision-making and action to achieve prediction and assessment of bridge performance. However, this method has high re - quirements for data quantity and quality and may also present risks of overfitting and poor interpretability [14] . 2.3. Digital twin 2.3.1. Origin and definition of digital twin In recent years, DT has been widely used in various fields [40] for the monitoring, analysis, and predictive maintenance of numerous physical assets, including buildings [41 – 43] , factories, ships, cranes, aircraft, cities [44] , and railways [45,46] . DT allows for personalized de - scriptions of physical assets in the form of computational models or parameters of interest, which evolve over time and maintain synchro - nization with the physical entities via data collection devices. The origin of the DT can be traced back to 2002, when Grieves [47, 48] proposed a conceptual model in a speech and used it in a course at the University of Michigan in 2003. Although the term "Digital Twin" did not appear in the model, it already encompassed all the essential ele - ments of a DT, including real space, virtual space, bidirectional connection between real space and virtual space via data or information flow [49] . Therefore, it is widely recognized as the earliest introduction of the concept or idea of DT [50] . In 2010, the term "Digital Twin" was first coined by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) [51] , along with its formal definition. Although DT has gained increasing popularity in academic research and industry practice, there is no universally accepted definition for it. Scholars in different fields have proposed various interpretations and definitions of DT. By synthesizing these previous definitions, this paper proposes the following definition of DT in the context of health moni - toring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges: a high- fidelity digital virtual representation of a cable-supported bridge, inte - grated with multiple technologies, capable of real-time data interaction with the physical entity and autonomous model updating, providing decision support and critical services for the health monitoring and condition assessment of the physical entity. 2.3.2. Digital twin characteristics Fig. 2 illustrates the DT framework, which comprises three essential elements [47,52] : (1) the physical entity in the physical environment; (2) the DT in the virtual environment; and (3) the bidirectional connection between the physical entity and the DT, represented by ar - rows in the figure, with arrow directions indicating the direction of the connection. The "Collect" connection in Fig. 2 denotes the process of collecting data from the physical entity. Due to the vast amount of collected data and the possibility of some data being invalid or having indistinct features, data processing is necessary, corresponding to the "Process" connection. The "Feedback" connection indicates that the DT model can provide reverse feedback on the data after its establishment. The "Service" connection represents various services offered by the DT, such as simulation, decision-making, control, health monitoring, con - dition assessment, and data-driven operations of the physical entity, aimed at enhancing system convenience, reliability, and productivity. DT possesses capabilities such as high integration and intelligence, real-time bidirectional data transmission [53] , autonomous and rapid model updating [54] , and the ability to reconstruct stress fields in areas lacking measurement data [24] . These capabilities effectively comple - ment the deficiencies of traditional monitoring and assessment methods, assisting stakeholders in making reliable and swift decisions. Conse - quently, this enhances bridge performance, extends service life, and ultimately reduces maintenance and operational costs for the infrastructure. 2.3.3. Concept clarification There are multiple concepts similar to the DT, such as Simulation (SIM), Building Information Modeling (BIM), Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), and the Internet of Things (IoT). These similar concepts can easily be confused with the DT. Therefore, comparing these concepts with the DT can help to clarify the distinctions between them. While the DT may seem similar to SIM, it is far more than that. A DT is a real-time, high-fidelity depiction of the operational dynamics of its corresponding physical entity. Operational dynamics are a crucial aspect of DT because its behavior is grounded in near-real-time data sourced from the actual physical entity. SIM emphasizes potential occurrences in the real world, rather than what is currently happening. A DT can serve multiple purposes such as monitoring, control, assessment, diagnosis, and prediction, extending beyond mere simulation. BIM serves as a digital depiction of the physical and functional at - tributes of a building object [55] . Although both BIM and DT rely on virtual models, BIM emphasizes the existing physical entity it corre - sponds to, while DT has the capability to depict something that is nonexistent or yet to be built. Additionally, DT must reflect the current state of the physical entity in a timely manner, which is not a require - ment for BIM [56] . Discussion on digital twin applications This section systematically reviews 19 practical applications of DT in health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges. These applications are categorized into real-world structures ( Section 5.1 ) and physical scaled models ( Section 5.2 ). The case studies collec - tively demonstrate the versatility of DT frameworks in integrating diverse technologies. These technologies include laser scanning, IoT sensors, BIM, machine learning, and finite element modeling. They enable real-time monitoring, predictive analytics, and condition evalu - ation. Key observations are as follows. (1) While all studies leverage DT principles, methodologies vary significantly in data acquisition (e.g., contact sensors vs. UAV- based imaging), modeling techniques (FEM vs. data-driven sur - rogates), and service outputs (fatigue prediction vs. seismic assessment). This reflects the context-specific adaptability of DTs but highlights a lack of standardized workflows. (2) Applications to full-scale bridges (e.g., Clifton Suspension Bridge, Yangtze River Bridge) validate DT robustness under operational conditions, yet computational demands for high-fidelity models remain challenging. In contrast, physical models (e.g., Sutong Bridge prototype) facilitate controlled validation but may not fully capture complex environmental interactions. (3) Few studies fully exploit bidirectional DT capabilities; most emphasize data-to-model synchronization but seldom use model- to-physical feedback for autonomous control or adaptive main - tenance. Additionally, interoperability between legacy moni - toring systems and emerging DT architectures requires further attention. These findings underscore DT’s transformative potential in enhancing bridge resilience but also reveal critical research needs, i.e., standardization of modular DT frameworks, validation of long-term autonomy under extreme events, and scalable solutions for legacy infrastructure integration. 6. The three-layer digital twin framework Through compiling and analyzing existing research, this paper pro - poses a three-layer framework for DT, as shown in Fig. 6 . This frame - work is applicable to the field of health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges, clearly presenting the content and processes of the DT system. It provides a reference for the research and application of DT in cable-supported bridges. Furthermore, the DT constructed within this framework incorporates the advantages of both the finite element model and the data-driven model, enabling it to better serve the physical entity. This framework consists of three layers: the data layer, the model layer, and the service layer. The data layer encompasses data collection, transmission, and pro - cessing, serving as the most fundamental layer within the three-layer framework. Cable-supported bridges requiring health monitoring and condition assessment typically possess historical documentation, including design, construction, inspection, and decision-making mate - rials. Furthermore, a SHM system is generally designed and installed on the bridge during its construction phase to facilitate subsequent moni - toring activities during operation. Therefore, data collection can rely on both historical documentation and the SHM system. To obtain as much bridge data as possible, instruments utilizing technologies such as cameras, UVAs, lasers, and radars can be deployed on cable-supported bridges as supplementary devices, in addition to traditional sensors. Data transmission employs a combination of wired and wireless methods, tailored to the actual conditions of the bridge, to achieve the most suitable data transmission arrangement. Data processing begins with pre-processing large volumes of data, including de-noising, outlier detection, cleaning, and normalization. Subsequently, further process - ing is conducted based on the detailed requirements of operation man - agers. Finally, the processed data is stored in a database for future use. The model layer encompasses model construction and model updating. The DT model is constructed using a combined approach of finite element modeling and data-driven modeling, harnessing the ad - vantages of both methods. For cable-supported bridges with ample historical documentation, such as sizes, positions, and material prop - erties, the bridge model can be established using finite element software Abaqus [109] . For cable-supported bridges with limited historical documentation, point cloud and image data from the database can be utilized. Specifically, point cloud data is first converted into a mesh model using surface reconstruction algorithms, then transformed into the INP file format acceptable by Abaqus to obtain the bridge’s Abaqus model. Subsequently, feature extraction is performed on the image data to obtain key data, which can be used for setting boundary conditions and subsequent simulation analysis in the bridge’s Abaqus model. This finite element model offers advantages such as good visualization and ease of simulation. In addition to the finite element method, data-driven modeling is also employed as a complement to the finite element model. It utilizes Python software [110] and some cutting-edge, efficient algorithms and programs to establish a data-driven model of the cable-supported bridge. When dealing with complex modeling situations or when the service provided by the finite element model is unsatisfactory, the data-driven Z. Zhang et al. A key characteristic of DT is the real-time connection and data transmission between the virtual model and the physical entity, which requires the virtual model to be updated in real-time. This framework provides an approach for updating the virtual model, which is based on the real-time updated database and achieves automated updating of finite element model parameters and data-driven model parameters through Python scripts. Specifically, Py - thon scripts are used to read data from the database in real-time and automatically modify the input file (INP file) of the Abaqus model to achieve real-time updating of the finite element model. Python scripts are also employed for recalculating and optimizing parameters in the data-driven model to achieve real-time updating. The service layer refers to the services provided by the DT to the physical entity, including monitoring, prediction, simulation, assess - ment, decision and management. These six services are all crucial for health monitoring and condition assessment of cable-supported bridges. They can be categorized into basic services and advanced services. The basic services include monitoring, prediction, and simulation, which provide essential data and analysis support for understanding the cur - rent and future behavior of the bridge. The advanced services include assessment, decision making, and management, which build on the basic services to support complex operational and management activities. Challenges and future trends 7.1. Challenges The application of DT may encounter some challenges. According to the results of the literature review in this paper, the following challenges may be faced. In terms of data management, higher-precision and durable sensors are required for data collection and data loss should be minimized. When collecting huge amounts of data, low latency and high reliability must be ensured during data transmission and processing. In terms of modeling, the real-time performance and fidelity of existing virtual models are not outstanding. Virtual models need to reflect physical entities in a more real-time and high-fidelity manner. Additionally, model updating and fusion require further enhancement. In terms of security and privacy, while DT may need to be connected to a network to exhibit its excellent characteristics, a large amount of data may face the risks of network threats and attacks, as well as data theft or tampering, necessitating strengthened supervision and control. In terms of service applications, the application of DT in cable- supported bridges is still limited, and more engineering practice and testing are needed to better serve health monitoring, condition assess - ment, and early warning. In terms of intelligence, scholars and stakeholders expect DT to be a system that can autonomously, adaptively, and self-evolvingly perform condition assessment, prediction, and decision-making based on collected data. However, current DT still requires human intervention, and advanced technologies need to further enhance its intelligence. In terms of integration, DT involves the integration of multiple technologies and disciplines, and how to efficiently and fully integrate these systems remains a challenge. In terms of standardization, there is currently a lack of unified standards in the bridge field to guide the construction of DT systems and the evaluation of model quality. 7.2. Introduction Over the last decade, the architecture, engineering, construction, and operations (AECO) industry has made substantial investments in digital transformation. Building information modeling (BIM) has become the mainstream modeling tool, Internet of Things (IoT) sen- sors continuously capture on-site process data, and digital twin (DT) technologies are increasingly used for visualization of geometry, progress, and operations (Afzal et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024b). Governments and industry associations in various countries have successively issued digital delivery and information management guidelines to promote data collection and sharing throughout the entire project life cycle. For example, the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development of the People’s Republic of China (2022) introduced the 14th Five-Year Plan for the Development of the Construction Industry, and a German-based consulting firm (Roland Berger 2016) published the report Digitization in the Con- struction Industry: Building Europe’s Road to “Construction 4.0,” which outlines the digital transformation of the construction sector. However, multiple international surveys have indicated that the direct cost of rework still accounts for 11%–15% of total project investment, while claims and schedule delays caused by quality defects are even on the rise ( Love et al. 2009; Olanrewaju 2025). This phenomenon highlights that despite the growing availability of digital tools and the accumulation of quality data, construction quality issues have not declined. Although current quality management practices incorporate risk control measures (risk identification, inspections, sampling, and approvals), significant gaps persist in linking abstract risks to concrete events, tracking the rapid evolution of risks, and ensuring the implementation of proactive measures ( Guo et al. 2025; Jeon et al. 2023; Mohamad and Tran 2021). Quality management benefits from the mature domain of safety in construction, which distin- guishes among risk, hazard, and accident and develops proactive identification methods (Chen et al. 2025b; Li et al. 2018; Pereira et al. 2018 ; Yang et al. 2012 ). However, unlike safety, quality is an intrinsic project attribute that extends beyond construction completion or handover (Bowen et al. 2002; Mallawaarachchi and Senaratne 2015). 04026105-1© ASCE J. Due to its long duration and complexity, the mechanism of quality defect formation and evolution does not fully align with the hazard–accident discontinuous mechanism in the safety field. The formation of defects may involve a long time span and be influenced by the cumulative effects of quality deviations, while also being triggered by various events. This includes both the risk– incident disruptive effect ( He and Atangana Njock 2023 ; Okonta et al. 2024) and the risk–(deviation/defect trigger)–defect gradual effect (Faqih and Zayed 2021 ; Ma et al. 2021). The former has a severe impact on quality incidents and safety, and the latter affects quality attributes over the long term, extending into the operational phase and throughout the building’s life cycle. However, this cou- pled mechanism is underexplored in existing research, and current quality hazard inspections in practice, being experience-driven and checklist-based, struggle to capture finer-grained hazard elements, including defect triggers and deviations. Although current individual innovations and technologies, such as defect detection (Guo et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2023), quality data integration (Kovacs and Micsik 2021; Yan et al. 2025b), and design specification checks (Ma et al. 2024; Sydora and Stroulia 2020), are well-developed and capable of addressing existing quality issues, they often are not related to preventive measures and interpretabil- ity. These approaches tend to emphasize the identification of issues at later stages rather than addressing the underlying latent factors that could trigger defects. Technically, the challenge lies in developing methods that can capture proactive measures and provide the necessary inter- pretability for early-stage quality hazard identification in con- struction projects. This requires addressing the complexities of integrating diverse data sources, such as model files [(Industry Foundation Class (IFC)/BIM metadata], real-time sensor readings (e.g., concrete strength, temperature–humidity, vibration records), and inspection reports (PDF scans of concrete-cube tests and rebar reinspection results) to event and decision logs (modifica- tion orders, and image records) ( Choi and Lee 2022 ; Iqbal et al. 2024; Tuhaise et al. 2023 ). Current DT development provides a solid foundation for integrating and presenting both structured and unstructured quality data with model views ( Cai et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024b ; Ramonell et al. 2023 ; Yan et al. 2025b ), and can incorporate checking algorithms to simulate the accumulation of deviations and improve the early-stage identification of defects. Therefore, this study focuses on investigating the underlying mech- anisms of quality defects and adapting application technologies to develop new methods, aiming to achieve the following objectives: 1. Define the concept of quality defect triggers, explore the risk– (deviation/defect trigger)–defect–incident evolutionary mecha- nism with an emphasis on the precursory nature of defect triggers and deviations, and clarify their identification characteristics at a finer granularity. 2. Develop a hierarchical framework that integrates construction processes, quality standards, quality data, and quality defect triggers, and apply ontology-based approaches to model their relationships, ensuring contextual adaptation. 3. Integrate multisource heterogeneous quality data and construct quality linked data (QLD) for identifying defect triggers within the context of construction DT, facilitating more precise and contextually relevant identification. Related Work Quality Issue Evolution Mechanism and Defect Trigger Identification In the construction field, quality management can draw on the more mature domain of safety management, which offers analytical perspectives for distinguishing among risk, hazard, and accident. According to reviews on safety management in construction (Alkaissy et al. 2020; Sousa et al. 2014; Zhou et al. 2015), early safety research began with accident analysis studies that examined numerous accident cases and developed causation models (e.g., Abdelhamid and Everett 2000; Suraji et al. 2001) and statistical analyses (e.g., Huang and Hinze 2003; Shin et al. 2018). Subsequently, the focus shifted to accident prevention studies such as design for safety (e.g., Behm 2005; Yang et al. 2012), and later progressed toward risk evaluation research (Li et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2021). Previous construction safety studies have recognized two stages in elaborating the mechanisms underlying the evolution of safety issues. The first stage, i.e., the context-general stage, conceptualizes the relationships among risk, hazard, and accident at a macro level. Early safety research, based on accident investigations, sought to develop generalizable risk management theories (Patterson and Neailey 2002). Here, risk precedes hazard, defined abstractly as the uncertainty affecting objectives, a latent indicator of potential hazards (Albert et al. 2020), consistent with general risk management standards such as ISO 31000:2009 (ISO 2009) (Sui Pheng and Wee 2001). The second, context-specific stage, defines these relationships at a micro level, emphasizing how specific hazards and risks emerge within project environments. In this stage, hazard precedes risk, as identifiable domain hazards (Carter and Smith 2006; Perlman et al. 2014) are used to assess risk through probability and consequence severity. Unlike progress or safety, quality is an intrinsic attribute of a project that does not end with the completion of construction or the handover of the project. Because of its long time span and the complexity of influencing factors, the current mechanism of quality defect identification is more analogous to the first stage in safety research. Fig. 1 illustrates the underlying mechanism of quality issue evolution, developed with reference to the mature frameworks established in safety management (Carter and Smith 2006). According to the evolution mechanisms of defect triggers, a quality deviation may constitute a defect trigger when its magni- tude closely approaches the tolerance limits defined by relevant standards or when its frequency becomes abnormally high. Specifically, a quality deviation exceeding the required value is classified as a quality defect. When the deviation exceeds the reference value but remains within the required value, it is considered a defect trigger. Additionally, if the frequency of a deviation surpasses the reference rate, it may also be identified as a defect trigger due to its cumulative impact. Introduction Dynamic and complex construction sites including incomplete structures and unsecured resources, are among the most vulnerable environments to extreme wind events [ 1 ]. Severe wind-induced dam - ages significantly attenuate the efficiency of construction projects by causing considerable schedule delays and further negatively impact neighboring infrastructures in operations (e.g., roads, power grids), and thus trigger notable disruptions and financial losses in communities [ 1 , 2 ]. For instance, along with around 50 billion dollars in damage and more than 250 fatalities, Hurricane Sandy has caused over 185 million dollars ’ worth of damage to the construction project of the World Trade Center [ 3 , 4 ]. Severe wind-induced disruptions could be classified in three folds: (1) structural or mechanical failures such as tower crane collapses due to excessive wind loads [ 5 – 7 ]; (2) functional failure such as the inability to make progress due to construction suspension and supply chain disruption before and after extreme weather events [ 8 ]; and (3) (cascading) damages due to the devastating impact of potential wind-borne debris that is imposed to construction sites (as well as neighboring communities including critical infrastructure systems) [ 9 , 10 ]. The wind-borne debris in construction sites pose a substantial risk as unsecured resources could easily become projectiles during extreme wind events, and cause mass casualty incidents or induce damages to critical infrastructure systems in operation [ 1 , 11 , 12 ]. For example, eyewitness accounts indicate a total loss of 7.5 million dollars in damage to the four-story hotel construction site in Batavia, IL due to the aftermath of an extreme wind event [ 13 ]. Additionally, the charlotte county in Miami, FL has warned that unsecured construction materials such as plywoods and portable toilets in jobsites are among the most common projectiles in the case of extreme wind events [ 14 ]. Local contractors in the charlotte harbor area pointed out that they need to effectively remove loose materials and tie them down as the impact of such debris to the neighboring communities would be substantial [ 15 ]. Meanwhile, according to a report from the United States Department of Homeland Security, an estimated cost for hauling and removal of 200,000 cubic yards of debris for Hurricane Irma was estimated at around 1.4 billion dollars [ 15 , 16 ]. The majority of the debris was clas - sified as construction-related materials or vegetation that became airborne during hurricanes, and it is noted that the associated hauling process took several months to complete by the local government [ 15 ]. While concurrent building codes and ordinances support designing wind-resilient structures with respect to severe weather conditions, construction companies are responsible for regulating and implement - ing hurricane preparedness plans to better protect their projects during * Corresponding author. Preparedness plans involve a list of activities that are geared towards mitigating wind-induced damage in projects [17], and such emergency operating procedures are typically based on the experiences and expertise of practitioners in companies. Meanwhile, over the years, emerging technologies in visual sensing and analytics have demonstrated a great potential to streamline the management task of practitioners in construction projects [19–26]. For example, the convenience of commercial-level unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) encourages practitioners to collect large-scale visual data to keep the as-is record of construction sites [20,21,27]. In addition to images from the ground level, aerial imagery collected from UAVs can provide an overhead view of resources in jobsites, which can assist with surveying and mapping [28], progress monitoring [29–32], and safety management [33–35]. In the context of hurricane preparedness in construction sites, UAVs have demonstrated the potential to capture an invaluable record of potential wind-borne debris from aerial vantage points [1]. Exploring through imagery would provide critical informa - tion regarding the type and location of potential threats, allowing practitioners such as safety directors and superintendents to better prepare and implement emergency operating procedures to secure job - sites prior to extreme weather events. In this paper, we propose a novel vision-based framework for con - struction site hurricane preparedness. By leveraging visual data from jobsites, we reconstruct a digital twin model of the at-risk construction environment that recognizes the type and the location of threats at the 3D level. In this regard, we first perform an image-based scene recon - struction, and then carry out the semantic segmentation on images to identify potential wind-borne debris at the 2D level. Building on the outcome of the 2D sematic segmentation, we project the semantic values onto the point cloud model to obtain the semantic information of po - tential wind-borne debris at the 3D level. Finally, for each instance of potential wind-borne debris, we estimate the associated quantity and assess the inherent threats based on the kinetic energy. A site-specific heat map is generated to delineate the risk associated with potential wind-borne debris with respect to the severity of given wind events. We evaluated the performance of the proposed methods on residential construction sites in College Station, TX, especially those associated with wooden dwellings where unstructured resources such as pine boards and plywoods are found. The proposed framework will help practitioners to effectively locate potential wind-borne debris in construction sites and understand the associated risk. Thus, it can support risk-informed de - cision-making by providing heads-up to practitioners in a timely fashion, which fosters awareness of the ways in which hurricanes cloud be destructive in construction sites. 2. Research background 2.1. Disaster preparedness and potential wind-borne debris Prior works on post-disaster management are geared towards activ - ities based on the severity of damage after disaster strikes in given re - gions [36,37]. In contrast, pre-disaster management focuses primarily on the adaptation of mitigation and preparedness plans through a pro - active risk assessment [38]. In this regard, Gregg et al. [39] carried out preemptive risk assessment building on the geographic location of given regions and the distance to hazard-prone areas, past experiences, and the probability of potential future incidences. According to [40], for every 2.5 dollars investment in pre-emptive efforts and practices in disaster management, a one-hundred-dollar bill can be saved by reducing the cost of disaster-related losses. Although the related con - texts would differ, such study infers the significance of proactive prac - tices and studies to identify threats and mitigate the potential impact of disasters in construction jobsites. In order to implement preparedness practices, practitioners such as safety directors or superintendents should first recognize potential threats in jobsites [41]. If not properly recognized, preemptive efforts are likely subjected to failures [41,42]. Interestingly, demographic variables such as education and training of practitioners may impact the extent to which proactive efforts are adapted [43,44]. In this regard, technology-driven studies (e.g., eye-tracking) have been conducted for the cognition of hazards among practitioners in construction sites [18,45–47]. The general concept of these studies is to assess the quality of practitioners’ visual search to recognize deficiencies, which can help better educate practitioners to flag and perceive potential threats in jobsites [48,49]. Although such works demonstrate the great potential in identifying the location of hazards in construction sites, when it comes to visual inspection on potential wind-borne debris, there is an addi - tional need to consider the geometrical characteristics (e.g., shape, volume) that needs to be further assessed to better understand the associated risk. To this end, such studies on hazard detection would not be expected to directly translate into desirable outcomes in assessing the extent of threats in the context of potential wind-borne debris. Meanwhile, studies have been carried out to characterize the behavior of general wind-borne debris. [10] evaluated potential damage of wind-borne debris to building envelopes with respect to the severity of wind events. Later, [50] classified the shape of wind-borne debris into three categories (compact, plate, and rod) as each shape demonstrates different behaviors in severe wind events. Building on the geometrical characteristics, [51,52] studied possible trajectories of rod and sheet type of debris in severe wind events. As such, a systematic foundation to quantify the extent of damage with regards to types and shapes of po - tential wind-borne debris has been established. Building on these, we propose a vision-based framework to automatically identify and assess threats of potential wind-borne debris in construction sites. The pro - posed framework for scene understanding is essential to support the localization of potential threats in construction sites and prioritization for preparedness planning. As a point of departure, in the following section, we review the research on scene understanding focusing on point cloud segmentation. 2.2. Digital twining and point cloud segmentation 3D scene understanding such as point cloud segmentation is a rising field of study that has a wide range of applications such as robotics [53], augmented reality [54], autonomous driving [55], and medical imaging [56]. The objective of scene understanding is to cluster points belonging to a specific object in point cloud models. In order to better process 3D data, prior works represent the point cloud models in the context of multi-view images [57], voxels [58], and meshes [59]. Such methods help the classification of points belonging to a target class, but not the instance segmentation for objects in a particular class. Moreover, the conversion of point cloud models to voxels and mesh representations is likely subjected to data loss, which may lead to poor performance in classification [60]. In this regard, [60] adopted a deep learning frame - work, referred to as PointNet, which directly uses point cloud data as the preliminary input. PointNet framework was the first to address point permutations, and the extracted deep-learning descriptors are robust to order invariances in the 3D point cloud. Despite the benefits of PointNet, one of the challenges is that a relatively small number of points (e.g., 1024, 2048, 4096) can be processed by its workflow due to the fixed size of the input layer of the deep neural networks. To this end, the process of semantic segmentation and scene parsing of point cloud models con - taining millions of points (e.g., reconstructed scenes of large-scale job - sites) has been identified as a challenge for PointNet architecture. Model-driven segmentation of point clouds enables the classification of points based on a set of hand-engineered cost functions such as in shape- fitting algorithms or region-growing workflows. In this regard, [ 62 ] proposed a method to address the segmentation of 3D point clouds of bricks in masonry walls, and [ 63 ] performed the segmentation of in - frastructures based on region-growing algorithms. Later, [ 64 ] carried out the automatic detection of safety regulation compliances (e.g., toe- boards) on point cloud data. Despite the benefits of such models (e.g., computational simplicity), insufficient robustness to geometrical vari - ances and poor performance on noisy and incomplete point cloud models have been identified as the limitation of model-driven point cloud segmentation frameworks [ 61 ]. In this regard, studies such as [ 65 ] have addressed point cloud segmentation through the supervised learning. For instance, [ 66 ] addressed the detection of scaffolding in point clouds through the Random Forest framework. Studies were performed for the categoriza - tion of planner patches [ 67 ] (e.g., columns, beams, slabs) and rebars [ 68 ] in laser scanning data using conventional classification approaches such as support vector machine (SVM). Later, [ 69 ] addressed the seg - mentation and classification of construction machinery in 3D laser scanning models, building on a descriptor using a synthetic training dataset. However, the assessment is not systematic, and heuristic approaches in jobsites are likely to be error-prone and labor- intensive. The advancement of machine vision and the convenience of UAVs have offered opportunities to collect large-scale imagery and generate digital photologs to keep the record of errands in construction projects. Journal of Infrastructure Preservation and Resilience Integrating multi-source data for life-cycle risk assessment of bridge networks: a system digital twin framework Ziheng Geng1, Chao Zhang2, Yishuo Jiang3, Dora Pugliese1 and Minghui Cheng1,4* Abstract Bridges are critical infrastructure assets that face a variety of stressors throughout their service life, requiring a life- cycle approach to assess their risk profile. Recent advancements in sensing and monitoring technologies provide a powerful data foundation to improve the accuracy of life-cycle risk assessment (LCRA). However, existing works that incorporate data for probabilistic risk assessment typically focus on individual bridges and rely on single-source data, limiting their scope and applicability. To this end, a system digital twin (SDT) framework based on Bayesian network (BN) is proposed to integrate multi-source data for LCRA of bridge networks. Specifically, the SDT can capture correlations and interdependencies across various scales, including within individual components (e.g., multiple failure modes), between components within a system (e.g., bridges along a route), and across interconnected systems (e.g., bridge and hydraulic systems). It integrates data from various sources including bridge inspections, traffic moni- toring facilities, and water watch stations. A coastal bridge network in Miami-Dade County, FL, is used as an illustrative example to demonstrate how the SDT integrates multi-source data for risk assessment. Additionally, several future scenarios are hypothesized to showcase the applicability and flexibility of the proposed framework in supporting risk management for infrastructure systems. Keywords System digital twin, Life-cycle risk assessment, Infrastructure management, System reliability, Bayesian network Introduction Bridges are critical assets within infrastructure systems, playing a pivotal role in ensuring the functionality and connectivity of modern communities and cities. How - ever, these assets are vulnerable, facing a variety of stress- ors throughout their service life. Factors such as natural hazards, aging materials, and excessive loading can accel- erate the deterioration of bridges and undermine their reliability under both normal and extreme condi - tions [1, 2]. This underscores the necessity for a life- cycle approach to risk assessment in bridge networks. Life-cycle risk assessment (LCRA) provides a holistic approach for assessing the evolving risks associated with a structure/system over its lifespan [3, 4]. This approach involves identifying potential hazards, estimating their probability of occurrence, and assessing their potential consequences. With the risk profile, LCRA facilitates informed decision-making in planning inspection, main - tenance, repair actions to ensure long-term sustainabil - ity and resilience [5, 6]. J Infrastruct Preserv Resil (2025) 6:9 Recent breakthroughs in sensing and monitoring technologies have significantly improved the acquisi - tion of multi-source data reflecting bridge conditions, thereby opening new avenues for enhancing LCRA of bridge networks. For example, non-destructive testing (NDE) techniques have been powerful tools for detect - ing material-level flaws in bridges. Common NDE meth - ods, such as ground penetrating radar [8, 9], close-range photogrammetry [10], infrared thermography [8, 11], and terrestrial laser scanning [12] have demonstrated efficacy in detecting defects, rebar corrosion, and crack - ing. Additionally, structural health monitoring (SHM) has emerged as an advanced approach for comprehensive assessment of structural condition. SHM systems utilize diverse sensors to capture physical measurements asso - ciated with loads (e.g., bridge weigh-in-motion for traffic loads [13]), environmental stressors (e.g., acoustic sensor for scour detection [14]), and bridge responses (e.g., opti- cal-fiber sensors for strain [15, 16] and vibration moni - toring [17]). Collectively, these innovations produce an extensive and comprehensive dataset that is expected to be used by LCRA models to improve prediction accuracy and reduce model uncertainty. With the ability to leverage diverse data sources in the real world, digital twin (DT) technology is increas - ingly recognized as a transformative tool for life-cycle management of infrastructure systems. Specifically, DTs are virtual representations of physical assets or systems, continuously synchronized with their physical counter - parts through bidirectional information exchange [18]. The distinguishing features of DTs lie in their ability to (a) integrate observations from physical asset to dynami - cally update the state of digital asset and (b) leverage the updated digital state to inform decision-making for the operation, maintenance, and management of physical assets. However, the development of DTs for LCRA of bridge networks faces two critical challenges. First, bridge networks are complex systems comprising multiple inter- connected sub-systems, such as bridges, transportation networks, and rivers. These sub-systems exhibit correla - tions and interdependencies across various scales, both within individual sub-systems (e.g., multiple structural components of a single bridge and multiple bridges along a route) and across different sub-systems (e.g., bridges subject to river scour). Therefore, a systems approach is required to effectively capture these interrelationships. Second, risk assessment of bridge networks requires the integration of heterogeneous data sources. For example, bridge inspections and river measurements inform the failure probability of bridges, while traffic volume data aids in assessing the consequences of bridge failures. These datasets typically differ in format and update fre - quency, underscoring the need for a data assimilation approach to seamlessly integrate multi-source data into the DT. To address these research challenges, this paper devel - ops a scalable computational framework for LCRA of bridge networks. The framework enables the develop - ment of a system digital twin (SDT) at a large scale (e.g., city- and county-scales), the integration of data from diverse sources and different systems, and the considera - tion of complex statistical correlations among the ran - dom variables and data sources within the same system and across different systems. A case study in Miami-Dade County, FL, is conducted to demonstrate the proposed framework. Specifically, data from diverse sources including bridge inspections, traffic monitoring facilities, and water watch stations are utilized to update the risk profile of the bridge network. To the best knowledge of the authors, this case study is the first DT that reaches the county scale, interlinks three systems for probabil - istic modeling and risk updating, and incorporates the statistical correlations. Additionally, three scenarios are presented to illustrate the flexibility of the proposed framework, showcasing the capacity of SDT to adapt to real-world changes. Literature review Life‑cycle risk assessment of bridge networks Extensive efforts have been dedicated to applying LCRA to spatially distributed bridge networks within trans - portation systems. Risk analysis typically involves two components: (a) evaluating the failure probability of indi- vidual bridges over time, and (b) estimating the direct and indirect consequences of bridge failures. For failure probability analysis, previous studies have developed time-variant reliability analysis frameworks for various failure-inducing factors, including floods [19, 20], colli - sions [21, 22], traffic loads [23, 24], material deterioration [25, 26], earthquakes [27, 28], tsunamis [1, 29], storm surges [30, 31], and climate change [7, 32]. Recognizing that adjacent bridges are exposed to similar environ - mental stressors and traffic patterns, event-based simu - lations [33, 34] and random field modeling [35, 36] have been employed to model the spatial correlation of bridge failures. For failure consequence assessment, estimat - ing indirect consequences, i.e., the cost associated with reduced functionality across the bridge network, remains a challenging task due to the need to simulate the varia - tion in traffic flow resulting from bridge failures. Exist - ing studies have advanced traffic assignment analyses by incorporating user decision-making behaviors [6], emphasizing extreme risk scenarios [4], and enhancing computational efficiency [37, 38]. Additionally, with the rapid advancement of machine learning, surrogate modeling techniques such as Page 3 of 20 Geng et al. J Infrastruct Preserv Resil (2025) 6:9 random forests [ 39], deep neural networks [ 40, 41], and graph neural networks [ 42, 43] have been increas - ingly utilized in the reliability analysis and LCRA of bridge networks to improve computational efficiency. However, a critical limitation of the abovementioned studies lies in their reliance on simulation-based approaches, where certain model parameters are assumed to remain constant over time. Therefore, these parameters fail to capture the dynamic evolution of bridge networks and may not be the best represen - tation of the actual conditions. It may lead to potential discrepancies between model predictions and real- world performance, limiting their applicability in engi - neering practice. Digital twin for bridge asset management Recent studies have explored the implementation of DTs in bridges asset management. For example, DTs have been developed using building information mod - eling (BIM) and augmented with inspection data such as 3D scanning [ 44], digital image correlation (DIC) [45], and weigh-in-motion (WIM) [ 46]. Extending these advancements, modeling enabling technologies, including geometry, measurements, simulations, and other data-driven techniques, have been integrated to develop cyber-physics DTs [ 47– 49]. Additionally, several studies have focused on the dynamic decision- making feedback loops within DTs, employing proba - bilistic graphic models as the computational cores and utilizing Bayesian inference to update the digital state and inform predictive maintenance decision-making [50– 52]. Despite significant advancements, existing DTs for bridge asset management suffer from two limitations. First, existing works predominately focus on developing DTs for individual bridges. However, bridges are inte - gral components of spatially distributed transportation networks, where the failure of a single bridge can trig - ger cascading effects, such as traffic detours and delays. These indirect impacts frequently surpass direct con - sequences by several orders of magnitude [ 53], under - scoring the necessity for modeling bridge networks as complex, interdependent systems. Second, current DTs with probabilistic modeling capabilities primarily rely on single-source data for risk updating. This reliance on siloed data source constrains the capacity of DTs to capture the dynamic evolution of real-world condi - tions, thereby undermining their predictive accuracy. Given the diversity and heterogeneity of real-world data, a comprehensive approach is required to syner - gistically integrate data from multiple sources for risk updating. Limitations and future work Although the proposed framework demonstrates fea - sibility and applicability in assessing life-cycle risk for bridge networks, it suffers from certain limitations. First, a significant challenge is the storage and computational efficiency associated with large-scale BNs. For example, the BN in this study comprises 6,478 nodes and 10,584 edges, occupying 97,484.8 MB memory to store its CPTs. This presents a substantial computational burden. As the size and complexity of the network grow, the expo - nential increase in memory requirements can severely hinder the practical implementations of the proposed framework. It can extract a trunk network from the origi - nal large-scale BN based on available data sources and given performance measures. By reducing the size of the network, it can significantly lower the computational overhead, facilitating efficient Bayesian inference. Build - ing on these advancements, future work should focus on seamlessly integrating custom BN with the proposed framework to develop scalable solutions that can han - dle the computational demands of increasingly large and complex networks. Second, due to the limitations in available resources, the current framework has not yet been validated. Future work should focus on assessing the performance and quality of the SDT to ensure its applicability in practical engineering scenarios. Specifically, the validation can be implemented with the aid of field and laboratory testing. The proposed framework can identify a list of vulner - able bridges within the bridge network. For these iden - tified bridges, field coring can be conducted to extract core samples from their structural components. These samples can then be tested in the laboratory to analyze their properties such as microstructural characteris - tics, apparent electrical resistivity, compressive strength, chloride ingress, and carbonation depths. These labora - tory analyses provide quantitative metrics that reflect the physical condition of bridges. To validate the framework, the same procedures should be applied to bridges classi - fied as low risk by the SDT. By comparing the laboratory results from vulnerable and low-risk bridges, valuable insights into the predictive accuracy and robustness of the framework can be derived. Third, the proposed framework primarily focuses on risk assessment of bridge networks. It integrates the probability of bridge failure with the associated failure consequences to identify the risk within the bridge net - work. However, it does not include the system’s response to address these threats. Future work should extend the framework to incorporate restoration and resilience. Resilience is a performance indicator that evaluates the system’s capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover from disruptions [54, 78, 79]. It highlights the ability of a system to recover to its previous functionality after a disruption. Restoration models, which define the recov - ery pattern of the system, are essential tools for quanti - fying resilience [80]. Incorporating these elements will enhance the utility of the framework in infrastructure management, better assisting in decision-making regard - ing inspections and maintenance of infrastructure. Summary and conclusions This paper develops a system digital twin (SDT) frame - work based on Bayesian network (BN) to monitor the life-cycle risk of bridge networks. A prototype graph structure of SDTs is first developed at the bridge level to capture the correlations within individual compo - nents (e.g., multiple failure modes of bridges) and to integrate data from various sources. The prototype is then extended to the network level by embedding spatial geometry of cities or counties. The resulting network- level graph structure can capture interdependencies both within a single system (e, g., bridges along a route and river gages monitoring the same river) and across interconnected systems (e.g., bridge, transportation, and hydraulic systems). With the graph structure, Monte Carlo simulation is conducted to generate a dataset to fit the conditional probability tables (CPTs) of the BN. To streamline the Bayesian inference over time, the BN nodes are classified into three types: data nodes, analysis nodes, and performance nodes. This classification facili - tates automated Bayesian inference using multi-source data with different update frequencies. A real-world bridge network in Miami-Dade County, FL, is used as a case study to illustrate the feasibility and applicability of the proposed framework for life-cycle risk assessment. The following conclusions are drawn: The graph structure is constructed using three databases: the bridge database, traffic database, and river database. By embedding the spatial geometry of the county, a graph structure with 6,478 nodes and 10,584 edges is created, integrating 1,034 Page 18 of 20Geng et al. J Infrastruct Preserv Resil (2025) 6:9 bridges, 1,261 STCSs, and 10 river gages within Miami-Dade County. This demonstrates the scal - ability of the proposed SDT framework. The graph structure utilizes a clustered graphi - cal structure to capture the statistically correlated variables, including river flow and stage data from both individual and adjacent river gages. These cor - related river flow and stage nodes are connected to a common source node, demonstrating the capacity of the SDT framework to model the complex cor - relations at the county scale. A future scenario is hypothesized to showcase how the SDT integrates diverse data sources for risk inference. The bridge conditions are assumed to deteriorate over time, whereas traffic volumes and river stage/flow are assumed to increase gradually. The results reveal a general upward trend in net - work risk, punctuated by localized fluctuations. This demonstrates the capability of the proposed framework in updating the risk of bridge network using available multiple-source data. Three representative scenarios are selected: the construction of new bridges, the closure of aging bridges, and data out of range. The proposed frame - work effectively addresses these changes by modify - ing localized CPTs without reconfiguring the entire network. The updated BN retains its integrity and functionality for risk assessment across all scenar - ios. The results show the flexibility of the proposed framework in accommodating the dynamic evolu - tion of urban bridge networks.
