However, there is a lack of efficient and standardised approaches for man- aging monitoring-related information that can help to provide dynamic and reliable information for continuous dam evaluation. With the importance of digital twins (DTs) being proven in better data integration and interoperability, a DT-based approach for comprehensive dam evaluation and its data integration method based on extended industry foundation classes (IFC) are provided in this study. This paper presents a new data structure and corresponding data classification strategy, using which information required for continuous evaluation can be effectively organised. Considering that existing evaluation methods cannot fully characterise uncertainties in the evaluation process, a cloud model based comprehensive evaluation framework is proposed. In this approach, a multi-rule cloud reasoning model is utilised to evaluate monitoring points. Also, a multi-dimensional cloud model and an improved Criteria Importance Though Intercriteria Correlation (CRITIC) method are leveraged to assess evaluation indices affected by multiple factors. Finally, the results of a case study verify that the pro- posed DT-based solution realises a continuous dam safety evaluation, which contributes to automated and efficient dam condition monitoring. Introduction Dam projects have created huge economic and social bene- fits for human beings throughout history, especially in water supply, flood control, and renewable energy production (Huang & Yan, 2009;Y €uksel, 2010). However, most as-built dams suffer from safety-related issues (e.g., excessive deformation (Chen, Gu, Bao, Wu, & Su, 2016), adverse seepage (Jiang et al., 2020), slope instability (Martt, 2005), etc.) due to the combined influence of many reasons such as increasing natural hazard risks, aging of existing infra- structure, lack of experience in dam safety management and operations, among others. These emerging safety issues may lead to potential dam accidents and further cause adverse impacts on the life, property, and environment of down- stream areas, if they are not detected and resolved in time. A comprehensive evaluation of dam service behaviour is the most important and scientific way to monitor dam oper- ation condition and diagnose potential problems (Wu, Xu, Gu, & Li, 2012). Recently, with the increasing awareness of risk prevention and control in the field of dam engineering, some research has demonstrated the necessity of the transformation of comprehensive dam evaluation mode from the traditional static and passive one to a dynamic and proactive one (Jeon, Lee, Shin, & Park, 2009; Supakchukul et al., 2019; Wu & Su, 2005). Its initiative aims to maintain dynamic information, support continuous condition monitoring of dam structures, achieve efficient decision-making, and finally minimise the risk of dam failure. The new mode of dam safety evaluation is of vital significance for diagnosing potential problems and taking reinforcement measures timely especially when some unpredicted catastrophic floods are coming or a united safety management of dam groups in the same river basin is required (Huang, Li, Hu, Zhang, & Zhao, 2020; Li, Bao, & Fang, 2005). However, in the traditional static mode, measured data that contains diagnostic information is read from different kinds of instruments or sensors and stored in one or several databases (Li & Wang, 2017), while detailed information (e.g., monitoring precision and frequency, correction param- eters and methods) of instruments are hidden in the instruction manuals and location information are presented in the monitoring instrument layout (usually a set of design drawings). The information related to dam safety monitor- ing is usually saved in different formats and separately stored in different management systems. In view of this, dam engineers may require significant time and effort to query and coordinate all necessary information for evalu- ation purposes. To support assessment-related tasks, some advanced tech- niques have been adopted to improve data availability and reliability. For example, using ASP.NET technology, Li and Wang ( 2017) constructed an integrated monitoring platform based on Brower/Server structure to enhance emergency response operations. In this platform, rapid access to moni- toring data is achieved by a data acquisition system. Janceti/C19c, Kranj /C20ci/C19c, and Rezo ( 2020) leveraged SQL (Structural Query Language) and GIS (Geo-Information System) tools in dam management. GIS allows easy storage, processing, and analysis of spatial data to support dam safety management. Although some efforts have been made to integrate multiple information and facilitate data utilisa- tion. The massive information is still stored and described in an informal manner. It may lead to misunderstanding of various monitoring data sources and further leave room for error, because complex information cross-references are quite frequent during comprehensive evaluation process. Building information modelling (BIM) or referred to as ‘digital twin ’ (DT), aiming to digitise and informatize vari- ous physical and functional characteristics of a real entity, has brought new ideas to assets condition monitoring (Kaewunruen, Sresakoolchai, Ma, & Phil-Ebosie, 2021; Lu, Xie, Parlikad, & Schooling, 2020) in recent years. By creat- ing a dynamic data-rich environment, DT provides a united and reliable platform to run complicated analyses and simu- lations (Haag & Anderl, 2018; Tao, Zhang, Liu, & Nee, 2019). However, a data schema for dam DTs that is oriented for comprehensive evaluation purpose and a data integra- tion/query approach that can help to automate data process- ing, still require further study. Meanwhile, most evaluation methods currently adopted cannot characterise the ambiguity and randomness in the evaluation process. However, ambiguity inevitably exists in the evaluation grades (Jiang & Fan, 2008) and monitoring data collected from monitoring instruments is always dis- crete and random (Zhang & Li, 2019). Hence the reasoning process from measured dam behaviour to evaluation grades is full of uncertainty. In response to this situation, the cloud model, as a cognitive model that can simultaneously describe the fuzziness, randomness, and discreteness of con- cepts and implement the uncertain transformation between qualitative concepts and quantitative description (Li, Liu, & Gan, 2009), becoming a promising alternative. Nonetheless, the combination of necessary knowledge in the field of dam engineering with the cloud model concept to realise auto- mated dam safety evaluation is still under investigation. The realisation of the dynamic and proactive mode of dam safety evaluation still faces many challenges at the cur- rent stage. This paper proposes a DT-enabled solution for comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour that contributes to tackling these challenges mentioned above and advancing the BIM provisions required to ensure dam safety during the operation and maintenance (O&M) phase of dam proj- ects. In the presented study, a new data structure is pro- vided based on the summarised information requirements of dam monitoring systems. Then, the related information is integrated into dam DTs by extending the industry founda- tion classes (IFC), which forms a fundamental basis of a reliable and robust dam evaluation program. After that, an automated evaluation framework is proposed to realise con- tinuous dam condition monitoring. In this framework, a data classification strategy to facilitate efficient establishment of evaluation index systems is proposed. To characterise uncertainties in the evaluation process, a multi-rule cloud reasoning model is utilised to evaluate monitoring points and a multi-dimensional cloud model is leveraged to assess evaluation indices affected by multiple factors. Meanwhile, an improved Criteria Importance Though Intercriteria Correlation (CRITIC) method with dam knowledge consid- ered is proposed to calculate weight values without human intervention. 2. Literature review 2.1. Current applications of digital twin in asset condition assessment Nowadays, the digital twin concept has been applied to assessment analysis of building assets because of its ability to provide convenient access to comprehensive and inte- grated information. The information (e.g., the actual envir- onment, applied loads and corresponding structural response) collected from Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) systems is the key ingredient of a DT (Tao et al., 2019). Some research utilises digital models as a visualised plat- form for intuitive interpretation of SHM data and simple assessment of structural condition of assets. For example, Boddupalli, Sadhu, Rezazadeh Azar, and Pattyson ( 2019) proposed a digital representation platform based on BIM to support the visualisation of the related SHM data. Valinejadshoubi, Bagchi, and Moselhi ( 2019) developed a DT of a modular building to store, query, and show real- time strain monitoring data. The proposed approach sup- ports the damage assessment of a column by setting a fixed threshold for monitored strain values. Kaewunruen et al. (2021) developed a ‘digital twin þ bridge risk inspection model’ to achieve preventive maintenance of bridges. This platform enables the comparison of real-time monitoring data with a ‘safety value ’ to assess structural vulnerability. There is no doubt that these studies mentioned above have highlighted the value of DT concept in asset assessment, but they only achieve informal data binding and display to per- form a fast assessment by comparing monitored structural response with standard values, which does not demand high interoperability and efficient cross-referencing between inte- grated information. Lu et al. ( 2020) proposed a DT-based anomaly detection framework to monitor the operation condition of building assets. This method enables the formal description of related information and can evaluate the mechanical condition of a centrifugal pump based on the mutual verification of its vibration monitoring data and working loads. This solution can only assess pump condition from one aspect (vibration STRUCTURE AND INFRASTRUCTURE ENGINEERING 905 performance), which is not sufficient to support comprehen- sive evaluation. It is merely suitable for the evaluation of a single function of complex structures or building assets with a single failure mode. However, a dam is an integrated structure which is exposed to complex natural environment. Many reasons may cause dam failure (International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD),), 2013). Considering this, a comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour demands the consideration of all relevant influencing factors. Also, the baselines to assess monitored variables should be deduced from the history monitoring data and condition records, which requires high-quality data integration to support complex cross-refer- encing. Hence a more complete and systematic data struc- ture and an efficient data processing workflow are important to ensure data availability and interoperability. Currently, the absence of a standard data schema for the formal description of comprehensive monitoring systems and the absence of a data processing strategy for keeping data consistent are main barriers behind the continuous evaluation scenario. 2.2. Review of comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour The evaluation methods of dam behaviour can be classified into two categories: physical simulation analysis and data- driven methods. Physical simulation analysis mainly lever- ages finite element method (FEM) to calculate structural responses, with model parameters being calibrated using monitoring data (Colombo, Domaneschi, & Ghisi, 2016). Data-driven methods refer to the data fusion, modelling, and analysis of massive information to mine useful informa- tion (Su, Wen, Chen, & Tian, 2016). Among them, compre- hensive evaluation of dam behaviour using multiple monitoring-related information is always a popular topic because of its significance to ensure dam safety during the O&M stage. As shown in Figure 1 , a complete evaluation index sys- tem is a multi-index and multi-layer system that includes well-organised and preprocessed monitoring data (mainly the monitored dam behaviour, such as stress, deformation, seepage, etc.). The comprehensive dam evaluation should be implemented from left to right. Hence, the reasonable and effective evaluation of each measuring point is the basis to ensure accuracy of the entire comprehensive evaluation pro- gram. After deriving the baseline of each monitoring point, the remaining challenge is to reason from a quantitative value to a qualitative index. For each monitoring point, the quantitative value is the difference between the baseline and monitored effect variable, and the qualitative index is an evaluation grade that indicates the safety status of this mon- itoring point, such as normal, abnormal, and so on. Most existing research utilises the concept of fuzzy sets to evaluate safety status of monitoring points (Shu, Bao, Li, Zhang, & Wu, 2020; Wu et al., 2012). In these solutions, evaluation grades are quantified by fixed boundary condi- tions based on human experience, and functions of membership degree are curves with fixed parameters. Hence, the way to obtain safety status of monitoring points is deterministic. Conclusions To achieve the dynamic and proactive mode of dam safety evaluation, a DT-enabled solution is proposed in this paper. The results of the case study verify the ability of the pro- posed approach to provide dynamic information and per- form continuous dam safety evaluation by taking full advantage of the standardised monitoring-related informa- tion in a dam DT. It is beneficial for indicating the overall safety status, supporting decision-making, and performing preventive maintenance before severe and even catastrophic consequences happen during the O&M stage of dam proj- ects. The main innovations of this paper are the following: 1. With the help of the standardised description of moni- toring-related information in dam DT environment, the availability and interoperability of required information for complex evaluation tasks are enhanced. Compared with traditional methods of dam management, this approach can provide reliable and dynamic data to sup- port continuous safety evaluation. 2. Based on available information and the cloud model concept, an automated evaluation approach that can consider uncertainties in the evaluation process, is pro- posed. This approach can present the general trend of dam safety status in a certain period. Hence, compared with the traditional static evaluation mode, it is of vital significance for diagnosing potential problems and tak- ing reinforcement measures timely. Although the proposed comprehensive dam evaluation approach can realise continuous monitoring of dam behaviour, as verified in the case study, there are still some shortcomings existing. For example, IFC entities related to dam structures like IfcDam, IfcDamGallery, and IfcDamFoundation are not included in the current IFC schema, which still need further investigation. Meanwhile, the measurement of evaluation indices is only performed based on the historical monitoring data, but the FEM-based simula- tion results are also useful especially when some monitoring data is missing or contaminated (Salazar et al., 2019). However, to realise the complementation of these two sources of information, further study such as the integration of simu- lation results into dam DTs and the ingenious combination of these two completely different kinds of information, is still required. Unfortunately, diverse geological and hydrogeological conditions and complex shapes of the foundation pit create barriers for reliable FPE risk prognosis and control. Furthermore, typical support systems during FPE use temporary measures, which have limited capacity to confront excessive loads, large deformations, and seepage. This study aims to establish an intelligent risk prognosis and control framework based on digital twin (DT) for ensuring safe and effective FPE processes. Previous studies have conducted extensive experimental and numerical analyses for examining unsafe conditions during FPE. How to enable intelligent risk prognosis and control of tedious FPE processes by integrating physics-based models and sensory data collected in the ﬁeld is still challenging. DT could help to establish the interaction and feedback mechanisms between the physical and virtual space. In this study, the authors have established a DT model that consists of a physical space model and a high-ﬁdelity physics-based model of a foundation pit in virtual space. As a result, a mechanism for effective acquisition and fusion of heterogeneous information from both physical and virtual space is established. Then, the authors proposed an integrated model and data-driven approach for examining safety risks during FPE. In the end, the authors have validated the proposed method through a case study of the FPE of the Wuhan Metro Line. The results show that the proposed method could provide theoretical and practical support for future intelligent FPE. Introduction Urbanization is proceeding at an unprecedented pace, with towering buildings rising from urban land and ground space being fully developed and utilized. Development of large-scale underground civil infrastructures (e.g., subways, pipelines, etc.) becomes favored by construction practitioners. Excavation processes of the temporary foundation pit are thus important during the construction of large underground civil infrastructures. However, foundation pit excavation (FPE) is known as one of the most dangerous construc- tion processes that threatens the safety of all involved construction personnel. Furthermore, various weather conditions, geological factors, and the hydrogeological environment of the construction site create barriers for ensuring safe and effective FPE processes [1,2]. Safety monitoring and risk prognosis of FPE are vital for ensuring timely acquisition of spatiotem- poral risk information (e.g., foundation pit deformations) and effective risk predictions. However, current ﬁeld practices heavily rely on visual measurements and inspections using traditional measuring equipment and techniques, which is time-consuming and requires engineers to have profound knowledge and extensive ﬁeld experiences. Even experienced Buildings 2023, 13, 247. https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings13010247 https://www.mdpi.com/journal/buildings Buildings 2023, 13, 247 2 of 31 engineers could hardly identify safety risks during FPE and make timely control actions. Collapse accidents may occur and cause fatalities. Previous studies have examined the stability retaining structures during FPE processes. Fathipour et al. [3] used a well-established lower bound analysis combining the ﬁnite ele- ment method and second order conic programming (SOCP) to examine active and passive earth pressures on retaining structures with unsaturated backﬁll.Fathipour et al. [4] also combined the lower bound theorem of limit analysis with the ﬁnite element discretization method to conduct an in-depth numerical study on the lateral earth pressure of backﬁlled retaining walls with reinforced soil layers of geosynthetics. Zhang et al. [5] examined the effect of wall toe resistance on the performance of GRS walls with different toe constraints. Zhang [6] et al. explored the effect of cornering on the stability of reinforced slopes. Liu [7] studied the lateral displacements of segmental GRS walls at the end of construction and after 10 years of creep under constant gravity loads. In addition, lots of studies have conducted extensive efforts in developing methods and tools for safe and effective risk prognosis and control during FPE [ 8–12]. However, limited studies have examined methods for (1) capturing the spatiotemporal changes during FPE processes; (2) updating ﬁnite element models using the captured ﬁeld anomalies; and (3) establishing effective risk prediction and control mechanisms for mitigating safety risks during FPE processes. Digital twin (DT) has been developed and extensively studied in the manufacturing domain for creating a high-ﬁdelity virtual space model that could reﬂect all behaviors of physical objects. Such a method could help for (1) capturing the interactive process between the physical and virtual space; (2) updating the virtual model based on ﬁeld discoveries; and (3) providing feedback to the physical model for guiding the manufacturing processes. Guo et al. [13] developed a system based on DT for intelligent risk predictions during mining operations in coal mines. Zhang et al. [ 14] examined the feasibility of using DT to control mine production processes under various scenarios. Song et al. [15] proposed a DT framework with inverse kinematics for modeling human skeletons. Gopalakrishnan, S et al. [ 16] have created a model-based feature information network (MFIN) based on DT for establishing digital representations of all systems and components. Guo et al. [ 17] proposed a DT-based health management framework by integrating a self-updating model for fault diagnosis and prediction. Ruppert, T et al. [18] developed a DT-based method for predicting production states by fusing information collected from real-time positioning systems. All these efforts provide the potential for establishing a risk prognosis and control system for safe and efﬁcient FPE. Timely risk information acquisition and diagnosis during FPE processes are vital for ensuring the safe and effective construction of underground urban infrastructures. Limited studies have examined the integration of emerging sensing techniques, ﬁnite element modeling and simulation methods, and real-time data transmission tools for timely risk capturing, analysis, and prediction during FPE processes given diverse geological and hydrogeological conditions and complex shapes of the foundation pit. The major contribution of this study is to enable a DT-based risk prognosis and control for safe and efﬁcient FPE. As seen in Figure 1, DT is a data-centric architecture based on the physical and virtual worlds. The left part of the ﬁgure represents the activities in the physical world and the right part represents the activities in the virtual world. Physical space is the three- dimensional world we are in. This includes the structure, components, environment, data collection, and smart devices of the building at the construction site. Virtual space is mainly used to create virtual models by computer and simulate the unperformed construction based on the data. The information technology of DT is embodied through massive data. Data collection, processing, and judgment are the core of the whole system. The maintenance model allows effective maintenance measures to be made based on the results of data processing. The considered object classes include electrical elements (light switch, light, speaker, socket, elevator button), safety elements (emergency switch, smoke alarm, fire extinguisher, escape sign), plumbing system el - ements (pipes), and other objects with useful information (door sign, board). Semantic information like class labels is extracted by applying AI-based image segmentation and then mapped to the 3D point cloud, segmenting the point cloud into point clusters. Introduction This research is about enriching gdt with small objects. By enriching, we refer here to the process of adding more categories of objects to the Gdts of basic elements in a building. By geometric digital twins, we refer here to a digital twin with geometric data only. A digital twin of a building here is defined as a regular-updated digital replica of a physical building that can represent the current condition of the building [1] . By small objects, we refer here to the elements that are smaller in scale in comparison with structural elements (like walls, floors, ceilings). In this paper, we focus on enriching geometric digital building twins by adding these elements. Meanwhile, instead of only segmenting point clouds, we extract text information such as object IDs to recognise object instances. Generating a geometric digital twin of an existing asset is a process that consists of the following steps: (1) capturing raw visual and spatial data in the form of RGB imagery and laser-scanned point clouds; (2) detecting geometric objects and geometric relationships of objects in the raw data. Step 1 of this process is significantly more automated than step 2 and requires much fewer labour hours [2] . The cost and effort needed to complete step 2 for most assets appear to counteract the perceived value of the resulting gdt. Step 2 can be broken down into the detection of large objects (such as ceilings, floors, walls) and small objects (such as fire extinguishers, smoke alarms) by their scale. Several recent methods have been proposed for the former ( [3] , [4] , [5] ), and have been vali - dated to robustly automate this task. However, no method has yet been proposed for the latter. This is the challenge that this paper aims to focus on. Apart from those relatively large structural elements, small elements (such as fire alarms, emergency switches) should also be included in an enriched building twin, these being helpful for facility managers. Text information such as room numbers and serial numbers (IDs) next to assets that can identify the corresponding assets (as shown in Fig. 1 ) is very helpful, especially when managing large facilities. These IDs exactly represent the corresponding object instances in an asset and make the link between physical assets and digital twins much clearer. Therefore, it is valuable to add the information to an enriched digital twin of buildings. Unfortunately, this work is currently mostly manual work. In summary, the great manual effort required to create an enriched digital twin is too costly when compared with the perceived value of the resulting model. For these reasons, there is a high demand for a higher degree of automation in the generation of an information-rich digital building twin. In this paper, the authors propose a novel framework to enrich a geometric building twin by fusing point cloud processing and object detection in images. The proposed method of information enrichment can be used to complete as-built models generated by other methods of creating geometric digital twins of structural elements. In particular, this paper presents the following contributions: a) Because the performance of detecting small-scale elements directly in point clouds is significantly lower than in images, unlike most previous methods that exclusively use point clouds as input, the approach presented here extracts semantic information from images by deep learning and then maps the extracted semantic information to laser-scanned point clouds. b) While most of the previous approaches only detect primary ele - ments (like ceilings, walls, floors, windows and doors), our proposed method includes small but highly relevant objects in the energy and the fire-safety sub-systems that are essential for maintaining and monitoring buildings (like smoke alarms, emergency switches); c) In order to create an information-rich building twin, other useful information (text and numbers) is detected in images by applying optical character recognition (OCR) technologies to detect object IDs and recognise object instances. Some examples are shown in Fig. 1 . The detected machine-encoded texts include the room number on the door sign, as well as numbers or text corresponding to the detected objects, which helps to identify the object instance in the physical asset. The rest of this paper is organised as follows: research background including state of the art is reviewed in Section 2 ; the proposed pipeline is introduced in Section 3 in detail; experiments and implementation details are shown in Section 4 ; conclusions and future work are dis - cussed in Section 5 . 2. Background In this paper, the authors aim to enrich a geometric digital building twin. Apart from structural objects such as ceilings, floors and walls, a rich building twin should also contain other small but important objects, for example objects from the energy and fire-safety sub-systems such as smoke alarms, emergency switches, etc. In our previous research [3] , we have already reconstructed ceilings, floors, and walls of buildings by initially detecting the void space inside rooms. These structural elements do not fall within the scope of this paper. Compared to structural ele - ments in a building, other components are usually small in size and have different geometry properties, which makes it hard to apply the same methods to detect those small-scale elements. Therefore, 2D information from images and 3D information from laser-scanned point clouds are connected and integrated into the proposed approach. We believe that this combination provides a significant advantage over using the laser- scanned point cloud alone, especially for detecting small-scale compo - nents in a building. In addition, text information, including serial numbers and IDs, can also be extracted from 2D images, and the detected information can be used to enrich the digital twin further. Recent research into small objects detection is discussed in Section 2.1 . As object detection and text recognition in images are achieved by deep learning in our approach, recent research in both fields is intro - duced in Section 2.2 and 2.3 respectively. Finally, research gaps are summarised in Section 2.4 . 2.1. Secondary object reconstruction in buildings With regard to elements located on wall surfaces, such as sockets and light switches, in [8] , the authors designed a robot that can recognise doors, door handles, and sockets to achieve the door task and plugging task. The electrical outlet pattern is detected in camera images by feature detection, and a laser scanning sensor is used to find the pose of a wall. In [9] , the authors detect light switches and sockets in ortho - graphic 2D images by a random forest classifier. They use a feature descriptor pool to measure the probability of the detection. A method was designed in [10] that allows a mobile robot to get on/off an elevator in a multistory building. An algorithm is presented for recognising elevator buttons, where the input image is first converted to a binary image, and then the candidates of buttons and floor numbers are filtered out and ambiguous candidates are rejected by applying a neural network. While most of these methods are used to help robots recognise specific objects in the environment and perform a given task, little work has been done in the AEC domain. In [6] , the authors proposed a method to detect objects such as switches, ducts and signs in a coloured point cloud. Depending on whether the objects have geometric discontinuities or colour discontinuities in the wall area, potential regions of interest are computed in depth images and colour images with regard to the wall plane, respectively. The region of interest is then matched to a pre - defined depth model database and a predefined colour model database that contain object classes in the scene. With regard to elements mounted on the ceiling such as lighting, in [11] , the authors proposed a recognition method based on thermal- Fig. 1. Text information in building. Y. Pan et al. Assuming the temperatures of these elements are different from other parts of the ceiling, the points of corresponding elements can be extracted from the point cloud. In [12] , the authors used two steps to recognise objects in thermal-mapped point clouds: segmentation with thermal information and classification with geometric information. The target objects are light fixtures on the ceilings, monitors on the wall and humans in the environment. In [13] , the authors extract the ceiling plane first and then convert the laser-scanned point cloud to an image of the ceiling. Fluorescent lightings and circular low-energy bulbs are detected from the image by Harris corner detector and Hough trans - formation. In [14] , a method to detect tunnel luminaires from the point cloud is proposed. In this approach, they use assumptions that are only valid in the tunnel, for example luminaires are located at higher points at the side of the tunnel and have brighter colour patterns than their surroundings. With regard to identifying pipes, in [15] , the authors proposed a method to detect pipe spools in a cluttered point cloud. The method used curvature estimation, points clustering, and feature matching to extract pipe spool objects. In an office building, pipes are rarely visible because they are usually located inside the walls or behind suspended ceilings. In [16] , the authors proposed a neural network to segment RGBD images into 13 building component classes which include classes of small components such as duct, plumbing, conduit, etc. In [17] , the authors used deep learning to detect and differentiate between different pipes in laser scanning point clouds of industrial facilities. 2.2. Object detection networks and transfer learning In computer vision, object detection refers to identifying an object and precisely estimating its location [18] . One of the most widely used algorithms in object detection is RCNN [19] . In rcnn, regions of interest are identified first and then classified by CNN to detect objects in the regions. Since original RCNN is relatively slow, some variants of RCNN have been proposed, like fast-RCNN [20] , mask-RCNN [21] . In the AEC domain, researchers have also applied and proposed different network architectures to achieve their research objectives, for example defect and damage detection ( [22] , [23] , [24] ), worker detection on construction sites ( [25] , [26] , [27] ). A neural network can be trained from scratch on a specific dataset. However, in order to achieve optimal results, it requires a large training set as well as substantial processing time [28] . Therefore, transfer learning [29] is proposed to overcome the problems and improve per - formance. Transfer learning is a process where a neural network is pre- trained on a related larger dataset and re-trained on a user-specific dataset. Currently, there are several large, publicly available datasets that are used to pre-train a neural network, such as ImageNet [30] , which contains more than one million images for training, the Pascal VOC 2012 dataset that contains more than 20,000 images [31] , the COCO dataset contains more than 300,000 images [32] with 2.5 million instances. 2.3. Text detection and recognition In a building, some elements contain texts and numbers that are also valuable for facility management, such as room numbers on a door sign. In large facilities, entities of some electrical elements (such as smoke alarms, emergency switches) usually have a unique serial number in order to clearly label entities and make facility management more effi - cient. It is also very helpful to attach this information to the objects in the building twin, recognising and identifying objects at an instance level. There are usually two steps to extracting the information from images: text detection and text recognition. With regard to text detection, neural networks that are used in object detection can also be used to detect text in an image, such as Mask-RCNN [21] because text area can also be considered a type of object. Re - searchers hav ... e inserted an intermediate step between text detection and text recognition. In this step, the detected text bounding box would be rotated to the position where its longer side is horizontal by assuming texts are oriented along the longer side. Two angles (clockwise and counterclockwise) can rotate the bounding box to the horizontal position and produce two new bounding boxes. One of the angles would flip the text. The two new bounding boxes are then the input for the text recognition step. The flipped texts can be discarded by the lower prediction score, and the results are shown in Section 4.2.3 . In summary, the input to the proposed processing pipeline are im - ages/videos and point clouds. Point clusters with semantic information are created by mapping semantic information detected by deep learning to the 3D point cloud. The 3D mesh model is reconstructed by fitting geometric shapes to point clusters and then enriched by useful infor - mation that is valuable for maintaining the building by detecting and recognising text information on or close to objects. 4. The aim of ray-casting is to make points visible in the real world that can also be seen in the point cloud. At the same time, it should not “ look through ” the wall either, seeing points that should be occluded. Therefore, the voxel size in Fig. 8 is essential. Fig. 21 shows a comparison of four different voxel size: 2 mm, 5 mm, 1 cm, 2 cm. As we can see, rays can still go through the wall with a resolution of 2 mm and 5 mm, which makes the scene behind the wall visible. With a resolution of 2 cm, the handrail and its fence cause too much occlusion, making a relatively large part of the wall that should not be occluded invisible. In this case, the voxel size of 1 cm provides the best result. Moreover, the test point cloud resolution is also 1 cm in Fig. 21 . This is not a coincidence, because a 1 cm resolution point cloud means the distance between neighbouring points is around 1 cm. Therefore, it is appropriate that the voxel size chosen for ray-casting is the same as the resolution of a point cloud, so that rays do not pass through a surface and at the same time avoid unnecessary occlusions. 4.4. Introduction The construction industry has long acknowledged the considerable risks inherent in tower crane operations. Numerous academic studies and industrial reports have demonstrated the potential compromise of crane stability due to unsafe hoisting operations including untested lifting, aggressive operation, and excessive swinging ( Wiethorn, 2018 ). While tower crane operators generally receive trainings regarding operational specifications, violations are inevitable during the actual construction process. Therefore, proactive intervention is necessary to guarantee operational safety. Previous research has introduced proactive hoisting monitoring methods that can leverage Internet of Things (IoT) techniques to address issues like excessive loads, blind spots, and collisions ( Zhu et al., 2023 ). Some studies have amalgamated data from various sensors to prevent intrusion events, particularly unauthorized entry into the hoisting operation area ( Gu et al., 2012 ). Nevertheless, these studies often overlook the operational roles of tower crane operators, and do not extensively monitor various violations that may occur during the entire hoisting process. Recent studies have increasingly adopted digital twin (DT) to capi - talize on the immersive experience for hoisting safety. The DT can simulate realistic hoisting operations before actual construction, facili - tating technical disclosure ( Sepasgozar, 2020 ), remote training ( Li et al., 2012 ), and other activities. Additionally, it provides comprehensive information to improve the navigation of hoisting routes ( Ji and Leite, 2018 ). Studies have also incorporated finite element mechanical models into the DT of the tower crane to assess stability during hoisting oper - ations ( Jiang et al., 2022 ). Existing applications of DT in tower crane operations primarily concentrate on simulation and mapping scenarios, resulting in underutilization of DT for controlling event occurrences. Unsafe tower crane operations Worker operations play a pivotal role in determining the safety risks associated with tower crane operations. In-depth safety analysis by Im and Park (2020) focused on crane safety regulations in South Korea, meticulously examining 288 safety provisions. Their findings high - lighted the need for enhanced monitoring of worker operations. Chen et al. (2022) suggested that the optimal management strategy for tower crane operators involves stringent supervision, comprehensive mainte - nance practices, and strict adherence to safety regulations. Moreover, the Crane Safety Index identified the operator proficiency as a crucial factor shaping the safety landscape of crane operations (Mohandes et al., 2022). Lingard et al. (2021) formulated a model to comprehend the root causes of crane safety events, qualitatively analyzing lifting-related ac - cidents and identifying a total of 77 contributing factors. Among these factors, several are intertwined with worker behaviors, such as de - viations from instructions, lack of familiarity with machinery, and non-compliance with procedures. Similarly, case studies conducted in Hong Kong (Tam and Fung, 2011) and South Korea (Shin, 2015) underscored the prominent role of worker operations in on-site safety management. On a global scale, various countries and regions have established standards that incorporate safety protocols for hoisting operations. Categorizing unsafe hoisting operations within these regulatory frame - works can be achieved through three principles. Firstly, by restricting the extraction of operational specifications to instances involving the practical use of tower cranes, excluding organizational management, installation, removal, and maintenance processes. Secondly, by focusing on operation specifications relevant only to tower crane operators, rather than other workers at the construction site. Thirdly, by scruti - nizing operation specifications directly linked to the activity itself, without encompassing workers’ physiological, psychological, or training aspects. Conclusion The study introduces an early-warning methodology that combines DT and knowledge graph to promptly identify unsafe hoisting opera - tions of tower cranes. A pivotal aspect of this methodology involves synchronously mapping operational data for the tower crane by creating a DT. Subsequently, this data undergoes analysis to detect unsafe hoisting operations using a bespoke knowledge graph. The effectiveness of this methodology is validated through meticulously designed exper - iments, wherein the results indicate that the confidence intervals of the mapped data pertaining to lifting, luffing, and rotation variations encompass the theoretical data. The results also reveal that the warning rate for identifying unsafe hoisting operations surpass 70%, while simultaneously maintaining the false alarm rate below 20%. Moreover, the DT consistency is evaluated, and high-quality mapping is realized in geometric size, physical properties, operation status and management rules. These findings underscore the efficacy of the proposed method - ology in pinpointing and preventing unsafe hoisting operations. Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that this study primarily focused on a scaled-down tower crane within a laboratory setting, neglecting the influence of the dynamic site environment on the preci - sion of the warnings. In more intricate on-site operations, there likely exist operator-specific habits, which introduce further disturbances that necessitate additional investigation. Additionally, the introduced DT may encounter cumulative errors in terms of data mapping. Future research directions can be pursued along two avenues. Firstly, there is a need to analyze the sources of consistency disparities in the DT of the tower crane and to develop a more comprehensive mapping framework. Secondly, it is essential to expand the range of operational scenarios for tower cranes by accruing empirical data from real-world hoisting activities. Introduction As critical components of transportation infrastructure, bridges are highly susceptible to damage due to external or internal factors such as natural disasters, heavy traffic loads, or material degradation, which can result in serious casualties and economic losses [ 1 ]. Historical disasters, including the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, the 2023 Pazarcik earth - quake, and the 2020 Medicane Ianos, as well as the flood in the Western Black Sea Basin of Turkey in 2021, have severely disrupted regional highway transportation systems, highlighting the vulnerability of bridges [ 2 – 5 ]. From 1996 to 2017, typhoons caused 83 % of Taiwan ’ s bridges to collapse [ 6 ]. In India, the major cause of damage to bridges is natural disasters, with 80.30 %, of which hydraulic-induced causes 51.19 %. Another major cause is the deterioration of materials (10.10 %), followed by design and construction (4.13 %), overloading (3.28 %) and human factors at 2.19 % [ 7 ]. These incidents underscore the urgent need for comprehensive disaster management strategies for bridges, encompassing pre-disaster warning, emergency response, and post- disaster recovery [ 8 ]. Despite significant advancements in disaster prevention and mitigation technologies, such as satellites, drones, and sensor networks, their integration and implementation across different disaster manage - ment phases of bridges remains fragmented. This fragmentation limits the acquisition, transmission, and management of real-time data, which is essential for informed decision-making during disasters. Current sys - tems often fail to provide the real-time, dynamic information needed for effective disaster management, resulting in delays in response and re - covery. Unlike other infrastructures, such as urban buildings or water networks, bridges face unique challenges in disaster management due to their critical role in maintaining connectivity [ 9 ]. For instance, flooding and earthquakes can compromise bridge stability and functionality, leading to severe consequences for rescue operations and regional mobility. These challenges highlight the need for a unified real-time disaster management system tailored specifically to bridge infrastructure. The digital transformation of infrastructure management and the adoption of emerging technologies have opened new avenues for real- time disaster management [ 10 ]. The rapid development of information and communication tech - nologies (ICT), such as the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), data analytics (DA), laser scanning (LS), machine learning (ML), and virtual and augmented reality (VR and AR), has made it possible to gather disaster-related data for real-time disaster assessment, emer - gency response, and post-disaster recovery [ 13 – 16 ]. Most of this research emphasises either enhancing the perfor - mance of bridges before a disaster occurs or facilitating rapid rescue and recovery efforts after a disaster. Bridges are load-bearing structures where minor damages can quickly escalate into critical failures under disasters. This highlights the importance of real-time, dynamic data integration to prevent potential collapses. Moreover, research on real- time disaster management has predominantly focused on urban envi - ronments, with limited in-depth studies focusing specifically on bridges [ 10 ]. This points to a critical gap in the integration of advanced tech - nologies within bridge disaster management, which is necessary to ensure comprehensive coverage across all phases of bridge disaster management — from real-time disaster warning and emergency response to disaster assessment and recovery. These studies suggest that leveraging BIM and DT technol - ogies can enhance the timeliness, coordination, and efficiency of bridge disaster management by unifying technology systems and improving the flow of critical information across all disaster phases. By examining these gaps, this paper highlights the urgent need for a comprehensive real-time disaster management system for bridges. This framework aims to achieve technology interoperability and information sharing in bridge disaster management, facilitating iterative and adaptive management practices throughout all phases of the disaster cycle to ensure bridges are more resilient and responsive to disasters. Methodology In this paper, a systematic review methodology was employed, adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework, to critically evaluate and synthe - sise existing literature on real-time bridge disaster management. The PRISMA framework provides a structured approach for conducting and reporting systematic reviews, ensuring transparency and reproducibility in the research process [ 28 ]. Data were systematically extracted and analysed from publications focusing on disaster types, enabling tech - nologies, and application frameworks. The findings were synthesised to provide a holistic understanding of current advancements and gaps in the field, ultimately informing the development of a conceptual real- time bridge disaster management framework. The process of article retrieval and screening, as illustrated in Fig. 1 , further supports the systematic approach taken in this research. • Identification . The process of selecting articles for the systematic review on bridge disaster management began with a comprehensive search using the Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus as the target databases for literature search and analysis. For Web of Sci - ence, the search utilised “ TS ” (Topic) as the main field, encompassing the subject, abstract, and keywords of the paper, while for Scopus, the equivalent field “ TITLE-ABS-KEY ” was employed. Advanced Boolean operators ( “ AND ” and “ OR ” ) were used to generate various keyword combinations, including “ Bridge ” and “ Disaster ” , “ Disaster management ” and “ Real-time ” , “ Digital Twin ” and “ Bridge ” , “ Digital model ” and “ Bridge ” , “ Bridge ” and “ Performance ” and “ Response ” . “ Performance ” and “ Response ” , “ Bridge ” and “ Recovery ” . A total of 574 papers were retrieved from the Web of Science, supplemented by 338 additional papers from Scopus, resulting in 912 papers initially identified. In contrast, the real-time nature of disaster response specifically addresses the immediate aftermath of an emergency event. Considering the issue of update synchronization between BIM and FEM, the parametric modeling of the IFC model and FEM is achieved based on the common geometric information extracted from multi-stage scans. Conside- ring the issue of information loss between two scanning stages, the FEM of the previous stage is used as a starting point, and the calculation results based on construction progress and temperature data are integrated to supplement the deficient structural geometric informa- tion. The creation of a continuous dynamic IFC model is achieved based on construction progress and deformed cable shapes that are derived from finite element calculations. The proposed method can serve for the construction simulation, cable force prediction and visualization of construction process of cable structures. The calculated deformation of the FEM at each stage is within 4 mm, demonstrating that the established model is in a state of equilibrium. The prediction deviations of cable shapes and forces between the FEM results from the previous stage and measurement results of the next stage are within 1.3% and 1.5%, respectively. The proposed workflow can be applied to larger or more complex cable-net structures. The modeling process is only based on node coordinates and connection relationship determination. Once the geometric relation- ship between the cables and other components is determined, the geometric models of the cables can be realized by approximating curved elements with straight segments, and other components can also be paramet- rically modeled based on this relationship. The finite element calculation approach achieves the equilibrium state of the cable net structure by applying external loads and internal cable forces. It is also applicable to other types of cable net structures. There are still some limitations in the work presented in this paper that can be improved in the future. For example, the current connecting members are only considered as a rectangular prism, and the complex internal components are not included. The IFC model includes only geometric data currently, while other information such as material data, load information, and construction information can be supplemented in IFC data. The FEM in this paper assumes that the displacement deformation at the cable anchorage is zero, and its boundary stiffness can be adjusted based on monitoring data or other information.
