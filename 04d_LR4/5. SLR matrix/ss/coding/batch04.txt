The global spatial distribution of major landslide disasters is shown in Figure1 [1]. The geological environment in China is complex, especially landslide disasters, which have become among the main types of geological disasters. According to the data of the frst national comprehensive risk survey of natural disasters, 132,000 hidden landslide hazards have been identifed and registered nationwide, which shows that the distribution of landslide disasters in China is very ex- tensive and that the demand for landslide monitoring and early warning is urgent. Landslides are usually caused by the coupling of a variety of complex factors, such as earthquakes, rainfall and climate change [2], and their occurrence is very concealed and sudden. In addition, their development law is complex, and accurate determination of the early warning threshold is difcult, which presents great challenges for slope stability monitoring and risk prevention and control. Landslides and other geological disasters not only severely damage the surrounding ecological environment but also directly endanger the infrastructure and safety of people’s lives and property. Therefore, it is highly im- portant to carry out real-time safety monitoring of slopes and comprehensively and dynamically determine their deformation state and development trend to efectively reduce disaster losses and improve the ability to prevent and mitigate disasters. Traditional slope safety monitoring technology (such as manual inspection, total station monitoring and displacement metre monitoring) has difculty addressing the challenges associated with landslide prevention and measurement. Although digital twin technology based on surrogate models has been widely used in slope safety monitoring, the current research still has the following gaps: (1) in slope safety monitoring, the methods of numerical simulation analysis and monitoring data analysis are relatively mature, but real-time monitoring data and numerical simulations are not integrated for comprehensive monitoring; (2) the prediction accuracy of common surrogate models is often insufcient when dealing with highly nonlinear and complex scenarios of slope deformation and (3) existing models often lack adaptive optimisation mechanisms based on real-time monitoring data. In view of these research gaps, this study proposes a digital twin modelling method for slopes based on a surrogate model and its adaptive optimisation ability, with the aim being to replace expensive numerical simulations with high-precision surrogate models and directly derive the full-feld displacement of slopes from limited monitoring data. The diferences between the digital twin model and the numerical simulation model; the pure data-driven model are shown in Table1. A numerical simulation model is a high-fdelity model based on a physical mechanism that generates high- quality data by solving partial diferential equations, but its computational cost is high, and it has difculty meeting the requirements of real-time analysis. The pure data-driven model directly learns the relationship between the input and output from the monitoring data without the constraint of a physical mechanism. Although its calculation efciency is high, it strongly depends on the quality and quantity of monitoring data, which contradicts the fact that the number of slope monitoring points is limited. In this study, a numerical simulation model is used to construct and update the high-precision virtual slope, and a pure data-driven model (i.e., a surrogate model) ensures the real-time calculation of the digital twin model. The framework of the slope digital twin monitoring system proposed in this study is shown in Figure2. The framework is composed of fve core parts: an actual slope, a virtual slope, a slope surrogate model, an Internet of Things (IOT) data acquisition system [24] and a visual display platform. Through the dynamic interaction and efcient integrated processing of multisource heterogeneous data, the system achieves full-range real-time monitoring of the actual state of the slope. As the physical foundation of the digital twin system, the actual slope contains real geological structure information, such as information related to accurate stratigraphic division, fracture occurrence and rock and soil mechanical parameters. The digitisation of its geometric and physical properties can be achieved by UAV tilt photography, geological section modelling, laser scanning and other technologies. A variety of sensors are arranged inside and on the surface of the slope, including in- clinometers, earth pressure boxes, vibration accelerometers, rain gauges and displacement metres, to continuously collect real- time dynamic data such as surface deformation displacement, internal earth pressure, pore water pressure and environmental parameters and provide real-time data input for the construction and updating of virtual slopes. As the digital mapping of the actual slope, the virtual slope is constructed on the basis of the mechanical parameters of the rock and soil, geological structure characteristics and real-time sensing data. It can dynamically refect the actual state of a slope and predict and analyse its future behaviour. It is the core of numerical simulation and state deduction. As a lightweight calculation module in the framework, the slope surrogate model extracts key features from the high-dimensional simulation data generated by the virtual slope based on deep learning technology and constructs a high-precision approximate calculation model. The module has the characteristics of rapid response and low demand for computing resources. It can quickly output stability assessments and early warning in- formation in scenarios where high-fdelity numerical simula- tions are difcult to run in real time and signifcantly enhance the real-time performance of the digital twin system. The IOT system performs data transmission from the actual slope to the digital twin platform and relies on wireless communication technologies such as Lora, 5G, Nb-IOT and edge computing methods to transmit multisource heterogeneous monitoring data to the cloud processing centre in real time. In the process of data integration, the system performs cleaning and fusion to ensure the reliability and consistency of the data and provide stable data support for the dynamic updating of the virtual slope and surrogate model. As the core interface of human‒computer interactions, the visual monitoring platform integrates real-time monitoring data, a three-dimensional virtual model and prediction analysis re- sults. Nevertheless, conventional LiDAR systems sometimes need more support in their extensive applicability owing to their cumbersome dimensions, elevated expenses, and intricate operating pre - requisites [ 24 , 25 ]. In light of this study context, exploring and developing more portable, cost-efficient, and user-friendly LiDAR monitoring systems has emerged as a significant research focus. In order to realize the all-round monitoring of the quality of residential projects, this paper proposes a sky-space-ground integrated remote sensing monitoring methodology, constructs a data-SSIoT-RBIM integrated digital twin framework, selects multi-source sen - sors such as LIDAR, GPS, automatic monitoring robots and so on according to the monitoring requirements, and develops and designs a multi-sensor-integrated cloud platform for monitoring the quality of residential projects, so as to realize sky-space-ground integrated monitoring of residential projects. This framework provides a new research paradigm for intelligent big data management and multi- source heterogeneous data fusion for residential project quality monitoring. 2. Methodology 2.1. Digital twin architecture and framework 2.1.1. SSIoT-RBIM system architecture This research introduces an SSIoT-RBIM architecture for digital twins to oversee the quality of residential construction. The ar - chitecture comprises data acquisition, transmission, digital modeling, data/model integration, and service layers (see Fig. 1 ). The data acquisition layer serves as the cornerstone of the digital twin and is responsible for collecting the necessary information from various data sources. This layer deal with the challenges of heterogeneity and large amounts of data from the building level, including data of different types, formats, and sources. In particular, each sub-asset has its unique sub-data identifiers that can provide the required data, information, or models when the corresponding query is received. Various data collection technologies are used in this layer, including contactless technologies (e.g., RFID, image-based technologies), distributed sensor networks, wireless commu - nications, and mobile access. The selection and implementation of these technologies need to be customized based on the asset type and expected data processing needs to ensure real-time data collection and efficient management. The principal duty of the data transmission layer is to guarantee the secure and efficient transfer of gathered data to a higher level for subsequent modeling and analysis. This layer accommodates several communication technologies, encompassing short-range access networks (including WiFi, Zigbee, NFC, M2M, and Z-Wave) and broader-range communication technologies (such as 3G, 4G, LTE, 5G, and LP-WAN). As technology progresses, although WiFi remains the predominant wireless LAN technology, alternatives such as Li-Fi and LP-WAN are becoming acknowledged as viable options for creating digital twins at the city and building levels, particularly regarding security and unlicensed frequency bands. Critical concerns at the transport layer are network energy efficiency, transmission velocity, data security, and the imperative to maintain data integrity and privacy protection throughout transmission. The data modeling layer is essential for creating and managing digital representations of physical assets. This layer utilizes Building Information Modeling (BIM) and City Information Modeling (CIM) to store intricate data regarding buildings and urban assets, incorporating external factors that influence these assets, including meteorological and socio-cultural contexts. The Data/Model Integration Layer is essential to the digital twin architecture and effectively integrates and processes all data resources. Introduction Cable-stayed bridges constitute a prominent category of long-span structures acclaimed for their superior structural efficiency, architec - tural aesthetics, and construction adaptability [1,2] . In practice, reliable bridge condition assessment is essential for ensuring operational safety and supporting risk-informed maintenance, because long-span cable- - stayed systems are highly sensitive to localized deterioration and stiff - ness/force redistribution in safety-critical components. Nevertheless, the inherent complexities of these structures, such as geometric non - linearities and material property uncertainties, pose considerable chal - lenges for accurate condition assessment. In long-term service, key components such as girders, stay cables, pylons, and anchorage systems inevitably deteriorate due to fatigue damage [3] , material aging, and environmental corrosion, which can reduce structural performance and trigger safety risks [4] . Since condition assessment fundamentally relies on comparing measured and predicted structural responses, establishing reliable Finite Element (FE) models and conducting effective parameter identification are critical but technically demanding tasks for integrity assurance and maintenance optimization [5 – 7] . Consequently, the fi - delity of bridge FE models governs the precision of structural response analysis and prediction capabilities [8,9] . The mechanical responses of cable-stayed bridges are governed by numerous interdependent parameters, such as material properties, stayed-cable pretension forces, tower stiffness, and bearing support conditions [7,10] . Conventional parameter identification methods have often relied on single-objective optimization frameworks [11] , wherein individual response metrics such as stayed-cable forces [12 – 14] or bridge deflections [15] serve as optimization targets. Although compu - tationally tractable, these approaches exhibit a fundamental deficiency: optimization of individual response parameters fails to ensure consis - tency across other critical structural behaviors, resulting in parameter estimates with limited adaptability and reliability. However, integrating these techniques into a cohesive framework spe - cifically tailored for cable-stayed bridge parameter identification re - mains an open challenge. This work represents the first integrated solution that systematically addresses both complexity and uncertainty challenges in large-scale bridge parameter identification, providing a robust and computationally efficient approach essential for practical engineering applications and advancing the state-of-the-art in structural health monitoring and digital twin technologies. 2. Methods This section presents the methodological framework for digital twin- enabled multi-objective optimization in bridge parameter identification. The methods comprise problem definition, multi-objective optimization formulation with constraint systems and sensitivity analysis, and an improved NSGA-III algorithm featuring adaptive mechanisms for enhanced convergence and robustness. Each component addresses computational complexity and measurement uncertainty in large-scale structural parameter identification. 2.1. Problem definition Let θ = [ θ 1 , θ 2 , … , θ n ] T ∈ Θ ⊂ ℝ n denote the vector of parameters to be identified, where the feasible domain Θ is determined by lower and upper bounds θ min K ≤ θ K ≤ θ max K , ensuring physical plausibility and nu - merical stability according to design specifications, material tolerances, and field calibrations. The identification problem encompasses 156 parameters organized into four categories ( Table 1 ): (1) pretension forces for 144 stay cables grouped into 36 symmetric sets; (2) deck material properties including elastic and shear moduli; (3) pylon structural parameters comprising elastic moduli and moment-of-inertia correction factors; and (4) modal damping ratios for the first five modes. Given the high-dimensional nature of this optimization task, sensitivity analysis is employed to identify the most influential parameters for subsequent multi-objective optimization, thereby maintaining solution accuracy while achieving computational efficiency. The ﬁrst employs geostatistical methods like the Kriging method, sequential Gaussian models, and multiple-point geosta tistics to describe variables regionally through variogram functions ( Bian et al., 2024 ). However, these models often fall short in capturing the complex cyclicity and environmental interplays in lithological sequences ( Bi et al., 2015 ). The second approach utilizes Markov models, such as Markov random ﬁeld (MRF) and chain models ( Wang et al., 2024a ), to construct stochastic geological proﬁles. This method has been applied in the assessment of slope and tunnel stability (Wang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018b ). MRF methods, aligning closely with strata formation processes and spatial heterogeneity, provide more interp retable geological insights compared to variogram-based approaches ( Elfeki & Dekking, 2001; Wang, 2020 ). Additionally, by discretiz- ing geological proﬁles using MRF, the resulting units align with the cyclical process of DES simulation, thereby enhancing the integration of model modules. Conse- quently, analyzi ng geological uncertainty via MRF emerges as a logical progression towards achieving more accurate simulation results ( Cho, 2007; Fent on & Griﬃths, 2008 ). With the rapid advancement of internet-based informa- tion technology, new possibilities have emerg ed for inno- vating tunnel PSM models ( Phoon et al., 2022 ). Current PSM models largely remain theoretical and disconnected from on-site realities ( Persson & Araldi, 2009 ), leading to slow, inaccurate responses to site conditions and limiting their ability to provide actionab le guidance to stakehold- ers, thus reducing their practical value ( Yu et al., 2023 ). The core issue lies in the lack of eﬀective interaction and deep integration between construction sites and digital models ( Li et al., 2024a ). In the context of transitioning towards Industry 4.0 ( Phoon et al., 2024 ), the introduction of digital twin (DT) technol ogy oﬀers a potential solution. The contemporary consensus deﬁnes DT as a theoretical ﬁve-dimensional framework comprising physical en tities, virtual entities, connections, DT data, and services ( Liu et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2018). DT describes, maps, and pre- dicts the semantic, geometric, and behavioral characteris- tics of physical entities through virtual representations and data-driven models ( Samaniego et al., 2020 ), thereby enabling functions such as real-time perception, dynamic control, and information sharing ( Tao et al., 2019b ). Since its introduction to tunnel engineering around 2020, DT has been the focus of recent studies exploring its application (Opoku et al., 2021 ). Ye et al. Research Background and Context Smart cities are increasingly recognized as a model capable of addressing future climate challenges driven by rapid urbanization, prompting the development of related research and business models [1]. Concurrently, the Fourth Industrial Revolution has intro- duced digital twin technology—leveraging IoT, AI, and big data—to drive transformative innovations in construction and infrastructure management [2]. The concept of the digital twin was first proposed by Grieves in a 2002 lecture [3] and was later refined into a more advanced framework by NASA in 2010 [ 4]. Within smart cities, digital twins are being increasingly applied to fields such as urban planning, traffic management, and environmen- tal monitoring [2]. Notably, ongoing research actively explores the integration of real-time data with Building Information Modeling (BIM) for infrastructure management [5,6]. BIM serves as an effective data format for managing project information across its entire lifecycle, enabling facility condition monitoring through real-time data synchronization [7]. Numer- ous efforts are underway to enhance construction and maintenance efficiency through this integration [8,9], with BIM improving data interoperability and optimizing maintenance Buildings 2025, 15, 1312 https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings15081312 Buildings 2025, 15, 1312 2 of 22 processes for architectural asset performance [ 7]. Furthermore, BIM is evolving into a technology that bridges the physical and digital realms by merging real-time data with static models [ 10]. Recent studies suggest that integrating BIM with digital twins can significantly enhance Life Cycle Sustainability Assessments (LCSAs) and maintenance efficiency [11,12]. Against this backdrop, there is a pressing need for innovative approaches to overcome the limitations of traditional static maintenance methods and enable real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance in urban infrastructure. 1.2. Motivation and Objectives Noise Barrier Tunnels (NBTs) are essential urban infrastructures that mitigate traffic noise, requiring advanced technologies for effective long-term maintenance and perfor- mance [13,14]. However, the current methods, which rely on periodic visual inspections and passive maintenance, fail to capture real-time conditions, reducing efficiency and com- promising safety [7]. The 2022 fire at the North Uiwang Interchange NBT on South Korea’s Second Gyeongin Expressway, which caused five deaths and over 40 injuries, highlights the urgent need for systematic maintenance and real-time monitoring [15]. Despite increasing interest, the integrated use of BIM and digital twins in infrastruc- ture like NBTs remains limited. While prior studies have explored BIM and IoT integration in construction and maintenance workflows, most remain theoretical with few real-world applications. For NBTs, which require continuous oversight, research on digital twin appli- cations is particularly needed [13]. Most existing work focuses on conceptual frameworks for data integration using BIM and IoT, yet practical validation in infrastructure contexts remains inadequate [7]. In contrast, combining BIM and digital twins offers clear advan- tages, such as intuitive 3D-based maintenance, real-time condition monitoring via IoT, and predictive maintenance support [16]. However, applying these integrated technologies to NBTs is still in its nascent stages [ 17]. The utilization of real-time sensor data within a BIM-based digital twin facilitates the comparison of actual structural or environmental conditions with predefined performance thresholds in the model. In the event of the detec- tion of anomalies, such as abnormal vibration, pitch variation, or irregular illuminance, the system provides immediate feedback to enable preventive action. Therefore, this study proposes the development of a BIM-based digital twin moni- toring system for solar-powered NBTs in South Korea. The system aims to overcome the limitations of traditional periodic inspections, namely their inability to capture dynamic changes and to improve maintenance efficiency by supporting early anomaly detection through data analysis. 1.3. Research Scope and Methodology To surmount the limitations of conventional periodic inspections and enhance the efficiency of maintenance, this study developed and implemented a BIM-based digital twin monitoring system for solar-powered NBTs in South Korea. The research commenced with a review of extant studies on digital twins and BIM to define the project’s objectives and requirements. A 3D BIM model of the tunnel and a design automation tool were created using Autodesk Revit and Dynamo. An IoT sensor network was then deployed to collect real-time data, including but not limited to vibration, tilt, illuminance, air quality, and water detection, from both the interior and exterior of the tunnel. The collected data were stored in CSV and JSON formats and synchronized with the BIM model to dynamically reflect the tunnel’s condition within the digital twin en- vironment. The sensor readings were interpreted in two distinct ways—firstly, as direct measurements, and secondly, as values compared against predefined baselines or thresh- olds according to the sensor type. For instance, initial tilt angles were used to detect Buildings 2025, 15, 1312 3 of 22 abnormal pitch changes, and vibration intensity was assessed relative to safe operating limits. While real-time alerts are delivered through the dashboard based on detected devi- ations, the BIM model provides a spatial reference to contextualize sensor locations and conditions. Therefore, anomalies are interpreted through the data stream itself, rather than being directly visualized within the 3D model. The overall methodology is outlined in Figure 1. While basic alerts based on device-reported flags were displayed on the dashboard, a more detailed evaluation of anomaly patterns, such as irregular time intervals or abnormal sensor readings, was performed offline using raw data analysis. This two-tiered approach allowed for verification of data integrity and sensor reliability under field conditions. The final phase of the methodology evaluated the system’s integration performance and its ability to support real-time monitoring and maintenance decision-making, providing insight for future enhancements. Integration of BIM and Digital Twin Concepts A digital twin is an advanced technology that integrates real-time data with a virtual representation of a physical object or system to enable continuous monitoring and predictive analysis. Originally introduced by Grieves in 2002, and further developed by NASA in 2010, the digital twin concept has evolved into a dynamic system for prediction and operational optimization [3,4,18]. Building Information Modeling (BIM) is a digital framework that captures the three- dimensional geometry, material properties, and construction sequence of buildings and road infrastructure, enabling lifecycle data management. Although BIM is inherently static, its integration with digital twins transforms it into a dynamic environment capable of reflecting real-time conditions via sensor and operational data [13,23]. Recent studies have shown that BIM provides the foundational layer for digital twin environments, where the integration of IoT-based sensing and AI-driven predictive algorithms significantly improves operational monitoring and safety management, particularly in tunnel and NBT applications [24–27]. Continuous condition updates in this integrated environment improve maintenance decision-making and infrastructure resilience. In addition, Kritzinger et al. (2018) categorized digital twins in manufacturing and affirmed their potential in predicting and maintaining complex systems [ 28]. However, their focus on manufacturing highlights the need for further exploration in the context of construction infrastructure. Kim and Kim (2020) applied digital twins to predict the lifetime of NBT components, demonstrating how they address the limitations of periodic inspections and validate the integration of BIM with IoT-based monitoring [13]. Similarly, Xu et al. (2021) demonstrated that real-time prediction and maintenance models based on digital twins can enhance the management of complex systems, providing valuable insights for the system design in this study [20]. Interdisciplinary advances are essential for the development of BIM-DT frameworks, especially for NBT monitoring. Alshammari et al. (2021) highlighted the importance of cybersecurity, such as encryption and authentication, to protect real-time sensor data, which is critical for NBT reliability [29]. (2024) extended the scope by incorporating digital twins into immersive metaverse environments to support enhanced design review, training, and lifecycle management [33]. In summary, the integration of BIM and digital twins enables dynamic infrastructure representation that goes beyond the static limitations of BIM alone. Previous studies and interdisciplinary developments confirm the value of this approach in improving mainte- nance efficiency and safety. However, several limitations emerged that constrain its full practical application. Most notably, intermittent power supply issues during the demonstration led to data loss and delayed sensor activation, particularly for the air quality and tilt modules. For instance, Air Quality Sensor 01 did not commence data collection until the first week of the demonstration and exhibited approximately 69.3% coverage during its active period. Despite the implementation of proper synchronization mechanisms, sporadic network instability also contributed to temporal discontinuities in the data stream. Another key constraint lies in the limited real-time diagnostic capability of the current dashboard implementation. While the system does support immediate alerts based on sensor-reported flags (e.g., battery failure or sensor state anomalies), more advanced anomaly patterns (e.g., gradual pitch angle drift or irregular illuminance cycles) require offline post-analysis. This architectural separation, therefore, limits the system’s capacity to provide immediate, data-driven maintenance suggestions. Furthermore, the demonstration was conducted on a single infrastructure type and site, which limits the generalizability of the findings. To ensure the effectiveness of the system in real-world scenarios, further validation is necessary to ascertain the compatibility of sensors with diverse infrastructure types, such as bridges or metro corridors, as well as to determine environment-specific thresholds and the system’s long-term operational resilience. Finally, the current system is deficient in the realm of real-time recovery and buffering mechanisms, which are imperative for the management of transient data outages resulting from power or network disruptions. Without such mechanisms, the reliability and precision of monitoring may be jeopardized under field conditions. 5.3. Direction for Future Research To enhance the scalability and utility of the proposed BIM–digital twin system, future research should prioritize the integration of real-time analytics and predictive models. While this study applied threshold-based and interval analyses offline, the incorporation of such logic into the live platform will enable automated anomaly detection and early intervention. As Kaewunruen et al. (2021) observed, predictive maintenance necessitates dynamic analysis that extends beyond basic alerts. Secondly, the validation of the system across diverse infrastructure types, including bridges and tunnels, will assess its scalability and adaptability [37]. It is acknowledged that each context may necessitate customized thresholds and sensor configurations, and the broader implementation will contribute to the generalization of BIM–digital twin frameworks. Thirdly, enhancing the user experi- ence and system resilience is imperative. Operator feedback has underscored the need Buildings 2025, 15, 1312 20 of 22 for more intuitive dashboards, customized alerts, and real-time visualization. Future sys- tems should incorporate secure data transmission, buffering, and failover mechanisms to ensure continuity during power or network disruptions. Finally, the establishment of inter- operable data-sharing frameworks could facilitate collaboration among cities, operators, and researchers. In accordance with Wang et al. (2024), the implementation of standard- ized platforms would expedite the adoption of digital twin technologies and facilitate the development of more comprehensive predictive maintenance strategies [39]. In summary, advancing analytics, expanding deployment, and improving usability and security are key to realizing the full potential of BIM–digital twin systems as robust infrastructure management platforms. INTRODUCTION Concrete has established itself as one of the most widely used materials in modern construction thanks to its low cost, high strength, and ability to adapt to multiple geometries and structural sizes [1]. The incorporation of adva nced sensors has significantly increased the possibility of monitoring concrete, allowing continuous and accurate tracking of its structural condition [2]. Over time, concrete structures can suffer various types of deterioration, such as corrosion, fatigue, or damage from environmental facto rs, making constant monitoring essential to ensure their integrity [3]. According to [4], these sensors are capable of recording variables such as deformation, temperature, humidity, and pressure, thus facilitating the early detection of possible structural failures . In the field of structural monitoring of concrete, different types of sensors are used, including fiber optic, piezoelectric, and pressure sensors [5]. These devices provide essential information about deformation and internal stresses that accumulate over time, allowing for early identification of cracks or unusual structural behavior [6]. Thanks to these technologies, it is possible to obtain accurate data on the response of concrete to both static and dynamic loads, which is essential for properly guiding maintenance and repair work [7]. According to [8], the use of advanced sensors in concrete monitoring improves accurate fault detection and reinforces preventive maintenance strategies. Early identification of defects in structures using sensors has established itself as a highly effective strategy, as it significantly reduces maintenance and repair costs by enabling interventions before damage reaches an advanced stage [9]. This technology also promotes sustainability in the construction sector, as it facilitates continuous monitoring of structures, extends their useful life, and reduces the need for major repairs or complete reconstruction [10]. The effectiveness of structural monitoring depends largely on the quality of the data collected, its correct integration with forecasting systems, and the ability to generate alerts sufficiently in advance [11]. In this regard, technological advances have enhanced ITEGAM-JETIA, Manaus, v.12 n.57, p. 896-905, January/February, 2026. information analysis and processing tools, making it possible to detect and predict structural failures even before they become visible [12]. Likewise, the incorporation of artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms has increased the accurac y of predictions, simultaneously optimizing maintenance management in critical infrastructures [13]. The incorporation of sensors in IoT-based systems enables real-time data collection, allowing for continuous monitoring with a high level of accuracy [14]. This massive collection of information provides a solid basis for identifying patterns in structural behavior and anticipating potential failures [15]. According to [16] The integration of these sensors with remote monitoring platforms has strengthened the control of large-scale infrastructure, significantly reducing the costs associated with inspections. As infrastructure ages, it is essential to monitor it constantly to de tect progressive deterioration that is not visible to the naked eye [17]. Regular inspections using specialized sensors enable damage such as internal concrete corrosion to be identified at an early stage [18]. Advances in detection technologies have made it possible to supplement concrete monitoring with methods such as thermography and infrared imaging, which facilitate the identification of anomalies that, in many cases, are not detected by conventional sensors [19]. The combination of these tools provides a more comprehensive assessment of structural condition and increases accuracy in detecting defects such as hidden cracks or the presence of accumulated moisture, factors that could compromise the stability of the structure [20]. Likewise, recent studies indicate that, in the future, structural monitoring will rely on distributed sensor networks, which will optimize the process of supervising and evaluating the condition of structures [21]. With the evolution of these systems, monitoring is expected to become more efficient and accessible, enabling timely and less costly interventions [22]. II.
