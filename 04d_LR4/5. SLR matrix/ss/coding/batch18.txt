ABSTRACT Managing underground utility tunnels (UUT) poses challenges such as safety risks, limited accessibility, and high training costs. To address these, we developed an effective VR-based UUT management training simulator, integrating digital twins with a multi-degree-of-freedom (DoF) motion platform to provide an immersive, cybersickness-reducing experience. Advances in actuator and computer graphics technology have increased the research and development of virtual reality (VR)-based training simulators that use multi-DoF motion platforms. In this study, the proposed system provides precisely synchronized visual feedback through a head-mounted display and kinematic feedback via four-DoF motor-based actuators, realistically simulating UUT conditions, including momentum shifts such as falls and micro-swaying. To mitigate cybersickness, visual and kinematic acceleration control techniques are employed, ensuring users can comfortably and safely experience the UUT without stability concerns. We used a LiDAR sensor to model the actual UUT for realistic visualization in the VR environment and created various scenario-based content, such as facility installation, inspection, and device repair, based on the physical characteristics inside the UUT. This integrated approach enables users to effectively manage and operate UUT facilities in a safe, controlled virtual setting, bridging the gap between traditional training and real-world operations. UUTs are used to collectively manage multiple types of urban utilities, such as electricity, telecommunications, water, sewer, and gas, in a single underground corridor. They can increase installation and maintenance efficiency, improve urban aesthetics, and reduce ground settlement and traffic congestion by sharing a common infrastructure path. Therefore, UUTs are essential for sustainable urban development in highly urbanized cities or areas with large-scale development. Depending on the size of the country, UUTs range in length from tens of kilometers to tens of thousands of kilometers. Highly developed cities have UUTs of 2 km per thousand inhabitants [1]. In South Korea, urbanization and industrialization in the 1970s spurred the rapid growth of UUTs, totaling about 500 km as of 2024, starting with Y eouido in Seoul in 1977, to house critical infrastructure such as communication networks and power cables. As these UUTs age, inadequate ventilation and humidity control contribute to problems like condensation and corro- sion. Such conditions can trigger fires, network failures, and power outages, with the potential to paralyze cities and cause substantial economic losses. Consequently, numerous studies have been conducted to ensure the sustainable operation and maintenance of UUTs, with most countries implementing design standards and regular maintenance guidelines [2], [3]. Past incidents associated with condensation and corro- sion have underscored the need for effective prevention systems, focusing on improved fire prediction, detection, and response, as well as better environmental management inside the UUTs to reduce equipment malfunctions and downtime [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. To mitigate these risks, workers must conduct frequent inspections and repairs under challenging conditions, including narrow passages and high electromagnetic fields from high-voltage power cables. Prolonged exposure to such environments may lead to physical and psychological distress, and even severe health issues over time [9], [10], [11]. Therefore, addressing the human factor—ensuring worker safety, training, and preparedness—is just as critical as improving infrastructure itself. However, existing approaches focus on improving disaster response and predictive systems but do not address worker safety and training through immersive solutions. In this study, we introduce a novel virtual training method for UUTs by integrating digital twins with a four-degree of freedom (4-DoF) motion platform, thereby enhancing immer- sion and enabling more realistic emergency response and maintenance training. Unlike existing VR training systems, our proposed simulator couples a motion platform, motion recognizer, and high-resolution content while incorporating cybersickness reduction techniques to ensure prolonged user comfort. We leverage threshold-based control—derived from vestibular–visual differences and actual treadmill velocities—to further mitigate cybersickness. This approach is complemented by tracking natural walking patterns via motion capture sensors to estimate and adjust treadmill velocity, ultimately providing a stable and engaging VR experience. We adopted methods from previous studies that identified minimized visual–vestibular gaps as critical for reducing cybersickness and conducted a usability evaluation to comprehensively verify the system’s effectiveness. As a result, this solution not only provides a safer and more controlled training environment but also has the potential to advance VR training technology and safety practices in UUT maintenance and operation, ultimately contributing to the broader field of sustainable urban infrastructure management. RELATED WORKS The proposed VR training systems face two critical chal- lenges: providing naturalistic, immersive motion experiences and mitigating the resulting cybersickness that can arise when visual and vestibular cues are not perfectly aligned. Addressing these issues requires examining both the evo- lution of motion platforms designed to enhance realism and the methods developed to reduce cybersickness in VR environments. A. MOTION PLATFORMS Motion simulation performance is a key factor in the develop- ment of realistic VR training simulators. To simulate multiple DoF motion, most motion platforms adopt motion-cueing algorithms (MCAs) based on a hexapod motion system [12], [13], [14], [15]. Because of their geometric configuration and independent control of each actuator, hexapods can precisely control 6-DoF motion, rendering them versatile for applications that require high accuracy, such as flight and vehicle simulations. Furthermore, modern commercial hexapod systems are equipped with advanced control algorithms and user-friendly interfaces that render integration into various simulation settings facile [16], [17]. However, these systems are driven only within a limited translational range in the space of the structure. They cannot simulate walking, running, or other forms of movement, which considerably reduces immersion and realism when used in VR applications such as physical rehabilitation and sports training. Treadmill simulators are used to simulate walking and running movements in physical rehabilitation, sports train- ing, and military training. Although treadmills that enable users to enjoy physically active VR adventures have been developed [18], [19], [20], they require users to slide across the floor, wearing specially designed shoes with their bodies strapped to the device, resulting in an unnatural walking experience. A high-speed two-dimensional (2D) treadmill with a power transmission mechanism called ‘‘Geared Omni- pulley’’ has been devised [21]; however, the system is VOLUME 13, 2025 19599 N. CYBERSICKNESS REDUCTION METHODS V ection is a sensation of illusory self-motion without actual movement and has been studied comprehensively. Visually induced motion sickness (VIMS) resembles conventional motion sickness but occurs without physical movement [23]. It is categorized into subcategories, namely cybersickness in virtual environments and simulator sickness in driving or flight simulators. When individuals are exposed to virtual environments on large or curved screens, they experience cybersickness. This condition is characterized by nausea, oculomotor disturbances, and disorientation caused by sensory discrepancies between visual, somatosensory, and vestibular inputs. These symptoms occur rapidly, affecting 20%–50% of users [24], [25], [26], [27]. The condition generally occurs when visual cues in a virtual environment do not align with the body’s somatosensory and vestibular senses. Therefore, posture control based on individual sensory priorities is crucial during VR experience [28]. Recent research has demonstrated that VR-based language education systems can enhance immersion and engagement, thereby supporting the effectiveness of VR in educational training. However, the study did not address specific strategies for mitigating cybersickness [29]. Additionally, Anwar et al. introduced a comprehensive framework for evaluating Quality of Experience (QoE) in immersive media consumption within autonomous vehicles, termed the ‘‘Mov- ing Metaverse.’’ The study emphasizes key QoE factors such as presence, immersion, and cybersickness, and discusses the challenges of motion-induced sickness in VR environments. To address these issues, the authors advocate for adaptive content design, network optimization, and the integration of standardized assessment methodologies to minimize motion sickness and enhance user immersion [30]. Sensory conflicts in VR environments are a critical topic of research. Finding effective and intuitive locomo- tion techniques is a challenge for minimizing fatigue and cybersickness. Moreover, maintaining immersion remains challenging. Studies have analyzed scenarios in which users physically move in one direction in a real environment. However, the study reported that motion sickness still occurs when moving in a different direction because of the sensory mismatch between visual and vestibular stimuli. Studies on cue conflict theory have investigated the individual factors that induce cybersickness to diagnose and alleviate symptoms in VR environments. A study found a potential method for assessing visual cue sensitivity to vari- ability [31]. The results demonstrated that a higher sensitivity to motion parallax in VR was a significant predictor of motion sickness severity. Studies have investigated object motion in a VR headset to induce moderate levels of motion sickness. After completing the motion task, the participants viewed stereoscopic videos inducing discomfort in the VR headset. The variables of sensitivity to motion sickness induced by VR varied among individuals. Furthermore, observers tended to self-regulate motion sickness through head movements. The results also revealed that sensitivity considerably affected rotational head movement. Although the suppression of head movements was observed during translational motion, the effect was significant during rotational motion. This phenomenon suggests that rotational motion is a major factor that induces motion sickness in VR. Another study investigated the potential of synchronizing physical movements with visual stimuli to alleviate VR- related discomfort. For instance, a system that uses a motion platform to simulate various levels of visual–vestibular syn- chronization conditions can reduce cybersickness [32]. The results indicated that users experienced lower levels of cyber- sickness when their overall comfort improved. This improve- ment occurred under the synchronized visual–vestibular con- dition in multiple trials. The provided conditions simulated visual–vestibular synchronization between visual rotation and synchronized visual–vestibular motion to generate basic movements. Furthermore, the study included vestibular motion in a fixed self-referenced visual environment. Minimal sensory adjustment was required when visual and vestibular sensations were aligned with one another. Thus, based on the sensory conflict theory, the severity of cybersickness is likely to be regulated according to individual sensitivity characteristics. The techniques used to synchronize motion platforms could be used to design interactive VR systems. However, developing strategies to minimize cybersickness is crucial in motion platforms driven by voluntary movements. In early studies, cybersickness was considered less sen- sitive to the effect of translational motion than to that of rotational motion. However, limited studies have been con- ducted on generalized reference values for the measurement of cybersickness in VR. A consensus is yet to be reached regarding the specific optical flow and intensity that cause cybersickness. This can be attributed to various reasons; however, limited robust empirical evidence regarding the recommended strategies for VR developers to minimize cybersickness could be a major factor. For this reason, one study generalized the measurement of cybersickness among VR users by distinguishing between their sensitivities to translational and rotational motion [33]. CONCLUSION This study provided a realistic UUT training system with minimal motion sickness to participants by precisely con- trolling visual and physical object movements in a virtual environment using a 4-DoF motion platform. The proposed system provides realistic visual and various kinesthetic motion feedback, such as sudden fall while walking and micro-vibration caused by trucks passing by on the ground, by closely examining the real UUT environment and modeling it using a LiDAR sensor. Furthermore, visual and kinesthetic acceleration control methods were applied to minimize motion sickness in participants experiencing VR on a motion platform to provide a comfortable experience training for participants. To evaluate the proposed simulator, we analyzed the cybersickness induced by experiencing the UUT training program with 17 participants of various ages. The results revealed that compared with existing VR solutions, the training not only reduced dizziness and motion sickness but also demonstrated the effectiveness of facility management training in a UUT. The proposed motion platform should be improved con- siderably. Because most of the interior of the UUT is a long straight passageway, the 4-DoF motion platform proposed in this study is sufficient for simulating such a tunnel. However, to satisfy the various types of VR applications, supporting yaw or sway motions is difficult. As the system has 4 DOFs, we are currently working toward adding yaw to create a 5-DOF motion platform to realize an unlimited yaw movement capability of 360◦ through a rotary drive on top of the motion base [53]. This paper presents a framework for heat risk monitoring, enabled by a construction site digital twin. This framework leverages geometric modeling, incorporates real-time weather data from a weather station, and utilizes computational simulations for assessing spatio-temporal heat risks. Its effectiveness was validated through a case study in Stephenville, Texas, USA, where it demonstrated superior fidelity when compared to using the conventional black-globe thermometer. Introduction Heat is a leading cause of weather-related fatalities, posing a serious threat to occupational health and safety [ 1 ]. Of all US industries, the construction sector is particularly susceptible to heat risks, accounting for the highest number of occupational heat-related fatalities [ 2 ]. Here, heat risk refers to the possibility that heat stress (i.e., environmental heat load on the human body) may cause harmful heat strain (i.e., physiological response to heat stress). Such harmful heat strain can lead to decreased work performance [ 3 – 5 ], impaired cognitive function [ 6 – 8 ], and an increased risk of heat-related illnesses, such as heat exhaustion and heat stroke, which are consequences of thermoregula- tory failures due to extreme heat exposure [ 9 – 13 ]. Concerns about heat risks are intensifying in the global construction industry (e.g., Asia [ 14 – 16 ], Europe [ 17 ], Australia [ 18 ], and Africa [ 19 ]), with future projections indicating that climate change will worsen these issues [ 20 ]. To effectively mitigate these risks, it is imperative to understand where and to what extent heat risks threaten the health and safety of construction workers [ 21 , 22 ]. This knowledge leads to the development of informed heat mitigation strategies, such as implementing frequent breaks, rescheduling work activities to cooler periods, and promoting hydration. Nonetheless, measuring heat risks in outdoor environments poses challenges due to the variable nature of heat conditions, influ- enced by factors like shading and different surface materials [ 23 – 25 ]. Additionally, the dynamic nature of construction sites, where the ma- terials and structure heights continually change with ongoing con- struction activities, further complicates these measurements [ 26 ]. Current methods for evaluating heat risk in outdoor construction envi- ronments typically involve mobile environmental sensors carried by workers [ 27 ] or weather stations deployed at a specific location [ 28 – 31 ], often fail to capture the spatial distribution of heat risks. These methods may overestimate or underestimate the risks because they do not account adequately for the spatial and temporal variations in heat exposure [ 32 ]. Consequently, this results in a gap in understanding the heat exposure faced by construction workers, posing challenges for effective safety management. To address this knowledge gap, this study proposes a digital twin- enabled heat risk monitoring framework that facilitates spatio- temporal heat risk analysis for data-driven risk management in out- door construction environments. As illustrated in Fig.1 , this framework is designed to incorporate both visual and weather data collected from a construction site, simulate microclimates to analyze spatial heat risks, and offer actionable and timely information for risk-informed decision- making. Research background 2.1. Heat risk assessments in construction Occupational safety institutions, such as the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), generally employ the wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) to evaluate environmental heat risk [ 22 ]. WBGT is a thermo- physiological model that requires measurements of air temperature, natural wet bulb temperature, and black globe temperature [ 33 ]. NIOSH establishes Recommended Alert Limits (RALs) and Recommended Exposure Limits (RELs) based on the WBGT [ 22 ]. Similarly, ISO 7243 and OSHA provide a structured approach for assessing environmental heat stress using WBGT [ 33 , 34 ], while the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) sets thermal limit values (TLVs) based on WBGT [ 35 ]. Guidelines from NIOSH and ISO emphasize the importance of considering both temporal and spatial factors when measuring environmental heat risks. For instance, NIOSH suggests hourly WBGT measurements during the hottest months and spatial measurements close to the work area, with separate measurements for different subareas if environmental heat varies within a single area [ 22 ]. Similarly, ISO 7243 recommends taking WBGT measurements over one hour during the hot summer months when heat stress is most likely to occur [ 33 ]. It advises addressing spatial and temporal variations by determining the necessary number of measurements and calculating an averaged WBGT value over one hour. Following these established guidelines, the initial step in managing heat risk safety is to plan how to effectively assess spatio-temporal environmental heat risk onsite. Despite prior efforts in heat risk assessments in construction [ 27 – 31 ], current on-site measurement approaches have intrinsic limitations in addressing the spatio-temporal aspects of heat risk assessments. In the spatial context, previous works measure weather conditions using a mobile environmental sensor carried by a worker [ 27 ] or a weather station deployed at a single location [ 28 – 31 ]. Both on-site measurement approaches are limited in capturing the spatial distributions of heat risks due to challenges including logistical and cost constraints associated with multiple on-site measurements, and the need for frequent modifi- cations to adapt to the dynamic nature of construction environments (e. g., deployment locations) [ 36 ]. These limitations can lead to inaccurate evaluations of overall heat stress levels in specific areas, as measure- ments are inherently affected by their specific locations [ 32 ]. Another practical aspect often overlooked is the temporal aspect, specifically the timeliness of translating weather condition measurements into envi- ronmental heat risk assessments (e.g., WBGT). Timely insights on heat risk assessment are crucial for determining robust safety management plans. However, previous works often fail to address this aspect by not clarifying how to quickly inform safety management practitioners of heat risks derived from the measured weather conditions. Such trans- lation should be continuously accessible to practitioners for timely in- sights. These spatial and temporal challenges in understanding heat risk assessments in construction settings make it difficult to apply the established safety guidelines effectively to jobsites. The main objective of this study is to address this information gap by proposing a digital twin-enabled heat risk monitoring framework that overcomes spatial measurement limitations through microclimate simulation and provides actionable, data-driven insights to practitioners in a timely manner. 2.2. Digital twin applications in construction The application of digital twins in the construction industry is still in its early stages, but has been steadily increasing in recent years [ 37 ]. Although not explicitly defined [ 38 ], the concept of a digital twin generally encompasses three key elements: a physical entity in physical space, a virtual counterpart in virtual space, and a connection between the two for data exchange [ 37 ]. In line with digitalization trends in the construction industry [ 39 ], studies have explored digital twins across different phases of construction projects, including design and engi- neering phase [ 40 – 42 ], construction phase [ 43 , 44 ], and operation and maintenance [ 45 – 47 ]. For instance, Jiang et al. [ 48 ] proposed a digital twin framework to monitor unsafe behaviors of tower crane operations. Similarly, Zheng et al. [ 49 ] introduced a digital twin framework for structural health monitoring, particularly to prevent building collapse disasters. Furthermore, Lee et al. [ 50 ] and Liang et al. Digital twin-enabled heat risk monitoring 3.1. Geometric data collection and processing The geometric data is integrated into a virtual space for spatial heat risk analysis. As depicted in Fig. 2 , a series of image processing tech- niques are employed, including UAV (Unmanned Aerial Vehicle) photogrammetry and the Canny edge detection [ 52 ]. UAV photogram- metry, a widely employed remote sensing in construction [ 53 – 58 ], en- ables the efficient collection of site images [ 59 ]. The results derived from UAV photogrammetry include both an orthophoto and geometric models of the site, such as a 3D virtual representation of the construction workplace and a digital surface model (DSM). While an orthophoto delivers a planimetric image map of the construction site [ 60 ], a DSM represents an elevation map of the site [ 69 ], offering a top-down view with spatial relationships of construction resources. As a proof of concept, the spatial resolution of DSM is set to 1 m by 1 m for data processing to integrate with other geometric datasets. Following this, exterior walls are extracted from the DSM, which has been used to identify and outline edges from construction images [ 61 – 63 ]. Finally, surface material information is registered into the orthophoto, which is resized to match the spatial resolution of the DSM. This procedure generates a surface material map, representing surface material types (e. g., asphalt, soil, grass) and detected wall locations. 3.2. On-site weather data synchronization via a weather station The on-site weather data is collected and integrated into a virtual space for continuous heat risk analysis. To this end, a weather station was developed to record and transmit weather conditions through a cellular network, as shown in Fig. 3 (left). This customized weather station enables near-real-time synchronization of weather data on-site. It comprises a pyranometer ( Fig. 3 - ① ), a wind sentry anemometer ( Fig. 3 - ② ), and a temperature and relative humidity sensor ( Fig. 3 - ③ ). Collectively, these sensors allow the system to capture global horizontal irradiance (GHI), air velocity (V a ), air temperature (T a ) and relative humidity (RH). The collected data is stored in two different places to ensure integrity and continuity: an internal storage device, the data logger ( Fig. 3 - ④ ), and a secure digital memory card for external storage. To ensure near-real-time data transmission, the system employs a cellular module ( Fig. 3 - ⑤ ) to relay the collected information to a virtual space. The graphical user interface of the data transmission is shown in Fig. 3 (right), representing the results of near-real-time transmission. The weather station is powered by a rechargeable battery ( Fig. 3 - ⑥ ) with a charging regulator to ensure a consistent power supply ( Fig. 3 - ⑦ ). This weather station is expected to be deployed on-site in an open area where there is no shadow interference during the daytime. 3.3. Functional safety of systems with digital twins In railway transpo rtation, economically-efficient innovative methods of ensuring functional safety are actively developing alongside predictive analytics. That is due to the fact that the key solutions for ensuring functional safety of railway systems consist in the applica tion of multi -channel hardware and multi -version software, which significantly increases the system’s cost and often limits its mass deployment. We must note certain difficulties in terms of upgrading and modifying such systems due to the requirement to redesign their redundant components subject to new needs. Those are due to the systems’ adaptation to another type of rolling stock, variation of track tonnage, change of line class, etc. The current problem consists in designing affordable mass -producible train control and protection systems that comply with the stricter requirements for functional safety. In order to achieve the price/safety balance, a number of methods are used, including virtual information processing channels, digital twins, and other methods and their combinations. By way of example let us point out the experience of operation of a dual -channel train protection system in Russia’s railways. They operate over 30 000 onboard train protection systems (KLUB-U), more than 300 stations with computer -based interlocking systems, more than 1000 km of computer -based automatic block systems. All of those systems follow the dual-channel principle. Over more than 30 years of those systems’ operation not a single case of wrong-side failure has occurred. Meanwhile, the matter of information redundancy is as obvious as that of the hardware redundancy. The best-known examples of the technology’s application include onboard and trackside devices by Bombardier, while, in case of more vital systems, the company uses a combination of dual-channel hardware and and dual-version programming. The implementation of this safety principle causes additional expenditures that grow exponentially along with the number of computer-based interlocking systems. Let us examine the applicability of the new approach to ensuring safe train control using single - channel axle counters that involves comparing current axle counter data with the information embedded in the receiver. The informatio n comparison procedures perform the function of the second axle counter channel thus enabling the creation of a system with dual-version programming rather than one with a dual -channel onboard train control system, but, to an extent, with dual - channel axle counters. Let us imagine that within an onboard system that complies with all functional safety requirements there is certain unique information. Such information, for instance, may represent the number of axles in the train that travels along a line. The safety condition consists in the fact that, ahead of the train, there are k axle-counting stations that, having counted the number of axles that crossed the station, transmit this information to the locomotive for comparison with the information recorded in the vital onboard device. In case the measured number of the train’s axles matches the predefined information with respect to the coordinate of the i-th (i = 1,2,…,k ) axle-counting station, a decision with a given level of safety regarding the vacancy of the line behind the train’s tail is taken. Each i- th axle-counting station out of a series of vacant line sections has its own geographical coordinate and, consecutively, the summation of the distances between such coordinates defines the distance between the head of the next train and the last axle-counting station behind the tail of the preceding train. If the information does not match, then the section occupied by the first train is extended to the next axle-counting station. Hereinafter the common matter of information communication between trains over a digital channel for v ital information communication is not considered, as compliance with the safety level is assumed (coding, encryption etc.). Conclusion A high level of functional safety of railway systems can be achieved even as part of a single-channel architecture as long as such economically efficient innovative methods as digital twins or virtual information processing channels are used. The above methods ensure, on the one hand, a reduction of the amount of equipment and significantly lower cost of the systems and, on the other hand, requires the creation of additional software and substantiation of the acceptability of the achieved level of functional safety. There i s still a concern that the cost of additional activities is comparable to the obtained economic effect of migration towards single -channel architectures. This matter requires further research. The innovative methods of ensuring functional safety are effici ent both at the design stage, and in the course of system operation. Artificial intelligence -based methods might well allow achieving high accuracy and reliability of hazard prediction, thus enabling their anticipation and prevention. Introduction Airports play a vital role in the transportation system, and as the economy develops and the demand for domestic and international movement of people and businesses grows steadily, the actual throughput of existing airports has exceeded the design capacity at that time, and they need to be modernized and expanded [ 1 ]. Airport expansion projects usually include the construction of new terminals, the expansion of existing terminals and the construction of new runways and taxiways. With the increase in the number of large-span steel structure forms and the rapid expansion of the construction scale, large- span steel structure is a popular structural form for the construction of the terminal building. For the large-span steel structure, due to the huge and complex structural construction process, construction methods and construction techniques are complicated, the probability of risk in the construction phase is higher than other structures, while the construc - tion industry is increasingly demanding for safety management intelli - gence. This study is based on the construction process of the overall jacking of the steel roof grid structure of an airport renovation and expansion project, using digital twin technology, activating and passivating the cells through the finite element software Midas/Gen by means of the “life-and-death cell method”, and simulating the construction of the overall jacking phase of the solid grid, using the completed grid as the initial state of the jacking phase, and then simulating the overall jacking, installing sensors on the calculated key force members to collect data for the trial jacking and formal jacking, and then monitoring the construc - tion process based on the actual data. The initial state of the jacking stage is the assembled mesh frame, and then the simulation of overall jacking is carried out. Sensors are installed on the calculated key force members to collect the data of trial jacking and formal jacking to realize the monitoring of the construction process, and then features are extracted based on the actual data, and the prediction models of GA-BP neural network, Random Forest and XGBoost are constructed, and the prediction accuracy and generalization ability of the three networks are compared and analyzed. Comprehensive use of multiple methods can improve the accuracy and reliability of the stress prediction of the steel mesh frame, and also provides a reference for future projects using the overall jacking method for construction. 2. Methodology Fig. 1 illustrates the different parts of the proposed framework. The framework is divided into three main parts. First, the characteristics of structural safety risks are analyzed. The second part uses tools such as finite elements to build a digital twin model identical to the real con - struction project and data collection, and the third part builds a machine learning model to predict the safety risks during the construction of the terminal building. The first part of this flowchart is to conduct a preliminary study to identify the various types of factors that may affect its structural safety risk and to explore the prediction strategy of structural safety risk based on the identified factors. The second part of the flowchart is to collect data from the construction through on-site fieldwork studies and with the help of various types of geometrical models created through BIM technology in the design phase to generate the corresponding finite element physical models and to arrange the sensors to form a digital twin model in the end. These fieldwork research studies aim to prepare a dataset of the actual state of the structural safety risk samples, whose specific characteristics have been characterized in the first part. In the third part, the dataset is utilized to build and train a machine learning model to learn the relationship between the features, and then the ma - chine learning model is evaluated by appropriate performance metrics, and ultimately the structural safety risk is predicted using a superior predictive model. Methods Features and Defects FEA (Finite Element Analysis) FEA is a numerical analysis method widely used in engineering to predict the stress, deformation and vibration of structures. By decomposing the structure into a finite number of cells and approximately resolving the stress and displacement fields within each cell, the behavior of the steel structure is simulated more accurately [ 6 , 9–11 ]. Involves simplifications and assumptions about structures, materials and boundary conditions, which may lead to some deviation of the analysis results from the actual situation, and for complex structures, it requires high performance computers, a large amount of memory and storage space, which leads to a longer time-consuming analysis process NAMs(Numerical and Analytical Methods) NAMs are usually based on theoretical equations and assumptions that simplify the structure and solve for analytical solutions. For example, for simple steel structures, methods such as beam theory or thin-wall structure theory can be used to predict the stress distribution [ 12–15 ]. NAMs may be difficult to apply directly to complex steel structure construction processes with many uncertainties and complex boundary conditions. This paper describes a digital twin approach for checking the clearance of underpass roads in highway widening projects using online map data. Introduction Many existing roads cannot meet the up-to-date traffic demands in modern society since they were designed according to the past traffic conditions [ 20 ]. Road widening is one of the most effective methods to improve the level of service (LOS) of roads [ 42 ]. However, road widening projects can significantly influence the surroundings along the target road, such as building demolition, reconstruction of the crossing roads, pipeline relocation, etc. [ 1 , 4 , 10 ]. In many cases, several low-level roads cross the main road upwards or downwards [ 11 ], and the main road goes over some low-level underpass roads by bridges or culverts. Therefore, sufficient clearance should be reserved for the underpass roads to ensure that vehicles and people can safely go via the un - derpasses [ 48 ]. However, the clearance of an underpass road can significantly constrain road widening projects. The primary reason is the comprehensive effect caused by road widening, the cross fall of the existing main road, and the vertical alignment of the underpass road, as shown in ① , ② , ③ in Fig. 1 . The main road is widened, and the cross fall of the main road can lower the edge of the main road. In addition, the low-level underpass road can fall on one side and rise on the other side, controlled by its vertical alignment. Sometimes, the widened part of the main road is constructed over the rising side of the low-level underpass road, reducing the clearance of the low-level underpass road. Furthermore, the settlement of piers and deflection of girders can reduce the clearance of the low-level underpass road, as shown in ④ , ⑤ in Fig. 1 [ 3 , 9 ]. Thus, it is necessary to check the clearances of low-level underpass roads after widening the main road. Road widening should be conducted based on the status of the existing road. The data relating to low-level underpass roads is also essential to check the clearance. However, the design and construction documents of the existing main road and the low-level underpass road cannot always be available, hindering the road widening process [ 33 ]. Thus, it is necessary to collect data on the existing main road and the low-level underpass road and reconstruct the digital models of the roads to assist the road widening process and the clearance check. In the preliminary design stage of road widening, conducting a field survey and detailed design is time-consuming and costly [ 50 ]. Data such as digital surface model (DSM), digital elevation model (DEM), and aerial photographs can be downloaded from online databases to assist de - signers in proposing preliminary design schemes [ 28 ]. Digital Twin (DT) paradigm is an ideal tool to assist road widening projects. DT is a set of virtual information constructs that fully describes a potential or actual physical manufactured product from the micro atomic level to the macro geometrical level. At its optimum, any infor - mation obtained from inspecting a physically manufactured product can be obtained from its DT [ 21 ]. At the design stage, the DT can provide * Corresponding author. Digital twin for road engineering A road is a long-shape infrastructure, and its digital twin method is quite different from structural entities [ 34 ]. Nevertheless, existing studies have employed DT in road engineering design, operation and maintenance phases, such as urban planning and road maintenance. First, some studies only focus on building the DT for existing roads. Justo, et al. [ 31 ] proposed a semi-automatic method to extract and establish the road element model following the IFC 4.1 standard from point clouds, such as road alignment, traffic signs, guardrails, and some semantics by property sets. Barazzetti, et al. [ 2 ] developed a two-step procedure to detect and classify roads automatically from LiDAR data. Jiang, et al. [ 28 ] proposed a systematic and cost-effective highway DT method based on online map data by digital image processing, hori - zontal alignment fitting, vertical alignment fitting, and cross-section processing. The highway DT considers the primary elements of the highway, such as alignments, central reserves, hard strips, lanes, hard shoulders, verges, and side slopes. Me ˇza, et al. [ 38 ] presented a sys - tematic method to establish a fully functioning DT of a road constructed using secondary raw materials. An integrated data environment syn - thesising a digital model and monitored data is established. Intignano, et al. [ 25 ] proposed a systematic workflow based on the Heritage-BIM (Building Information Modelling) methodology to build a digital twin of an old stone pavement road using point clouds obtained from laser scanners and enrichments its semantics. The workflow realised the reverse engineering of existing linear infrastructure with geometric and non-geometric information, which can assist the infrastructure's con - servation, maintenance, and restoration. Secondly, some studies focus on planning and design, and in these studies, a DT can be built for the existing road and surroundings to assist in urban or rural planning. Jiang, et al. Abstract: The construction industry is one of the most perilous sectors globally, with significantly higher fatality rates compared to other industries. Collision with heavy construction machines is a major cause of worker injuries and fatalities. This research proposes a digital twin -enabled anti-collision system to provide collision warnings and improve construction safety. A five-dimensional digital twin model encompassing the physical layer, digital data layer, virtual entity layer, service layer, and connection layer is established. The physical layer designs multi-sensor fusion approach to respectively collect image data, depth data, and motion data , achieving comprehensive perception of construction sites. The digital data layer designs multi-modal analysis methods to obtain on-site information such as category, position and motion status. The virtual entity layer integrates geometry model, physical model, behavior model, and rule model to achieve the synchronization between physical entities and digital entities. It also divides construction sites into dangerous zones, warning zones, and safe zones to perform collision warnings based on the status of construction robots and workers. The services layer provides supervision, motion states monitoring, zone prediction, and data management for the DT system. The connections layer establishes the information interconnection and synchronization between these layers. Case study is conducted to verify its performance in visualization, motion states monitoring and collision warning. It is proven that the proposed method can achieve a prediction accuracy of over 96% for dangerous zone s and over 95% for warning zone s. The proposed digital twin -enabled anti -collision system can greatly improve the safety for construction sites. Keywords: digital twin; collision warning; smart construction 1. Introduction The construction industry is one of the most dangerous sectors due to its high rates of accidents and fatalities [1]. As statistics from the Hong Kong government in 202 3, the construction industry has the Smart Constr. Article 2 highest accident and fatalities rates among all industries. Its accident rate per 1000 workers is 29.1, double that of the manufacturing industry. Its fatality rate per 1000 workers is 0.162, which is three times as much as the manufacturing industry. Automation and robotics are considered alternatives to alleviate safety concerns in construction [2]. McKinsey estimates that more than 46% of construction machines will be replaced by robots by 2025 [3]. Although construction robots can relieve workers from hazardous tasks, collisions between human-robot interactions (HRIs) are still inevitable. Hence, it is still necessary to design an anti -collision system to improve construction safety [4], as shown in Figure 1. Especially in Hong Kong, construction machines and workers need to operate in narrow spaces, which significantly increases the likelihood of collisions. Figure 1. Human robots interactions in construction sites. The safety monitoring methods for HRIs have been extensively studied in various industries such as manufacturing, construction, and agriculture to improve their efficiency and safety [5,6]. Multi-source sensors [7] such as cameras, Bluetooth devices, Inert ial Measurement Units (IMUs), and Ultra-wideband (UWB) sensors can be used for data collection in HRIs. RGB-D cameras and UWB sensors can be employed for proximity estimation to prevent potential accidents between heavy machines and workers. Machine learni ng methods [8] have also been discussed for the processing, fusion, and prediction of multi -source data. Computer vision methods [9] have been studied for the location, pose estimation, and activity recognition of robots and humans. Intelligent decision -making methods such as space partitioning and bounding volume hierarchy have also been studied for collision detection of crane -lifts in modular integrated construction [10]. Besides, digital systems [11] that combine various sensors have also been designed for proximity estimation, activity recognition, and collision warnings in HRIs. Based on predicted collision events, these systems will take corresponding actions (e.g., giving warnings to humans, stopping robots, or modifying robots’ paths) to ensure safety. Smart Constr. Article 3 Although significant achievements have been made, the safety monitoring methods for HRIs in complicated and dynamic construction sites remain challenging: HRIs with more uncertainties : Compared with the manufacturing industry, HRIs in construction sites are characterized by significant uncertainty [12]. In manufacturing workshops, robots are installed in standard production lines, where they perform operations efficiently with fixed HRI modes. However, in some construction projects (e.g., demolition projects, road projects), robots need to move proactively, adjust their postures dynamically, and carry out operations until the projects are completed. Therefore , HRIs in construction sites do not follow fixed modes and involve more uncertainties. The comprehensive perception of on-site robots and workers is the basis for safety monitoring of HRIs in construction sites. High risks of collision: Collision risks in construction are particularly prominent, and collisions with construction robots—often heavy machines—are major causes of fatalities [13]. For instance, statistics show that in Germany last year, collision -related incidents accounted for 36% of all fatal accidents on construction sites. On a global scale, collision-related accidents make up 22% of all construction accidents, solidifying collisions as a major contributor to on-site incidents. In road construction projects specifically, over 50% of fatal injuries to road workers result from collisions with motor vehicles [14]. Therefore, precise anti-collision methods are considered key to improving the safety of HRIs with high risks. Complex HRIs system: Although robots can greatly improve the level of automation in construction, they also introduce new challenges to HRIs. The occlusion and noise generated by robots can affect workers’ perception of the environment, potentially leading to safety hazards. Additionally, there is information that workers are unable to perceive, such as the planned motion paths of robots and their next actions. As a result, HRI systems are characterized as complex systems that involve the synchronization of heterogeneous data, the integration of analysis algorithms, information communication between different roles, seamless decision-making, and services for various stakeholders. In conclusion, the main objectives of this research are as follows: (1) To develop multi-sensor perception and analysis methods which can realize comprehensive perception of machines, workers, and construction sites. (2) To design on-site collision prediction and warning methods for intelligent decision-making, so as to improve the safety of HRIs in construction sites. (3) To propose an efficient HRIs system which can realize the seamless integration of the “perception, analysis, decision-making, execution”. As a rapidly evolving technology, the digital twin (DT) [15] is widely regarded as one of the most promising technologies for achieving real -time information synchronization in dynamic and complex scenarios. DT refers to a virtual model that provides a com prehensive description of a physical entity, enabling the seamless integration of virtual space with physical space. Multi-source data collected from a physical entity is analyzed and mapped to the virtual entity for simulation, monitoring, and maintenance [16]. DT can also be applied in the safety monitoring system of HRIs, enabling real-time perception and analysis between physical and virtual construction sites, as well as timely decision-making and monitoring. To achieve these objectives, this paper combines artificial intelligence (AI) technologies and digitalization techniques to develop a digital twin -enabled anti-collision system. Firstly, a multi -sensor fusion approach is proposed to realize a comprehensive understanding of entities in physical construction sites. Besides, a multi -modal analysis model is developed to enable real -time and comprehensive monitoring of HRIs in smart construction sites. Moreover, a 5 -Dimensional DT system is designed to Smart Constr. Article 4 achieve the closed-loop optimization of “perception, analysis, decision-making, execution” for HRIs in construction sites. Literature review This section provides a comprehensive review of recent research works, focusing on collision warning methods for HRI, digital twin in smart construction. 2.1. Collision warning methods for HRI Collision early warning in HRI primarily comprises three interconnected components: perception, analysis, and decision-making, each playing a critical role in ensuring operational safety. Perception serves as the foundational step, involving the collection of environment and object-related information through diverse sensing technologies. Ultra -Wideband (UWB) and Global Positioning System (GPS) [17] are commonly deployed to obtain real-time location data of objects. Cameras capture visual image data, while thermal sensors acquire temperatur e-enhanced images to distinguish humans from inanimate objects [18]. Additionally, RGB -D cameras and LiDARs provide 3D point cloud data, enabling detailed spatial reconstruction [19]. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are mounted on objects to collect moti on parameters such as velocity, acceleration, and angular rates [20]. Wearable devices have also been extensively studied for collecting both physical and mental data of workers [21]. Analysis focuses on interpreting the collected data to comprehensively understand the motion states of interacting entities. This step relies on advanced algorithms, including deep learning and computer vision techniques [22], to extract meaningful features. For instance, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are designed to classify, detect, and segment workers and robots from images or videos [23]. Temporal features are often integrated into Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to analyze motion trajectories and patterns, facilitating activity recognition and continuous trajectory monitoring [24]. Moreover, 3D CNN-based methods can detect proximity between robots and workers, a key indicator for triggering early collision warnings [25]. Decision-making involves risk identification and the formulation of safety actions based on analyzed data and predefined rules. Core tasks include collision warning activation, path planning, and trajectory adjustment. For example, an integrated octree and bounding box strategy has been applied to collision detection in crane-lift path planning, enabling efficient spatial conflict checks [26]. In dynamic construction sites, occupied areas of workers and hazardous objects can be mapped onto a lane map, with collision warnings triggered by monitoring proximity thresholds. Collision warning rules have also been studied as varied scenario and application requirements , including static distance (SD) rules, time-to-collision (TTC) rules, spatial occupancy (SO) rules. The SD rules are a common approach in industrial settings where a fixed safety threshold (e.g., 2 meters) is defined . Warnings are given when the distance between a robot and a worker falls below this threshold [2 7]. The TTC rules are used in Smart Constr. Article 5 high-speed environments (e.g., autonomous forklifts). TTC calculates the time until a potential collision based on relative velocity and distance, with warnings adjusted dynamically (e.g., an alert is triggered when TTC < 3 seconds) [28]. The SO rules are also used in confined spaces, such as construction tunnels. Here, warnings are activated when a worker enters a predefined “dangerous zone” around a robot regardless of distance, so as to account for limited working space [29]. Furthermore, uncertainty factors (e.g., sensor noise, sudden human movements) are increasingly incorporated into decision -making models to enhance accuracy [30]. Knowledge graph methods have also been widely adopted for risk identification and safety management, enabling structured reasoning about complex interaction scenarios [31]. This multi-layered framework highlights the importance of aligning perception accuracy, analytical depth, and context-specific warning rules to address the dynamic and uncertain nature of HRI environments. 2.2. Digital twin in smart construction With the rapid development of information technology, digital systems [32,33] such as cyber-physical system (CPS), edge-cloud system, and DT system have been widely applied in the construction industry. Cyber-physical synchronization between the ongoing construction progress and building information models (BIM) can be realized via the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, and 5G technology, allowing stakeholders to share on-site information and make more efficient decisions [34]. Besides, multi-dimensional information can also be integrated with BIM platforms for various tasks such as scheduling, planning, cost estimation, and daily storage. This provides more comprehensive and extensive information synchronization and integration for construction projects [3 5]. Liu et al . [ 36] developed a CPS for real-time monitoring and visualization of greenhouse gas emissions in prefabricated construction. The operating states of cranes, transfer vehicles, and construction machines are monitored using acceleration sensors, GPS, and Wi -Fi to calculate greenhouse gas emissions. Machine model numbers, quantities, power, and greenhouse gas emission factors are stored in the cyber database. Furthermore, workers can also be incorporated to design human -cyber-physical systems (HCPS) [3 7], aiming to improve workers safety and well-being. DT systems [38] are digital representations of physical systems that possess the same properties and synchronized information as real -world entities . DT systems can be used for full lifecycle operations, simulations, and management, ultimately enabling better decision making. The applications of DT in construc tion have also been widely studied. Five-dimensional digital twin models are utilized for closed-loop control of the “hanging-adjusting angles-fine positioning-piling” process to avoid collisions [39]. The biggest advantage of DT is its ability to integrate with various technologies including AI, cloud computing , machine l ... en instantiated as virtual entities with N-dimensional information in Unity 3D, thereby enabling synchronization between the physical and virtual spaces. This research collected a total of 30 videos with a total duration of approximately 20 minutes. These videos cover over 417 behaviors performed by excavators and trucks, including typical operating behaviors such as swinging, digging, moving, and lifting. All video frames are annotated with 2D bounding boxes for excavators, trucks, and workers to define their spatial positions in the RGB-D image. For excavators, five key points are further annotated to capture their motion, namely Body_End (rear end of the body), Cab_Boom (joint between cab and boom), Boom_Arm (joint between boom and arm), Arm_Bucket (joint between arm and bucket), and Bucket_End (tip of the bucket). Besides, the IMUs data collected from these five keypoints are used to capture the motion trend of excavators. Based on these annotations of boxes and motion data the behavior process, the operating zones of the excavator are labeled as 241 dangerous samples, 834 warning samples, and 593 safe samples. Conclusion and future work In this research, a digital twin -enabled anti -collision system is designed to improve the safety for human-robot interactions in smart construction sites . This research has the following scientific contributions. (1) Multi-sensor fusion approach and multi-modal analysis model are developed to realize a comprehensive understanding of physical entities construction sites. (2) Geometry models, physical models, rule models and behavior models are designed to realize precise synchronization and collision warning in virtual space. (3) A 5D digital-twin enabled anti-collision system is proposed to achieve the closed-loop optimization of “perception, analysis, decision -making, execution” for HRIs in smart construction sites. Although great progress has been achieved, there are still some limitations to be addressed in the future. When dangerous or warning zones are misjudged as safe ones, the system lacks a dynamic response mechanism (such as secondary verification, hierarchical emergency strategy) which may lead to hidden safety hazards being covered up. In addition, the “black box” nature of deep learning models will increase the uncontrollability of misjudgments, and the limitations of sensor s may also affect the recognition accuracy. To solve the above challenges, future work will focus on the following directions: (1) Improve the misjudgment response mechanism: Design a dynamic verification module. When the system judges a zone as safe, it will automatically trace back the sensor data and model reasoning process. If abnormal features are found, secondary monitoring will be triggered. At the same time, establish a multi -level response strategy, dynamically adjust the warning levels according to the misjudgment probability, and push manual review requests when necessary. (2) Expand sensors and improve model interpretability: Introduce infrared thermal sensors, ultra-wideband sensors, and lidar to enhance the accuracy of entity recognition . Besides, attention mechanisms, model distillation and other technologies can also be studied to parse the decision logic of deep learning networks and reduce the “black box” risk. (3) Strengthen system reliability: In response to the high safety requirements of the construction industry, optimize the real-time performance and robustness of the DT system, improve the anti-interference ability in complex scenarios through edge computing and cloud collaboration, and ensure the consistency of virtual-real mapping and the accuracy of early warning.
