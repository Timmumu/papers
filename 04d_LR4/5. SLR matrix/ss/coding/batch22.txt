T: Digital Twins for Construction Projects—Developing a Risk Systematization Approach to Facilitate Anomaly Detection in Smart Buildings
A: This study aims to analyze and discuss the risks facing construction projects by reviewing some of the processes and procedures that address risks through the use of the digital twin technology. The paper studies generic risks and their treatment, and it develops a proposal for risk management systematization using the Digital Twin for Construction Projects approach, previously developed by the authors. It addresses how to classify risks so that the digital system is fed with the proper information and data, which is based on processing and analysis, to reach understandable decisions and overcome anomalies. The research reached a set of results, the most prominent of which is that the digital twin can be used to enhance risk management in construction projects through adapted techniques such as the ones proposed in the paper; namely, a risk treatment procedure and a custom risk matrix. The paper studies generic risks and their treatment, and it develops a proposal for risk management systematization using the Digital Twin for Construction Projects approach, previously developed by the authors. It addresses how to classify risks so that the digital system is fed with the proper information and data, which is based on processing and analysis, to reach understandable decisions and overcome anomalies. The research reached a set of results, the most prominent of which is that the digital twin can be used to enhance risk management in construction projects through adapted techniques such as the ones proposed in the paper; namely, a risk treatment procedure and a custom risk matrix. In addition, risk management treated according to a digital approach helps to improve the prediction capabilities, and this helps human decision-makers to avoid potential unplanned costs and failures, and to maximize efﬁciency. The study also recommends new investigations in the ﬁeld of safeguarding shared information and data to protect from intentional and accidental mismanagement in order to reach a comprehensive digital system. Keywords: digital twins for construction projects; engineering project management; construction management; risk management 1. Introduction Construction projects are considered complex endeavors due to the volume of opera- tions and activities that are involved, and they are also one of the types of projects most exposed to risks of all natures. When approaching this topic in the construction industry, one must be aware of the ramiﬁcations of implementing such an initiative that involves a considerable number of diverse stakeholders. For this reason, it is necessary to develop clear approaches to construction risks and analyze them in detail to identify the most prominent sources that cause anomalies and to ﬁnd the appropriate solutions to assist management. Risk management is one of the most important tools that decision makers can employ to avoid exposure to failure in construction projects [ 1]. It aims to increase the probability of positive effects and reduce the probability of events that negatively affect the project activities [1]. When conducting activities under this paradigm, technical, operational and ﬁnancial aspects must be considered simultaneously in order to deliver effective results and not unnecessarily burden the project team. In this paper, we will deﬁne and analyze a taxonomy approach through which im- proved success can be achieved to avoid, diminish or completely remove risks within construction projects with the help of digital solutions. This goal is intended to address the current gap between the level of sophistication encountered in the managerial approach to risk, as it can be encountered in manufacturing or software development, and the ca- pabilities of modern cyber-physical systems that can be employed on construction sites. Telecom 2023, 4, 135–145. https://doi.org/10.3390/telecom4010009 https://www.mdpi.com/journal/telecom Telecom 2023, 4 136 The main contribution proposed is based on a speciﬁc understanding of the conceptual approaches presented in the literature, including a self-developed model, and the tailoring of the risk management procedure to the needs of construction projects forecasted to be implemented with the help of digital twins. It should be noted that the construction projects are surrounded by risks due to the scope and the complexity of the activities they include and, therefore, it is expected that risks will occur during implementation related to such aspects as: costs, time, human resource management, procurement and commissioning, contracts between project parties, occupational safety, negligence, etc. This negatively affects the achievement of the project objectives, so the risks must be accurately and timely evaluated to avoid project anomalies such as shutdown, failure or cost and time overruns. The current paper analyzes the existing studies and scientiﬁc articles that discuss the applications of digital twins in managing construction projects and mitigating potential risks in order to achieve a comprehensive vision that guides the employment of digital twins for risk management in construction projects. Our previously published vision of digital twins for construction projects (DTCP) [2] is used as a thinking pattern upon which additional tools are integrated and calibrated for use. By linking the results of the previous studies and scientiﬁc articles and extending our approach, we sought to obtain a digital taxonomy through which construction projects can be managed better and operations management can be organized in a digital way that natively exhibits less risks and keeps one step ahead the ones that remain. The process of identifying risks in construction projects can be considered one of the most difﬁcult for the project managers due to the fact that the resulting delivery pressures usually supersede the need for risk management, although, in a paradoxical way, it is the very unmanaged risk that creates anomalous delays and cost issues. Various tools must be available for those involved to confront the potential risks and manage them in a way that ensures avoiding their occurrence/re-occurrence, such as providing the correct information about potential risks, making decisions on time and implementing them, and using alternative plans that ensure the achievement of project objectives within the prepared operational plan [ 3]. Additionally, the risk identiﬁcation phase should be the trigger of external risk alleviation measures that the organization can employ, such as seeking better insurance, establishing cooperation networks or creating public-private partnerships. In each direction, it is critically important to follow up on the results of the risk identiﬁcation stage during the implementation stage and to correct the solutions according to the concrete circumstances. When addressing these issues within digitalized construction projects, the DTCP approach mentioned above can be speciﬁcally focused on: • Unexpected or changing environmental conditions; • An imbalance in the workforce and the result of the employees; • Accidents for human resources that cause bodily injury; • Technical anomalies such as design, workmanship and technical issues; • The occurrence of ﬁre, earthquakes or approaching dangerous places; • Cybersecurity and privacy issues related to the employment of digital technologies; • Interfacing problems generated by integrating different solutions; • Insufﬁcient or conﬂicting solutions from the DTCP database; • Intentional damage, theft or other crime inﬂicted by third parties. While considering the issues above, a project management team should seek to cus- tomize the analysis to the actual situation at hand and to generate proportional solutions in terms of costs, operational efforts and improvement potential. 2. Methodology The current study’s methodology relied on the analysis and review of previous re- search, with the aim of linking the previous results and matching them to the existing technologies related to digital twins in order to reach an integrated system for construction and, in larger sense, industrial project management. The effort employed aims to combine Telecom 2023, 4 137 simulation and the most common components of digital twins to provide a logical and technical framework that helps decision makers in their work related to the operations needed to manage the successive activities in the construction project stages. In order to achieve this, the authors have performed a document analysis using the READ method, also used in other ﬁelds [4], that involves readying the materials, extracting the data, analyzing the data, and distilling the conclusions. This was complemented by the experience of the authors in managing technical projects in two different project management cultures, in the Middle East and Europe. The documents collected and readied for processing were selected through the follow- ing criteria and work stages: • The use of mostly recent studies and articles that were published during the past ﬁve years, 2017–2022, in order to keep pace with recent developments and generate a correct image about risk management and the role of digital twins in it; • The selection was performed using the databases of ScienceDirect and Google Scholar, which cover a large variety of the scientiﬁc publications in this domain; • In the searches, the study used keywords derived from our initial concept DTCP , namely “digital twins for construction projects”, in various combinations. A total of 55 of studies, articles and scientiﬁc papers in the same ﬁeld were obtained, and they were used to extract the relevant information to reach a comprehensive perception about risks and their management in construction projects, and the possibilities of em- ploying the digital twins. Subsequently, 32 works were selected and used in the thorough analysis and discussion process based on the content that aligns with the current study, informing the inferential discovery process, which was structured based on the authors’ experiences in the ﬁeld of project management. The ﬁnal step of the methodology performed the distillation of the know-how into new contributions that can support the development of DTCPs: • The brainstorming of possible conﬁgurations of the technical systems needed to assist the project managers to conduct the risk management process; • The aggregation of information into generic solutions that reﬂect the sum of previous experiences encountered in construction projects by the authors; • The discussion of possible use cases in which the proposed approach can be imple- mented successfully to deliver a perceivable impact. 3. Analysis of Digital Systems in Construction Management The AEC (Architecture, Engineering and Construction) industry exhibits large scale complexity and is subject to a diversity of risks as it contains many interdependent activ- ities. Thus, the use of smart technologies to mitigate and reduce risks and help manage operations and activities is both welcomed and necessary [5]. By employing virtual envi- ronment operations in all phases of construction project implementation (such as building information modeling, virtual/augmented reality assistance, advanced analytics based on sensor data, etc.), project management relies on large amounts of data to improve the performance and decision-making processes [6]. It should be noted that the employment of digital twins in the management of the construction industry increases productivity, saves time in addition to reducing costs, and contributes to enhanced risk and process manage- ment, which in turn improves the quality and safety [7]. Projects that depend on modern technology are becoming more energy efﬁcient and rely on sustainable innovation through the use of technologies such as robots, artiﬁcial intelligence, nanotechnology, etc. [8]. The digital transformation in the construction industry reveals new principles and guidelines for exploiting previously untapped opportunities to improve the performance and quality and reduce the potential risks. In addition, these technologies, speciﬁc to the Industry 4.0 and Industry 5.0 approaches, also create niches for the use of big data within an Internet of Things approach that leads to advanced Building Information Modeling Telecom 2023, 4 138 (BIM) [9]. Conclusions With the continuous increase in the development of the economy and the employment of digitalization, studies have shown that the world today is moving towards the use of modern technologies in most areas. This is expected to include all aspects of industry, business, healthcare and other domains, with a desire to improve product performance, expedite services and generate quality of life. The digital twin has become an important component in all ﬁelds, including the AEC industries, and it should be noted that the digital twin has many advantages in reducing risks, as has been proven in other ﬁelds, such as medical interventions. From an economic and technical perspective, it is able to analyze and detect risks and provide an ideal model in taxonomic systematization and help decision makers take actions to avoid problems and unwanted consequences. The current study focused on a guided review of the literature, through which an in- depth insight was gained about the risks facing construction projects and the connection of the concept of digital twin to risk management in construction. In this way, it helps close the identiﬁed research gap, providing specialists with a structured way of developing digital twins for construction projects (DTCP) that can beneﬁt research and practical initiatives. Comprehensive solutions that include managing all risks faced by construction projects using digital twins, as well as detailed studies and research, are still in the process of development, seeking to link common frameworks within a functional approach. This paper presented a straightforward conceptual model to achieve this goal, which could contribute to enhancing the access of more companies to this type of development. The analysis mode proposed within the article brings more clariﬁcation to the follow- up activities and what should happen when a fully deployed virtual copy assists the project implementation. In the event of a risk, the system sends an alert and then identiﬁes and investigates the risks to be addressed. It is worth noting that the model can provide valuable assistance within the compliance and maintenance process of occupational health and safety issues and provides contributions through which the impacts and costs can be reduced, and the quality can be improved. In addition, it helps researchers in developing a more robust digital system based on a structured taxonomy. Through this model, the authors propose a way in which the project activities could be further automated by the deployment of machine learning algorithms and artiﬁcial intelligence solutions, which are a natural extension when implementing digital twins in other areas, such as smart manufacturing. Taking into account the particularities of the proposed concept, the authors also put forward a dedicated evaluation procedure in the form of a simpliﬁed ﬂowchart and a recommendation for a risk matrix, with the assessment levels reduced by the use of the digital twins. Telecom 2023, 4 144 Finally, after conducting the study, it was determined that there is a need to allocate a set of resources related to electronic data protection and enhanced information security as a new type of safeguard that is speciﬁc to digital systems and virtual worlds. Moreover, this should be integrated from the start with the operational functions of the digital twins to minimize possible losses. Future research should further analyze the need to overcome various human machine interface issues, as well as the psychological impact of job au- tomation and regulatory compliance, especially in the case of tasks that require mandatory human intervention. The main limitations of the research are related to the narrow domain of studies that can be aligned with the goals of the DTCP concept through analysis and the use of individualized experiences for scrutiny, both of which will be addressed in future contributions. Introduction Ensuring the safety of buildings is a critical component of urban disaster prevention. In addition to external factors, such as natural di - sasters, the safety of buildings can also be affected by internal factors such as material performance degradation and component creeps [39] . However, structural health is dynamic, and when the health status of a building varies greatly from the design expectations, accidents may result. For example, the Jiaxin Hotel in Quanzhou, China unexpectedly collapsed in March 2020, causing 29 deaths and 42 injuries during its reconstruction 1 . In another incident, 53 people died when an old building in Changsha City was suddenly damaged and collapsed in April 2022. 2 To prevent further loss of life owing to lapses in building safety, ongoing monitoring is required. Thus, structural health monitoring (SHM) technology has been widely used to collect and analyze data to diagnose structural health status using non-destructive sensors arranged inside the structure [22] . Early studies and applications of SHM in civil engineering focused on projects related to water conservation, such as bridges and dams [18] . However, with the increase in the construction of large or structurally complex buildings, SHM technology has been gradually introduced into the field of building engineering. Multiple aspects of SHM technology have been investigated, such as high-performance sensors, data collec - tion, automatic monitoring systems, and methods of identifying struc - tural damage [4,15,36] . However, accurately evaluating and predicting a building ’ s health state based on SHM data is challenging owing to the diversity in the structural forms of building engineering, the complexity of the internal structural components (e.g., beams, plates, and columns), and the sub - stantial variation in the states of these two factors [31] . To address this issue, numerous studies have focused on improving the methods of SHM data analysis. For example, to prevent the impact of anomalous moni - toring data, Zhang et al. [38] described a simple and efficient approach that incorporates complementary information obtained from multi-view local binary patterns and the random forest algorithm to distinguish data anomalies. Related data-processing methods, including Bayesian inference method, the Kalman filter algorithm, fuzzy logic, and various machine learning ap - proaches, have also been explored [24,13,21]. In addition to the data-processing methods, data models are an important component of addressing the complexity of building safety. For example, the structural mechanics model supports the analysis of structural health using the finite-element method. In this study, a new building SHM information model based on digital twin technology was proposed to extend and complement the structural mechanics model. The objectives of this study were to: (1) manage the monitoring sensors located on the internal components of a building and their monitoring data in space and (2) analyze the external influences on the building. Subsequently, the association of the monitoring data of different struc - tural components as a whole in real time helps restore the dynamic health state of the building. Furthermore, the external environment is another crucial factor that can cause damage to buildings. Thus, the second objective of our study was to analyze the external influences on the building. The model offers a foundation for monitoring data man - agement, analysis, and the dynamic representation of the structural health state. Digital twin technology refers to the process of mapping an object in the real world to create a highly accurate digital representation and reflecting the information collected in the real world onto the digital model to make predictions based on the simulation, analysis, and visu - alization of the natural world [27]. A digital twin maps the attributes, structures, states, performances, and functions of physical entities and systems within a virtual world [6]. Grigor et al. [14] addressed the methodological development of structural simulation, analysis, and control models for historic buildings using the concept of digital twin technology and demonstrated that developing a digital twin model for a historical building allows for more extensive usage of SHM data. Addi - tionally, these models facilitate a greater understanding of the real-time structural behavior of complex buildings to ensure general structural safety with minimal technical interventions. Jiang et al. [17] introduced the concept of a digital twin within the field of bridge engineering and established a general, non-deterministic, digital twin-driven multiscale fatigue life prediction framework for steel bridges, which enhanced the understanding of the fatigue evolution mechanism throughout the bridge lifecycle. The direct exploration of establishing a digital twin model for building an SHM system is limited and requires further investigation. However, studies on the potential applications of the digital twin concept in evaluating smart cities may provide a point of reference. Batty [3] analyzed the construction methods of digital twin cities, including that of high- and low-frequency cities. Similarly, White et al. [30] analyzed the modeling and construction of a digital twin smart city and found that it is possible to create an accurate digital twin by combining a large BIM with the big data generated by the Internet of Things (IoT) sensors. Further, Jaume et al. [16] conceptualized the use of digital twins in urban management and defined the basic structure of a city’s digital twin. Additionally, Du [12] reported that creating digital twin buildings may involve constructing physical information systems that use buildings as carriers and utilize BIMs, geographic information systems (GIS), the IoT, artificial intelligence, big data, and other tech - nologies. In comparison, Zhu et al. [40] introduced digital twin tech - nology in the construction of the Sichuan–Tibet railway and explored the methods for classification and coding of physical elements. As shown above, realizing a digital twin model of building health monitoring is important by combining a BIM and a real-scene 3D model. In particular, BIM technology can provide the distribution details of a building’s internal components (such as beams, plates, and columns) in a 3D form and integrate information from construction to operation to provide a foundation for collecting and managing a building’s health data from monitoring sensors. To this end, BIM technology has been introduced into the process of monitoring building health, including the visualization of monitoring information, comprehensive management of structure monitoring information, and establishment of a structural safety early warning system [25,23,19,29]. For example, Boddupalli et al. [5] adopted a BIM as a health monitoring computing and digital platform to organize and visualize health monitoring data and verify the advantages of BIM technology in these capacities. Further, Yang [34] and Deng et al. [11] explored the application of BIMs in SHM visuali - zation, while Theiler and Smarsly [28] proposed a BIM-based health monitoring information description model by extending the industry foundation class (IFC) model. This model uses IFC as a formal basis to describe the information of the SHM system and promote the develop - ment of the interoperability between the SHM systems. Although BIMs provide the internal location information of a struc - ture for monitoring sensors, which is conducive to analyzing the re - lationships among different structural components, health monitoring systems should also focus on the impact of a structure’s external envi - ronment. For example, the impact of surrounding disturbances and seismic waves should be monitored. Therefore, research on the SHM of a building based on its spatial location is required. One such tool that may be utilized for this purpose is GIS, which is essential for urban information analysis [33], emphasizes the geographical and spatial characteristics of objects, and provides accu - rate positioning and spatial analysis of buildings. An important devel - opment in the current GIS field is the real-scene 3D model, which results from the oblique photogrammetry method, and possesses the charac - teristics of high accuracy, fidelity, and measurability and has become an important technology in the construction and management of smart cities [10,35]. Recently, the Chinese government issued a notice to promote the construction of a 3D realistic geospatial scene in China, aiming to develop new 3D geospatial information products to describe and represent the real 3D spaces of industrial production, living envi - ronments, and ecological development [8]. However, the real-scene 3D model represents the “skin,” or exterior, of the building without the interior component information, which inhibits its capacity to meet sophisticated needs such as indoor navigation, fire evacuation, and building construction. In summary, it is necessary to merge the complementary real-scene 3D model and BIM to establish an accurate digital twin model of buildings for SHM. Chen et al. [9] explored feasible approaches for converting IFC models into 3D Tiles based on an open-source solution for visualizing BIM in Cesium. Following the open-source solution, we aim to merge a BIM and a real-scene 3D model to create a digital twin model for building SHM. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2—the conceptual model of the digital twin, Section 3—the proposed methodology for merging a BIM and a real-scene 3D model, Section 4—the application of the methodology to the Nanjing Museum, and Section 5—conclusions. 2. Conceptual model of the digital twin 2.1. Analysis of model requirements To monitor and manage a buildings’ health information in real-time, its digital twin model needs to meet three requirements, as shown in Fig. 1: (1) The ability to manage structural information such as the building’s size and geometric parameters, as well as semantic informa - tion, such as the materials used for beams, plates, and columns and their prefabricated assembly. The structural information of buildings can be used for dynamic and static mechanical analysis of structures under the influence of the environment. (2) The ability to describe the external shape of the building in terms of its location, contour, and geometric shape. This information can be used to analyze the relationship between the building and its surroundings, such as the impact of foundation pit excavation on surrounding buildings during the construction period or the impact of surrounding disturbances on the building. (3) The ability J. Xu et al. Measurement 217 (2023) 112955 3 to manage the build-state monitoring information. In addition, the model should support managing dynamic monitoring information, such as stress, displacement, and vibration, from various sensors. These sensors collect real-time information on the external representation of the building structure and internal changes in the structure. Building structure information, external shape information, and sensor data can be considered the geometric and attribute information of a GIS, and existing GIS data models can be referenced. The BIM is convenient for managing building component information and is widely used in the construction industry. However, this model can also repre - sent and manage building information during the planning, design, construction, and maintenance periods. Therefore, we selected the BIM to represent and manage the building structure information in this study. The real-scene 3D model provides accurate geographic location information, realistic effects, and accurate measurements. Therefore, we selected a real-scene 3D model to describe the geographic information of the building. Combining a real-scene 3D model with a BIM can meet the first two requirements outlined above and provide a foundation for locating sensors and analyzing the surrounding environment. For the third requirement, the building SHM data are essentially structured data that can be managed using a database. The monitoring data were then easily associated with the geometric data of the fused model as attribute data for the GIS model. Therefore, the fusion of a BIM and a real-scene 3D model was the key to building a digital twin model in this study. 2.2. Selection of data standards for the model fusion A basic model for converting a BIM into a real-scene 3D model is required to support fusion and combine BIMs and real-scene 3D models. For the GIS model, we selected a real-scene 3D model as the target/ foundation model for fusion because it simultaneously manages the geometry and dynamic monitoring data of a building. Thus, the data must first be transferred from the BIM to a real-scene 3D model to merge the two models. In recent years, researchers and engineers have developed many data standards for BIMs and real-scene 3D models. Based on the tie-point pairs of the two models, we used the rigid-body transformation method to determine the trans - formation relationship between the two coordinate systems. The trans - formation matrix is decomposed into several basic operations, namely, rotation and translation, using the SVD method. This makes the complex transformation easy to perform and realize. Considering the Old Hall of the Nanjing Museum as a case study, a fusion model was developed. The merging process between the BIM and real-scene 3D model can be automatically accomplished using a pro - gram, excluding the tie-point selection. This was performed manually in this study. The automatic transformation process consumes approxi - mately 25 min on a standard laptop (CPU is an Intel Core i7-10510U at 2.30 GHz). In this case, the programs for each merging step are separate. In the future, we can unify all the programs and select the tie points as input conditions to automate and accelerate the entire merging process. The fused model results show that the buildings ’ geometric graphics and semantic information are preserved after fusion. Furthermore, the spatial positions of the two models are consistent with the external and internal scenarios of the building display. Although we have achieved the integration of a BIM and a real-scene 3D model in terms of geometry, semantics, spatialization, and expres - sion standards, a few unresolved issues remain. To effectively speed up the display of a real-scene 3D model, the level of detail method is typically used to establish different detail levels of the model. However, the level of detail method of the BIM is not established in the current fusion process, which decreases its loading speed. Furthermore, the BIM of a building may include numerous detailed components and large volumes of data, which may not be required to complete the process. Thus, simplifying and reducing the BIM is a problem worthy of further exploration. In addition, effectively analyzing the SHM data collected by all the arranged sensors is a challenge. The digital twin model can connect monitoring data from a spatial angle that offers a synthetic perspective to analyze the data, which needs further exploration. 5. Conclusion Building safety is an important aspect of urban safety and disaster reduction. We propose a digital-twin-oriented building health moni - toring information model. The model fuses a BIM with a real-scene 3D model, achieves the integrated management of a building ’ s internal components and external environment, and combines a building ’ s spatial shape and state. The greatest challenge in establishing a digital model is the fusion of geometric, semantic, and spatial location infor - mation between the BIM and real-scene 3D model. This work was completed with the support of multiple open-source standards and software, such as IFC, 3D Tiles, OBJ, and BIM server, which make the fusion method easier to use. The fusion process applied a registration method based on rigid-body transformation and a BIM semantic trans - formation method based on JSON. The fused model integrates the monitoring data by providing a spatial description of the monitoring sensors. The monitoring data of the sensors distributed in the local part of the building can be connected in space, thus providing a foundation and support for analyzing the health status of the building from the perspective of the entire building. Thus, the response status of the building structure under the influence of the external environment can be dynamically represented, such as through the stress, strain, and vibration of the components. Considering Nanjing Museum ’ s Old Hall as an example, we established a digital twin model and developed a building safety monitoring system based on this model, which preliminary verified the accuracy of our digital twin model. However, dynamically analyzing the overall health status of buildings according to monitoring data obtained locally requires further investi - gation. The proposed digital twin model can provide an overall perspective and an integrated data management platform for different monitoring data from sensors arranged in a building. Future studies should focus on examining the dynamic inversion and real-time pre - sentation methods for the overall health status of buildings based on the digital twin model. The RV reducer is widely used as the construction robot joint because of its high transmission efficiency, high torque, and smooth transmission performance (Chen, 2018). At present, there have been a multitude of scholars pouring attention into transmission efficiency (Wang, 2021), optimization of design (Yang et al., 2021) and error analysis of RV reducers (Li et al., 2018), but little into the fault diagnosis aspect. The existing fault diagnosis of RV reducers is mostly based on deep learning (Chen et al., 2021; Long et al., 2021; Peng & Wang, 2019). Traditional fault characteristics are extracted from vibration signals (Shi & Shang, 2022), sound signals (Bai et al., 2022) or sound ‐vibration signals (Shi et al., 2022)t o achieve accurate fault classification. Considering the complex construction environment and working conditions of construction robots, it does not seem feasible to collect sound signals with the interference of a strong noise background. Therefore, deep learning based on vibration signals has become the mainstream method for fault diagnosis of robots. A sparse deep learning method was proposed by (Sun et al., 2018) to reduce the risk of overfitting in deep networks with a large number of layers and neurons. A deep transfer learning ‐based fault diagnosis framework was proposed by Shao et al. ( 2019a) to obtain faster model convergence and higher fault diagnosis accuracy. A one ‐dimensional residual convolutional autoencoder‐based feature learning was proposed by Yu and Zhou (2020) to better extract the features of vibration signals under noisy conditions. The above deep learning ‐based fault diagnosis methods all require sufficient measured data to train the neural network. But the measured data on faulty robot joints are difficult to obtain under the actual working conditions. The reasons may be as follows: (1) most of the construction robots are in normal working condition; (2) obtaining failure data through experiments is a time ‐consuming and costly process; (3) some fault data cannot even be obtained through experiments. To provide different kinds of fault data for the RV reducer, some scholars have established dynamics models (Qiao et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2019). However, there are such varying degrees of idealization in the dynamics modeling that the dynamics model cannot completely describe the motion behavior of the internal components of RV reducers. This will cause there to be a gap between the simulated signals of the dynamics model and the measured signals of the RV reducer. Therefore, the key to improving the accuracy of the fault diagnosis of the RV reducer is how to reduce the gaps between the simulated dynamic response and the measured dynamic response. To establish a relationship between simulated dynamic response and measured dynamic response, digital twin provides a feasible method. The main contributions are as follows. 1. There are a multitude of problems in traditional manual building construction technology, which have become the shortcomings of the new intelligent building construction technology. The devel- opment of construction robots with a high automation level has become an inevitable trend in the new era. To ensure the normal operation and accurate work of construction robots, a digital twin‐assisted fault diagnosis system is proposed in this work for the robot joint (RV reducer). The system can effectively diagnose faults in robot joints with insufficient data. 2. A CycleGAN ‐based digital twin model is proposed to generate different types of simulated data for fault diagnosis. Based on the features of CycleGAN, the model can map the X ‐domain data to the Y ‐domain data. The noiseless and faultless vibration signals generated by gear dynamics are the X ‐domain data of this digital twin model, and the measured data of various fault types are the Y‐domain data. The digital twin model requires only a small number of measured data as the training samples to generate the simulated data, which has the same distribution and fault characteristics as the measured data. 3. To verify the feasibility of the digital twin ‐assisted fault diagnosis system, we establish a test ‐rig simulating robot joint conditions and propose a CNN ‐ResNet classifier. First, we train the CNN ‐ ResNet model by utilizing different amounts of measured data. The results show that the fault diagnosis accuracy and conver- gence speed of the model decrease as the training sample decreases. Then, only 400 samples of measured data are used to generate the simulated vibration signals, which can be directly used for fault diagnosis. The results show that the simulated vibration signals can provide 98.86% accuracy, which can demonstrate that the proposed method can solve the problem of insufficient training data. Finally, the proposed method is compared with other mainstream methods. The results demon- strate that the proposed method can obtain higher fault diagnosis accuracy with fewer measured data. Introduction A general definition and methodology is presented as a framework for modelling historic struc- tures and estimating their preservation state. More details about this issue can be found in spe- cialised works (see for example Croci’ s book [1] for a general review, or Amer et al. [2] for a com- plete case study). Safety assessment of historic constructions consists in studying their structural stability in order to determine their preservation state and collapsing risks. More specifically, one may define three objectives: (i) to determine the present damage state of the studied structure; (ii) to understand the possible causes which have lead to the observed damage state; and finally (iii) to estimate its potential evolution. Various means are available to reach these objectives; they are summarised below in two categories: data collection methods to investigate the past and present states of a structure, and physical-mathematical modelling methods to bridge the present state with its possible causes, as well as to predict potential evolutions. Data collection methods allow to retrieve both qualitative and quantitative informations re- garding the present state of a structure, as well as its history. A first source of information lies in available documentation regarding the studied structure, such as architectural drawings or his- torical records mentioning events involving the structure. More generally, studying such docu- mentation allows to retrieve already-processed data regarding the structure and its environment. Field surveys are another way to collect data on a given structure. Contrarily to documentary studies, field surveys lead to generating new data on the present state of the structure or its envi- ronment. Laboratory tests are also a way to generating new data on the present state of the stud- ied structure; however, they are more specific than field surveys. Indeed, the results of laboratory tests are quantitative values characterising the studied system, and those values are defined only within the framework of a specific physical-mathematical model. The physical-mathematical modelling of a structure is a representation of its behaviour within a certain validity range, itself depending on a set of hypotheses regarding how to describe both the studied system and the phenomena a ffecting it. The objective of such a model is to bridge the documented and/or observed state of the system to its possible causes and potential evolutions. The modelling process may be summarised into several steps. The first one is choosing a set of hypotheses on which to build the model, as well as a modelling approach, based on the collected data. The hypotheses may regard which variables are to be used in order to describe the studied system, and which phenomena should be accounted for and how. The modelling approach includes which physical principle should drive the derivation of a mathematical problem representing the studied structure, as well as which types of study should be carried out. Then, available data should be integrated into this mathematical framework in order to bridge the physical system and the theoretical model. This can be made by estimating parameter values based on the surveys and laboratory tests from the data collection step. Finally, once the model is set up, theoretical results can be derived by solving the defined mathematical problem, analytically when possible, or numerically. However, the relevance of the model with respect to the study case must be assessed first by checking its validity for known problems. Note that, in the case of a numerical resolution, another modelling step is needed to evaluate the C. A. Tran, E. Barchiesi, R. Busonera, M. E. Yildizdag, I. Trivelloni and E. Turco 793 correspondance between the original analytical problem and the numerical problem which is actually solved. Afterwards, studies can be carried out to conclude on the present state of the structure and its potential evolutions. Several aspects may be considered in order to evaluate the current damage state of a historical structure and its ability to resist present and future loads. Those aspects are complementary and each of them contributes to the final safety assessment. A brief presentation of those aspects is given in the following; more details may be found in specialised works, such as [1,3,4], for example. The historical approach to safety assessment relies on learning as much as possible about the past events involving the studied structure and its environment, in order to estimate their structural e ffects on the current damage state and potential future loads. This aspect relies heavily on the available historical documentation regarding the structure. The qualitative- empirical approach uses the current state of the structure as a guide to find the possible causes of the current damage state and their potential consequences. This approach relies on the observation of the current state of the structure, and the use of experience as an intuitive guide. The quantitative-analytical approach consists in building a model based on all acquired data and the phenomena which have been selected to be investigated. The results of this model are to be validated first by comparison with the current state of the structure, then used to predict potential evolutions of the system. This aspect is determined by the chosen physical hypotheses and principle, which in turn must take into account the survey and characterisation of the studied structure. Keeping all the previous general considerations as a framework, the aim of the present work is to introduce a modelling approach for studying a specific type of historic masonry, as well as to identify available tools and methods which may be used. More specifically, Section 2 provides a short review of the physical modelling of masonry structures: what types of phenomena may be relevant to be accounted for, and which tools may be used to formulate them. Then, Section 3 focuses on the specific case of Sardinian nuraghi 1: how they were built, what is their current state, and how those data may be taken into account if their structural modelling. Finally, the concluding remarks gathered in Section 4 summarise the main points of this study, along with directions towards future works on this matter. 2. Physical modelling of masonry structures The specific characteristics of masonry structures lead to their specific behaviour and failure modes; this must be taken into account in the structural modelling of a historical masonry. Since masonry is an assembly of units and mortar, an alteration of the physical properties (including non mechanical ones) of either of those constituents may have an impact on the mechanical behaviour of the structure. Moreover, the interaction between those constituents may also lead to other types of damage. Historic structures have been interacting with their environment for typically extended pe- riods of time. As a first consequence of this, weathering cannot be neglected since it can have structural effects through potentially complex and multi-physical damaging mechanisms. A sec- ond consequence is that, even regardless of weathering, the mechanical behaviour of a historic structure may change because of the long timescales involved: the same loads (constant or cyclic) may lead to local damage over time. A brief overview of physical phenomena potentially leading to damage within masonry units and/or mortar is given in the following; more details can be found in specialised texts (see for example the books [1,5,6]. One of the main physical damag- ing factors comes from water and humidity, not only because of direct mechanical erosion, but 1We use the Italian words: singular nuraghe, plural nuraghi. 794 C. A. Indeed, when a salty solution flows into the masonry and dries up, the salts may crystallise into florescences, whether on visible external surfaces (efflorescences) or hid- den within the masonry (cryptoflorescences). The latter may lead to internal damage by applying pressure to the surrounding material. This phenomenon may become cyclic if the environment is such that a wetting-drying cycle takes place, thus damaging the masonry progressively. It is also noteworthy to specify that direct wetting is not always necessary: so-called hygroscopic salts may take in enough water from relative humidity to activate their dissolution-crystallisation cy- cles. Less directly, water and humidity may also lead to modifications in the physical properties of the material itself, especially for mortar or masonry units made from raw clay or sedimentary rocks. In a similar way, the ambient atmosphere content may also lead to erosion, as well as activat- ing chemical corrosion through acid-base reactions or reduction-oxydation. Such phenomena may be worsened by air pollution, due to the consequent higher concentrations of acidic gases. The specific chemical species and reactions involved depend on the types of materials consid- ered. One example which may be relevant to the study of historic masonries is the degradation of limestone by sulfur dioxide (SO2). This gas can combine with water into sulfurous (H 2SO3) or sulfuric (H2SO4) acids, which then corrodes limestone (CaCO 3) to form gypsum (CaSO 4,2H2O). More examples may be found in specialised texts, such as [5]. Temperature, and especially its variations, may have a high impact on the physical proper- ties of the materials, such as density or sti ffness. An increase in temperature may also activate or accelerate chemical processes which may lead to damage, and periodic temperature changes may influence the already-mentioned wetting-drying cycles and the induced crystallisation dam- age. Those effects may be even greater if they are unevenly distributed throughout the structure, leading to high heterogeneities in the material properties. In a similar way, direct radiation from sunlight may also lead to uneven temperature changes, regarding both the surfaces of masonry structures and the ambient air. This may worsen the cyclic phenomena mentioned above. Biological factors may also be taken into account: growing vegetation can lead to high direct mechanical e ffects, depending on the environment of the studied structure (see for example Croci’ s study of Angkor temples [7], where growing roots contributed to the unstability of the architectural remnants). Other biological factors may also activate or worsen physical (e.g. density modification, ion di ffusion and crystallisation. . . ) and chemical (acid-base or redox reactions) processes damaging the structure. The difference in properties between mortar and units may lead to internal e ffects, possibly leading to or worsening internal damage. For example, under compressive loads, if the mortar’ s stiffness is too low, local tangential stresses may appear at the contact surface between unit and mortar, thus leading to internal tensile stress within the unit, even if the external load is compressive. Final remarks and future challenges The present work has provided a short review of previous studies in order to outline a methodol- ogy for assessing the preservation and damage states of Sardinian nuraghi. Regarding their me- chanical behaviour more specifically, historical considerations about the construction and geo- graphical distribution of Nuragic structures have lead us to propose several key points to be fol- lowed in upcoming research efforts towards the mechanical modelling of nuraghi. The general objective is to build physical digital twins of Nuragic structures to study their cur- rent state and predicting their potential evolutions (see Figure 1 for a graphical summary). Such digital twins are intended as the sum of all available data regarding the studied structures, thus allowing a quantitative-analytical study of their behaviour. More specifically, 3D geometrical sur- veys would provide precise data for their present configuration, while historical documentation C. A. Tran, E. Barchiesi, R. Busonera, M. E. Yildizdag, I. The sought digital twins would then be the combination of those geometrical and mechanical models. The present work has raised several common characteristics regarding the structure and construction of nuraghi. First and foremost, they have been built by corbelling; this holds for the lower (and older) protonuraghi as well as for the higher tholos nuraghi, which are conic towers having possibly several floors. This corbelled structure highly influences the statics of nuraghi, and thus the types of mechanical models to be developed. Second, most if not all Nuragic structures were built without binding materials, so they behave as dry masonries highly relying on frictional e ffects. Third, the spatial distribution of nuraghi shows higher concentrations on specific types of soils; this can give information regarding the building materials, as well as how to model the interactions between the studied structures and the ground. The previous considerations have lead us to propose several modelling key points. Firstly, regarding the kinematic description, it seems necessary to account for the precise geometry of the studied structures, since they are dry corbelled masonries. Thus, a discrete description of each individual block would seem relevant; however, for some cases it may also be relevant to add continuous parts to model small packing stones in-between much larger blocks, or modern concrete reinforcement. Secondly, regarding the phenomena to be taken into account, it will be necessary to account for friction, as well as defining a damage variable. As to what influences this damage, multiphysical loads will have to be taken into account to model weathering. The present work has raised modelling ideas from the literature concerning water di ffusion and salt crystallisation, as well as thermal and chemical e ffects. Instead of adding all those phenomena at once, it might be relevant to add them progressively, focusing each time on the interactions between the phenomenon to be added and the ones which are already taken into account. Finally, as a consequence of the previous points, variational principles can be useful here, in order to build kinematically-consistent models which are able to account for complex interactions, namely the coupling between purely mechanical phenomena on the one hand, and damages from multiphysical phenomena on the other hand. Although non-variational models do exist for such interactions, a variational approach would allow to derive governing equations in a way which is less likely to induce loss of well-posedness. Introduction Dam projects have created huge economic and social bene- fits for human beings throughout history, especially in water supply, flood control, and renewable energy production (Huang & Yan, 2009;Y €uksel, 2010). However, most as-built dams suffer from safety-related issues (e.g., excessive deformation (Chen, Gu, Bao, Wu, & Su, 2016), adverse seepage (Jiang et al., 2020), slope instability (Martt, 2005), etc.) due to the combined influence of many reasons such as increasing natural hazard risks, aging of existing infra- structure, lack of experience in dam safety management and operations, among others. These emerging safety issues may lead to potential dam accidents and further cause adverse impacts on the life, property, and environment of down- stream areas, if they are not detected and resolved in time. A comprehensive evaluation of dam service behaviour is the most important and scientific way to monitor dam oper- ation condition and diagnose potential problems (Wu, Xu, Gu, & Li, 2012). Recently, with the increasing awareness of risk prevention and control in the field of dam engineering, some research has demonstrated the necessity of the transformation of comprehensive dam evaluation mode from the traditional static and passive one to a dynamic and proactive one (Jeon, Lee, Shin, & Park, 2009; Supakchukul et al., 2019; Wu & Su, 2005). Its initiative aims to maintain dynamic information, support continuous condition monitoring of dam structures, achieve efficient decision-making, and finally minimise the risk of dam failure. The new mode of dam safety evaluation is of vital significance for diagnosing potential problems and taking reinforcement measures timely especially when some unpredicted catastrophic floods are coming or a united safety management of dam groups in the same river basin is required (Huang, Li, Hu, Zhang, & Zhao, 2020; Li, Bao, & Fang, 2005). However, in the traditional static mode, measured data that contains diagnostic information is read from different kinds of instruments or sensors and stored in one or several databases (Li & Wang, 2017), while detailed information (e.g., monitoring precision and frequency, correction param- eters and methods) of instruments are hidden in the instruction manuals and location information are presented in the monitoring instrument layout (usually a set of design drawings). The information related to dam safety monitor- ing is usually saved in different formats and separately stored in different management systems. In view of this, dam engineers may require significant time and effort to query and coordinate all necessary information for evalu- ation purposes. Hence the reasoning process from measured dam behaviour to evaluation grades is full of uncertainty. In response to this situation, the cloud model, as a cognitive model that can simultaneously describe the fuzziness, randomness, and discreteness of con- cepts and implement the uncertain transformation between qualitative concepts and quantitative description (Li, Liu, & Gan, 2009), becoming a promising alternative. Nonetheless, the combination of necessary knowledge in the field of dam engineering with the cloud model concept to realise auto- mated dam safety evaluation is still under investigation. The realisation of the dynamic and proactive mode of dam safety evaluation still faces many challenges at the cur- rent stage. This paper proposes a DT-enabled solution for comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour that contributes to tackling these challenges mentioned above and advancing the BIM provisions required to ensure dam safety during the operation and maintenance (O&M) phase of dam proj- ects. In the presented study, a new data structure is pro- vided based on the summarised information requirements of dam monitoring systems. Then, the related information is integrated into dam DTs by extending the industry founda- tion classes (IFC), which forms a fundamental basis of a reliable and robust dam evaluation program. After that, an automated evaluation framework is proposed to realise con- tinuous dam condition monitoring. In this framework, a data classification strategy to facilitate efficient establishment of evaluation index systems is proposed. To characterise uncertainties in the evaluation process, a multi-rule cloud reasoning model is utilised to evaluate monitoring points and a multi-dimensional cloud model is leveraged to assess evaluation indices affected by multiple factors. Meanwhile, an improved Criteria Importance Though Intercriteria Correlation (CRITIC) method with dam knowledge consid- ered is proposed to calculate weight values without human intervention. 2. Literature review 2.1. Current applications of digital twin in asset condition assessment Nowadays, the digital twin concept has been applied to assessment analysis of building assets because of its ability to provide convenient access to comprehensive and inte- grated information. The information (e.g., the actual envir- onment, applied loads and corresponding structural response) collected from Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) systems is the key ingredient of a DT (Tao et al., 2019). Some research utilises digital models as a visualised plat- form for intuitive interpretation of SHM data and simple assessment of structural condition of assets. For example, Boddupalli, Sadhu, Rezazadeh Azar, and Pattyson ( 2019) proposed a digital representation platform based on BIM to support the visualisation of the related SHM data. Valinejadshoubi, Bagchi, and Moselhi ( 2019) developed a DT of a modular building to store, query, and show real- time strain monitoring data. The proposed approach sup- ports the damage assessment of a column by setting a fixed threshold for monitored strain values. Kaewunruen et al. (2021) developed a ‘digital twin þ bridge risk inspection model’ to achieve preventive maintenance of bridges. This platform enables the comparison of real-time monitoring data with a ‘safety value ’ to assess structural vulnerability. There is no doubt that these studies mentioned above have highlighted the value of DT concept in asset assessment, but they only achieve informal data binding and display to per- form a fast assessment by comparing monitored structural response with standard values, which does not demand high interoperability and efficient cross-referencing between inte- grated information. Lu et al. ( 2020) proposed a DT-based anomaly detection framework to monitor the operation condition of building assets. This method enables the formal description of related information and can evaluate the mechanical condition of a centrifugal pump based on the mutual verification of its vibration monitoring data and working loads. This solution can only assess pump condition from one aspect (vibration STRUCTURE AND INFRASTRUCTURE ENGINEERING 905 performance), which is not sufficient to support comprehen- sive evaluation. It is merely suitable for the evaluation of a single function of complex structures or building assets with a single failure mode. However, a dam is an integrated structure which is exposed to complex natural environment. Many reasons may cause dam failure (International Commission on Large Dams (ICOLD),), 2013). Considering this, a comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour demands the consideration of all relevant influencing factors. Also, the baselines to assess monitored variables should be deduced from the history monitoring data and condition records, which requires high-quality data integration to support complex cross-refer- encing. Hence a more complete and systematic data struc- ture and an efficient data processing workflow are important to ensure data availability and interoperability. Currently, the absence of a standard data schema for the formal description of comprehensive monitoring systems and the absence of a data processing strategy for keeping data consistent are main barriers behind the continuous evaluation scenario. 2.2. Review of comprehensive evaluation of dam behaviour The evaluation methods of dam behaviour can be classified into two categories: physical simulation analysis and data- driven methods. Physical simulation analysis mainly lever- ages finite element method (FEM) to calculate structural responses, with model parameters being calibrated using monitoring data (Colombo, Domaneschi, & Ghisi, 2016). Data-driven methods refer to the data fusion, modelling, and analysis of massive information to mine useful informa- tion (Su, Wen, Chen, & Tian, 2016). Among them, compre- hensive evaluation of dam behaviour using multiple monitoring-related information is always a popular topic because of its significance to ensure dam safety during the O&M stage. As shown in Figure 1 , a complete evaluation index sys- tem is a multi-index and multi-layer system that includes well-organised and preprocessed monitoring data (mainly the monitored dam behaviour, such as stress, deformation, seepage, etc.). The comprehensive dam evaluation should be implemented from left to right. Hence, the reasonable and effective evaluation of each measuring point is the basis to ensure accuracy of the entire comprehensive evaluation pro- gram. After deriving the baseline of each monitoring point, the remaining challenge is to reason from a quantitative value to a qualitative index. For each monitoring point, the quantitative value is the difference between the baseline and monitored effect variable, and the qualitative index is an evaluation grade that indicates the safety status of this mon- itoring point, such as normal, abnormal, and so on. In these solutions, evaluation grades are quantified by fixed boundary condi- tions based on human experience, and functions of membership degree are curves with fixed parameters. Hence, the way to obtain safety status of monitoring points is deterministic. Conclusions To achieve the dynamic and proactive mode of dam safety evaluation, a DT-enabled solution is proposed in this paper. The results of the case study verify the ability of the pro- posed approach to provide dynamic information and per- form continuous dam safety evaluation by taking full advantage of the standardised monitoring-related informa- tion in a dam DT. It is beneficial for indicating the overall safety status, supporting decision-making, and performing preventive maintenance before severe and even catastrophic consequences happen during the O&M stage of dam proj- ects. The main innovations of this paper are the following: 1. With the help of the standardised description of moni- toring-related information in dam DT environment, the availability and interoperability of required information for complex evaluation tasks are enhanced. Compared with traditional methods of dam management, this approach can provide reliable and dynamic data to sup- port continuous safety evaluation. 2. Based on available information and the cloud model concept, an automated evaluation approach that can consider uncertainties in the evaluation process, is pro- posed. This approach can present the general trend of dam safety status in a certain period. Hence, compared with the traditional static evaluation mode, it is of vital significance for diagnosing potential problems and tak- ing reinforcement measures timely. Although the proposed comprehensive dam evaluation approach can realise continuous monitoring of dam behaviour, as verified in the case study, there are still some shortcomings existing. For example, IFC entities related to dam structures like IfcDam, IfcDamGallery, and IfcDamFoundation are not included in the current IFC schema, which still need further investigation. Meanwhile, the measurement of evaluation indices is only performed based on the historical monitoring data, but the FEM-based simula- tion results are also useful especially when some monitoring data is missing or contaminated (Salazar et al., 2019). However, to realise the complementation of these two sources of information, further study such as the integration of simu- lation results into dam DTs and the ingenious combination of these two completely different kinds of information, is still required. Introduction Urbanization is proceeding at an unprecedented pace, with towering buildings rising from urban land and ground space being fully developed and utilized. Development of large-scale underground civil infrastructures (e.g., subways, pipelines, etc.) becomes favored by construction practitioners. Excavation processes of the temporary foundation pit are thus important during the construction of large underground civil infrastructures. However, foundation pit excavation (FPE) is known as one of the most dangerous construc- tion processes that threatens the safety of all involved construction personnel. Furthermore, various weather conditions, geological factors, and the hydrogeological environment of the construction site create barriers for ensuring safe and effective FPE processes [1,2]. Safety monitoring and risk prognosis of FPE are vital for ensuring timely acquisition of spatiotem- poral risk information (e.g., foundation pit deformations) and effective risk predictions. However, current ﬁeld practices heavily rely on visual measurements and inspections using traditional measuring equipment and techniques, which is time-consuming and requires engineers to have profound knowledge and extensive ﬁeld experiences. Even experienced Buildings 2023, 13, 247. https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings13010247 https://www.mdpi.com/journal/buildings Buildings 2023, 13, 247 2 of 31 engineers could hardly identify safety risks during FPE and make timely control actions. Collapse accidents may occur and cause fatalities. Previous studies have examined the stability retaining structures during FPE processes. Fathipour et al. [3] used a well-established lower bound analysis combining the ﬁnite ele- ment method and second order conic programming (SOCP) to examine active and passive earth pressures on retaining structures with unsaturated backﬁll.Fathipour et al. [4] also combined the lower bound theorem of limit analysis with the ﬁnite element discretization method to conduct an in-depth numerical study on the lateral earth pressure of backﬁlled retaining walls with reinforced soil layers of geosynthetics. Zhang et al. [5] examined the effect of wall toe resistance on the performance of GRS walls with different toe constraints. Zhang [6] et al. explored the effect of cornering on the stability of reinforced slopes. Liu [7] studied the lateral displacements of segmental GRS walls at the end of construction and after 10 years of creep under constant gravity loads. In addition, lots of studies have conducted extensive efforts in developing methods and tools for safe and effective risk prognosis and control during FPE [ 8–12]. However, limited studies have examined methods for (1) capturing the spatiotemporal changes during FPE processes; (2) updating ﬁnite element models using the captured ﬁeld anomalies; and (3) establishing effective risk prediction and control mechanisms for mitigating safety risks during FPE processes. Digital twin (DT) has been developed and extensively studied in the manufacturing domain for creating a high-ﬁdelity virtual space model that could reﬂect all behaviors of physical objects. Such a method could help for (1) capturing the interactive process between the physical and virtual space; (2) updating the virtual model based on ﬁeld discoveries; and (3) providing feedback to the physical model for guiding the manufacturing processes. Guo et al. [13] developed a system based on DT for intelligent risk predictions during mining operations in coal mines. Zhang et al. [ 14] examined the feasibility of using DT to control mine production processes under various scenarios. Song et al. [15] proposed a DT framework with inverse kinematics for modeling human skeletons. Gopalakrishnan, S et al. [ 16] have created a model-based feature information network (MFIN) based on DT for establishing digital representations of all systems and components. Guo et al. [ 17] proposed a DT-based health management framework by integrating a self-updating model for fault diagnosis and prediction. Ruppert, T et al. [18] developed a DT-based method for predicting production states by fusing information collected from real-time positioning systems. All these efforts provide the potential for establishing a risk prognosis and control system for safe and efﬁcient FPE. Timely risk information acquisition and diagnosis during FPE processes are vital for ensuring the safe and effective construction of underground urban infrastructures. Limited studies have examined the integration of emerging sensing techniques, ﬁnite element modeling and simulation methods, and real-time data transmission tools for timely risk capturing, analysis, and prediction during FPE processes given diverse geological and hydrogeological conditions and complex shapes of the foundation pit. The major contribution of this study is to enable a DT-based risk prognosis and control for safe and efﬁcient FPE. The left part of the ﬁgure represents the activities in the physical world and the right part represents the activities in the virtual world. Physical space is the three- dimensional world we are in. This includes the structure, components, environment, data collection, and smart devices of the building at the construction site. Virtual space is mainly used to create virtual models by computer and simulate the unperformed construction based on the data. The information technology of DT is embodied through massive data. Data collection, processing, and judgment are the core of the whole system. The maintenance model allows effective maintenance measures to be made based on the results of data processing.
