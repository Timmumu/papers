F: Digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system for seamless worker-robot collaboration in construction Xiao Lin , Ziyang Guo , Xinxiang Jin , Hongling Guo * Department of Construction Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, China ARTICLE INFO Keywords: Worker-robot collaboration Digital twin Safety monitoring Construction robot ABSTRACT Worker-robot collaboration (WRC) has emerged as a transformative approach to augmenting the productivity of the construction industry. However, the development of a safety monitoring method or system for stopping robot operations in emergency is imperative, especially for seamless WRC on site. This paper presents a digital twin- enabled safety monitoring system for seamless WRC on site, characterized by its comprehensive perception of dynamic entities and dynamic calculation of protective separation distances during seamless WRC. The effec - tiveness of the proposed system is substantiated through a series of experiments, the result demonstrates its proficiency in mitigating collisions during robot operation in both static and dynamic WRC scenarios. The system achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 frames per second, an average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and a positional perception error of 0.09 m. It not only provides a practical tool for the implementation of seamless WRC on site, but also offers valuable insights for future WRC research. 1. Introduction The construction industry has shown a keen interest in robotic technology to address issues such as labour shortage, low productivity, and poor working condition. Various automated construction robot systems have been developed [ 1 ], however, challenges arise when these systems are deployed in a highly dynamic and complex construction environment, leading to reduced precision and efficiency. Worker-robot collaboration (WRC) has been recognized as a promising method by leveraging the complementary skills of humans and machines to enhance the on-site usability of construction robots [ 2 ]. The predominant form of WRC in construction currently relies on remote control methods [ 3 ]. However, closer collaboration, both spatially and temporally, can transform the role of human workers from robot operators to teammates, enhancing the productivity and flexibility of on-site WRC [ 4 ]. Despite these benefits, safety concerns remain a significant barrier to seamless WRC in construction [ 5 ]. Since workers and construction robots share a same operational space in seamless WRC, physical risks arise due to planned or unexpected contact caused by the movement of either party [ 6 ]. When workers need to collaborate closely with robots to complete tasks, physical risks in WRC can further increase [ 7 ]. Therefore, an effective safety monitoring system is essen - tial for achieving seamless WRC in construction. Despite its critical importance, implementing a safety monitoring system on construction sites presents significant challenges. The un - structured and dynamic nature of construction scenarios may decrease the efficiency and precision of the safety monitoring system [ 8 ]. Traditional safety monitoring methods in industrial WRC, such as using physical or virtual fences to spatially separate robots and workers, are impractical. These methods tend to fragment a continuous workspace, reduce operational efficiency, and fail to adapt to rapidly changing construction environment. Therefore, there is a need for an advanced safety monitoring system with fine-grained monitoring capabilities and spatial flexibility that can be easily integrated into construction robots to support safe and seamless WRC on site. This research proposes a digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system (SMS) for seamless WRC on construction sites. The contributions of this research are two-fold. First, it introduces a digital twin (DT)- based system capable of comprehensively perceiving and integrating the spatial information of workers, construction robots and construction materials during WRC, and establishing their approximate geometric occupation representations for spatial collision monitoring. Second, it proposes a rapid separation distance (SD) and a dynamic protective separation distance (PSD) calculation method for real-time safety monitoring in seamless WRC. WRC in construction Combining the hierarchical structure of construction works proposed by Bernold [ 9 ], with the Level of Robotic Automation (LoRA) defined by Beer et al. [ 10 ], it establishes a robust theoretical foundation for cate - gorizing WRC in construction. Based on the work hierarchy at which WRC occurs, the collaborative system can be classified into three types: Division Collaboration (DC), Sequence Collaboration (SC), and Task Collaboration (TC) ( Table 1 ). Each collaboration approach involves construction robots with different activity capabilities and correspond - ing LoRA that align with their designated collaboration roles. DC focuses on appropriate labour division between workers and ro - bots, such as task allocation [ 11 , 12 ] and scheduling planning [ 13 , 14 ]. Construction robots in DC are usually assumed to have a high LoRA (Levels 7–10) and can autonomously execute assigned tasks consisting of complex processes similar to workers. Through adequate labour divi - sion, workers and robots perform tasks independently in terms of time or space, resulting in clear temporal and spatial boundaries regarding their operation space [ 8 ]. Correspondingly, safety risks are minimized because there is minimal shared workspace between humans and robots, as well as physical risks. SC involves a series of subtasks executed in a predetermined process to achieve the collaboration objective, with each subtask requiring specific skills from either workers or robots [ 15 ]. In SC, human workers typically handle the more fragile and dynamic subtasks, while robots undertake those with high physical demands. Related research focuses on designing optimal processes to maximize the full potential of human- robot collaboration. For example, MitterBerger et al. [ 2 ] developed a wood framework construction method, where robots hold and position wooden beams and workers then tie them together with ropes. Con - struction activities involving the installation and delivery of construc - tion materials are a key focus of SC research [ 16 ]. For instance, Wu et al. [ 17 ] analyzed the efficiency of bricklaying teams, where robots lay bricks supplied by workers. In SC, robots’ actions may need to be planned or authorized by human workers [ 18 ], but be executed auton - omously by robots, demonstrating a middle LoRA in Levels 4–6. Nevertheless, the autonomy of robots and complex spatial interaction increases physical risks in WRC. In TC, human workers and construction robots jointly complete a single challenging task, which involves the highest level of human-robot interaction (HRI). The task is typically characterized by the high de - mand of both physical and cognitive efforts, exceeding the sense and plan capabilities of current construction robots as well as the physical ability of human workers. Therefore, human workers usually act as operators and control construction robots manually or remotely through HRI devices, such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR) and force interaction devices [ 19 ]. For example, Chen et al. [ 20 ] presented a WRC approach for the sorting of construction wastes, where workers use AR devices to guide a robot based on their judgement of potential waste materials. Given the low LoRA (Levels 1–3) of construction robots in TC, physical risks are inherently controlled as robots are under human supervision. In practical applications, WRC is usually based on hybrid configu - rations. For instance, a robot only possessing motion capability might initially collaborate with a worker to form a human-robot team for TC, thus engaging with another worker in process-level SC. Such hybrid WRC strategies become particularly prevalent when construction ro - bots’ LoRA remains insufficient to support their independent partici - pation in higher-level collaborative activities. The operational compromise enables robotic systems to contribute to advanced con - struction operations while compensating for current technological lim - itations. However, the hybridity inevitably introduces systemic safety concerns across robotic applications - irrespective of their designated roles in WRC or attained LoRA levels. This further underscores the critical need for developing adaptive safety monitoring systems in WRC. 2.2. Safety monitoring method for WRC Safety monitoring methods are pivotal in enhancing robotic safety [ 16 ]. Unlike industrial robots that are typically mounted on static bases, construction robots are usually mobile, comprising mobile platforms, kinematic structure, task-specific end-effectors, and integrated sensors [ 1 ]. The foundational architecture, first established by Shimizu Site Robot [ 21 ], remains fundamentally retained by modern construction robots. While dedicated safety standards for construction robots remain under development, ANSI/RIA R15.08 [ 22 ] provides critical normative guidance as the first safety standard for industrial mobile robots (IMR). Particularly, its classification of Type C IMR, defined as “mobile plat - forms integrated with manipulator attachments”, is a configuration structurally analogous to modern construction robots. The standard mandates that manipulators mounted on mobile platforms comply with ISO 10218 [ 23 ] requirements, which specifies four principal collabo - rative methodologies: Safety-Related Monitoring Stop (SRMS), Hand Guidance (HG), Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM), and Power and Force Limiting (PFL). The subsequent publication of ISO 15066 [ 24 ] significantly advances WRC safety frameworks by providing detailed design guidelines for safe WRC methods. Therefore, different safety monitoring methods can be designed based on WRC methods adapted in production. SRMS demands that robotic operations are conducted at a pre - determined distance from human workers, commonly achieved through the implementation of physical or virtual barriers to demarcate zones separating humans and robots. While effective and widely used in in - dustrial environments, the method has been criticized for creating spatial segmentation and may potentially undermine on-site produc - tivity [ 8 ]. The HG method facilitates teaching and programming robots through direct physical interaction [ 25 ], however, SRMS must be the prerequisite for HG. SSM defines separation distance (SD) and protective separation distance (PSD) based on the speed of humans and robots, dictating that robotic actions are permissible when SD exceeds PSD. Since SSM requires simultaneous detection of the spatial position and speed of both humans and robots, the corresponding safety monitoring method requires higher sensing resources. PFL safeguards human workers from physical harm by limiting the force output of robots during contact [ 26 ]. Therefore, SSM stands out as the most appropriate and adaptable method for ensuring safe WRC on site. With the development of sensing technologies, SSM is becoming increasingly cost-effective and feasible. The key of SSM is to calculate SD and PSD according to the spatial motion information of both robots and collaborating workers, which requires advanced robot intelligence. For monitoring human bodies within collaborative workspace, sensors with three-dimensional perception capabilities, such as light detection and ranging (LiDAR) [ 27 ] and depth cameras [ 28 ], are commonly employed. For seamless WRC, limb-level monitoring becomes imperative for accurate SD sensing. The effectiveness of SD sensing is heavily influenced by how obstacles are represented. For instance, Lucci et al. [ 26 ] utilized depth cameras for the limb-level detection of human workers, but the collision representation relied on point clouds, which are less versatile than surface-based representations like triangle meshes. Similarly, Rose - nstrauch et al. [ 29 ] used Kinect V2 for human body detection, with a simplified representation consisting of joint keypoints without collision volumes. Li et al. [ 30 ] also adopted joint keypoints for SD calculations. However, in close WRC scenarios, keypoints may not always represent the closest points on a worker ’ s body relative to a robot, potentially leading to inaccurate estimation for SD. Surface-based representations for both workers and robots can provide more accurate SD measure - ments. Nevertheless, overly complex representations, such as the Skin - ned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model [ 25 ], need substantial computing resources, thus hindering real-time performance. Simplified geometric representations, including spheres, boxes, and capsules [ 31 , 32 ], offer a viable alternative, but the level of detail (LoD) must be carefully designed to strike a balance between computational efficiency and representation accuracy. While ISO 15066 provides calculation methods for PSD, obtaining necessary value for calculation, particularly the speed of workers and robots, renders it challenging. A common approach is to set a constant value for workers ’ speed to estimate PSD [ 33 – 35 ], where a maximum speed assumption for workers is often established for safety. However, the assumption also constrains robots ’ workspace, potentially reducing the efficiency and flexibility of WRC [ 36 ]. Besides, current research for safety monitoring concentrates on WRC in manufacturing, where robots usually operate on a tabletop scale, using stationary arms to handle small objects. However, in construction sites, components and objects such as drywall panels manipulated by robots may have a large size and should not be overlooked [ 18 ]. Conclusion Seamless WRC has considerable potential for enhancing productivity in construction but must be supported by adequate safety monitoring methods or systems. Although SSM is deemed suitable for WRC on site, the practicality of existing methods is limited due to that they are designed especially for industrial WRC that is less dynamic and complex. To bridge the gap, this paper introduced a digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system. The proposed SMS offers the following contribu - tions: 1) a comprehensive perception method for WRC entities, including construction workers, robots, and materials; 2) a geometric solid-based virtual representation method for WRC entities that balances sensing accuracy and efficiency; 3) a rapid calculation method for SD, and 4) a dynamic calculation method for PSD. The above-mentioned physical entities, their virtual twins, and relevant functions are inte - grated within a digital twin system. Experiments have shown that the proposed SMS achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 FPS, an average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and an SD perceiving error of 0.09 m. This system has been validated in both static and dynamic-conflict scenarios, demonstrating its safety-guarding capabilities. The future directions for further enhancing the proposed SMS are discussed as follows. First, this system will be validated on a broader range of construction robots beyond the mobile manipulator configu - ration, such as humanoid robots. Second, it will continuously benefit from more advanced perception methods for human workers and con - struction materials with less latency and higher accuracy. Finally, the incorporation of predictive methods for workers ’ and robots ’ motion will further enhance the performance of PSD calculation, empowering a more robust and flexible WRC on site. Introduction As a disruptive innovation, Modular Integrated Construction (MiC) is increasingly replacing traditional methods, offering benefits such as improved quality, simplified management, and shorter project du- rations (Pan and Hon, 2020; Zheng et al., 2020). During the MiC construction process, efficient module stacking is critical for timely assembly, high construction quality, and safety (Shan et al., 2019; Zhai et al., 2019). In practice, module stacking still depends on manual ob- servation and the expertise of crane operators (Zhu et al., 2022), which gives rise to several critical challenges. First, crane operators often exercise delayed control due to insufficient real-time information for accurate perception of module status, thereby increasing the likelihood of safety incidents, stacking errors, and module collisions (Kim and Chi, 2021). Second, the lack of timely and integrated information sharing among stakeholders causes delays and fragmentation, which hinder coordination and perception across the project team (Zhao et al., 2022). In contrast, Digital Twin (DT) technology provides a robust solution by replicating physical processes in a virtual environment and integrating sensors for real-time monitoring and prediction (Zhou et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Within the DT framework, module stacking is redefined to include real-time visibility, cyber–physical connectivity, and traceability. Furthermore, a DT is incorporated into collaborative platforms that facilitate access to and sharing of module information among stakeholders, enhancing information synchroniza- tion. Consequently, this paper aims to leverage the advantages of DT to achieve comprehensive visibility and traceability at module stacking sites through real-time data collection and positioning. Nevertheless, addressing delayed control and achieving predictive guidance requires further development of precise methods for module pose estimation and trajectory prediction within the DT framework. Computer vision-based methods have been proposed by researchers and practitioners (Zhang et al., 2019). Yang et al. (2023) suggested surveillance cameras for environmental perception and worker trajec- tory prediction to boost construction safety. Similarly, Cheng et al. (2022) introduced a stereo-vision technique for aligning precast con- crete bridge components. Considering that vision-based methods often fail to capture accurate 3D spatial relationships, Xie et al. (2025) proposed a framework that leverages point cloud data to detect human– object interactions on construction sites. However, on-site practices re- veal that these methods struggle in dynamic and variable construction environments, poorly addressing challenges like cluttered backgrounds, wind, vibrations, fluctuating lighting, and limited camera angles (Kop- sida and Brilakis, 2020; Omar and Nehdi, 2016; Liu et al., 2023). In contrast, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors can capture high precision, multisource stacking data in real-time, offering superior environmental adaptability and reliability. Combined with IoT devices, high fidelity virtual models in the DT framework can reflect the dynamic state, be- havior, and changes of the physical stacking system in a comprehensive perspective (Zhou et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Current methods for predicting trajectories with IoT sensors pri- marily use temporal models to analyze historical motion data se- quences (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang and Ge, 2022). These sequential relationships expose the temporal continuity and directional trends of motions, enabling more reliable future trajectory predictions. However, in complex spatial movements like module ro- tation or flipping, reliance solely on temporal relationships for the module trajectory prediction proves challenging. This is mainly because such movements entail multi-dimensional displacement and spatial pose adjustments. This difficulty often requires deep learning networks with advanced capabilities for handling complex nonlinear problems. Recently, deep learning models have achieved outstanding perfor- mance in multiple domains (Shang et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). Specifically, in tasks that involve extracting complex spatiotemporal features, Zhang et al. (2024) designed a multi-attention network based on spatio-temporal feature fusion to predict pedestrian crossing intention. Ali et al. (2025) proposed a spatio-temporal graph convolutional recurrent network with multi-scale feature extraction and dual attention mechanisms, achieving superior long-term urban traffic prediction on real-world datasets. Wang et al. (2025) recently proposed a large language model (LLM)-driven probabilistic trajectory prediction framework, which integrates spatio-temporal encoding with normalizing flows to model uncertainty and complex motion dynamics. Although these methods struggle to represent the complex spatial posture changes during the module installation process, they show that accurate pose prediction of modules depends crucially on effective modeling of spatial relationships and temporal dependencies among trajectory points, particularly in complex movements. Spatial structures elucidate the distribution and interrelation of points in space, vital for deciphering movement patterns. Consequently, this paper introduces a DT-enabled module stacking system (DTPTS) with a novel network, PosTraFormer, which enhances controllability by modeling the com- plex spatial posture changes during the module installation process through multi-layer graph convolutional networks (GCNs), and by estimating posture and predicting trajectories through the integration of spatiotemporal modeling. DTPTS en- ables real-time automatic mapping of stacking activities and unified information sharing among stakeholders, while PosTraFormer analyzes key stacking factors for enhanced trajectory prediction. Experimental scenarios and data preparation To verify the feasibility of the DTPTS an experimental platform for hoisting pose estimation and trajectory prediction is conducted in a real laboratory setting, as shown in Fig. 7. The setup comprises a physical stacking site, a digital stacking site, and an intelligent data management system. The physical stacking site mainly includes a buffer zone for storing modules, a building under construction, cranes, mod- ules, workers, gateway, and base stations. The buffer zone measures 0.7 m in length and 0.35 m in width, capable of storing up to 16 modules. The building model follows the mainstream Podium System construction method, standing 1.35 m tall with 15 floors, including a podium section at the bottom and a framework section above. Base stations are strategically deployed around the building. Each room is modular and demountable, closely resembling real construction instal- lation scenarios. Additionally, every module embeds intelligent RFID tags, UWBs, and IMUs. The smart crane has an operating radius of 1 m, a maximum payload of 10 kg, and a lifting speed of 0.1 m/s. A gateway is mounted on the crane hook to capture real-time data from modules and transmit control commands to the tower crane. The digital stacking site is developed by WebGL, which serves as the DT of the aforementioned physical assets. To facilitate unified management of di- verse structural data, a ‘‘Trinity’’ platform is developed, encompassing intelligent modules, workers, and cranes. The entire trajectory from hoisting the module in the buffer zone to placing it at the designated position is sampled at 2 Hz. A sliding window of fixed length is used to trim the trajectory starting from every alternate time step of the original trajectory (specifically, 5 s, 10 frames). Each trimmed trajectory is divided into two parts: the historical trajectory (input) and the predicted trajectory (output or la- bel), totaling 1200 trimmed trajectories. In the experiment, the dataset is split into training and evaluation sets in a 7:3 ratio. The training set is utilized to optimize the parameters of the trajectory prediction model, while the evaluation set monitors the convergence of the model. Additionally, the module storage and movement status, construction progress of each building layer, and occupancy status of the tower crane are automatically updated at the same timestamp during this process. Dynamic activity data of workers, such as their movement paths and actions on the construction site, are captured via smart wristbands and helmets. All the aforementioned data is integrated into the SIFC database and subjected to data calibration and synchronization to ensure consistency and accuracy among various data sources. To address possible jitter and anomalies in the collected data, Kalman filtering is employed for smoothing and noise removal. Polynomial interpolation is utilized to handle missing values. Finally, to standardize the scale of position and orientation data for model training, the data is normalized. 5.1.2. Data processing In this section, we describe the process of augmenting the lifting dataset through data augmentation techniques to ensure that various possible lifting scenarios are covered during model training, including normal lifting trajectories as well as anomalous situations (e.g., sen- sor malfunctions). As a result, the dataset size was expanded from a few hundred trajectories to 6000 trajectories. The data augmentation methods primarily include two techniques: using Time-Series Genera- tive Adversarial Network (TimeGAN) to simulate real lifting data and injecting anomalous trajectory points into certain trajectory data using noise. Real normal stacking data simulation. TimeGAN is used to gen- erate samples that resemble the distribution of real lifting data. The generator of TimeGAN learns the temporal patterns of the real data, thus simulating new trajectory data. This approach effectively enhances the diversity of the dataset, especially when the original data is limited. Specifically, we input real lifting data into the TimeGAN model and train the generator to produce trajectory point sequences similar to the real data. After several iterations, the generator can output a large amount of new data that is temporally similar to the real data. To validate the effectiveness of the generated data, we compared the distribution of the generated data with the real data using principal component analysis. Fig. 8 demonstrates that the generated data’s dis- tribution in the two-dimensional space closely matches the distribution of the real data. These generated data not only increase the sample size but also enhance the diversity of the dataset, helping to improve the model’s generalization ability, particularly when dealing with unknown lifting situations. This method identifies the most unfavourable defect mode for structural instability. Xu et al. [ 7 ] introduced a reliability-based method for calculating the critical stability load-bearing capacity of single-layer reticulated shell structures and validated its effectiveness through two case studies. Borri et al. [ 8 ] used the random defect mode method to study the influence of the distribution pattern and size of initial geometric im - perfections on the structural stability bearing capacity. Chen et al. [ 9 ] proposed a modelling method for initial geometric imperfections in reticulated shell structures based on random field theory and used it to investigate the effect of these imperfections on the structural stability performance. Furthermore, Cui et al. [ 10 ] proposed a simultaneous autoregressive model capable of simulating spatially varying initial geometric imperfections, including those in irregular reticulated shell structures. As can be seen from the above studies, the main methods currently used to account for initial geometric imperfections are the random defect mode method and the consistent defect mode method. However, it should be noted that the defect modes assumed in these methods are random, whereas in constructed structures the defect mode is deterministic. Therefore, when performing a safety analysis on an existing structure, calculations should be based on the actual defect shape. Digital twins, which serve as digital replicas of real-world assets, can effectively reflect the actual state of structures, including stresses, deformations and geometric imperfections. Many scholars have used three-dimensional (3D) laser scanning technology and reverse modelling techniques to capture geometric imperfections of structures and create geometric digital twins. Sangani et al. [ 11 ] used 3D digital image correlation equipment to scan tubular steel columns with initial geometric defects, and evaluated the residual capacity of tubular steel columns according to the scanned model. Chacon et al. [ 12 ] measured the initial geometric defects of planar steel frames using a terrestrial laser scanner. Tzortzinis et al. [ 16 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to measure the corrosion of bridges and combined 3D laser scanning with finite element to evaluate the residual capacity of steel beams. Ban et al. Lazzari et al. [ 18 ] proposed a method for measuring geometric defects and dimensional deviations in GFRP profiles using a 3D contact coordinate measuring machine. Feng et al. [ 19 ] used 3D laser scanning technology to obtain and evaluate initial geometric defects such as prestressed CFRP reinforced columns. Zou et al. [ 20 ] attempted to use 3D laser scanning technology to obtain the initial geometric defects of steel structural components, and proposed a method to build a digital model of the components based on 3D laser point cloud data. Xu et al. Aktepe et al. [ 22 , 23 ] used a 3D laser scanner to measure the geometric defects of cold-formed thin-walled steel in detail to propose a predictive expression for the initial geometric defects. However, most current research uses terrestrial 3D laser scanners to acquire point cloud data of structures, and the geometric digital twins produced are mostly in the form of 3D solid models. Terrestrial 3D laser scanners face challenges in measuring spatial structures with large spans, and it is not economically feasible to use 3D solid models for computational analysis of such structures. Introduction With the rapid advancement of tunneling engineering technology, large-diameter slurry shield (LDSS) technology has emerged and gained widespread adoption ( Chen et al., 2024 , 2025 ; Wu et al., 2023 ). it has been applied in significant projects such as the Slender West Lake Tunnel in Yangzhou ( Chenn kui, 2014 ) and the Sanyang Road Tunnel in Wuhan ( Wang, 2015 ). However, the diversity of the geological environments and the uncertainty of the construction scenarios have significantly increased the safety risks associated with LDSS operations ( Wu et al., 2024 ), particularly the precise prediction and control of tunnel face stability (TFS) ( Xu et al., 2021 ). As the diameter of the shield increases, the geological structures encountered by the tunnel become more complex ( Li et al., 2017 ; Yu et al., 2023 ), and the frequency of encountering multilayered strata and composite strata with varying mechanical properties ( Chen et al., 2025 ), such as intermixed soft and hard clay and sand, significantly increases ( Cong et al., 2023 ). These challenges not only markedly increase the complexity of the excavation environment but also greatly increase the difficulty and intricacy of maintaining TFS. Therefore, the TFS is crucial for ensuring the safe construction of an LDSS. Traditional research on TFS issues has been mostly centered around theoretical analysis, numerical simulation, and experimental studies. As a branch of data-driven methods, deep learning has been exten - sively utilized by many scholars owing to its powerful data processing and analysis capabilities, as well as its capacity to reveal and capture complex nonlinear relationships within data ( Liu et al., 2021 ; Phoon and Zhang, 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2020 ).。However, existing research indicates that the construction parameters in SM construction exhibit spatiotem - poral relationships; these relationships are difficult for conventional data-driven methods to capture and analyze, and their predictive per - formances are reduced ( Fu et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2024b ). Moreover, some existing studies mainly focus on the accurate prediction of TFS under traditional SM conditions; however, there is limited research on establishing suitable data-driven methods for precise prediction and control of TFS under LDSS conditions. Among the various data-driven methods, LSTM is commonly used for long time series data but can only predict future moments based on past information ( Li et al., 2019 ). This is insufficient for LDSS construction parameters, which may also depend on future moments ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Siami-Namini et al., 2019 ). To address this, BiLSTM, combining forward and backward LSTMs, is introduced to process LDSS tunnel construction data ( Graves and Schmidhuber, 2005 ). Additionally, TFS in LDSS construction is influenced by both past construction parameters and spatial relationships among parameters ( Fu et al., 2024 ). For example, different parameters are emphasized in soft soil and hard rock ( Chen et al., 2022 ; Zhao et al., 2019 ). To capture these spatial re - lationships, GCNs are introduced, representing data as graph structures and using the Laplacian matrix for convolution operations. During the development of data-driven method ( Wu et al., 2026 ), hyperparameter optimization is a critical preliminary step aimed at identifying the hyperparameter configurations that achieve optimal model performance during both the training and testing phases ( Xu et al., 2025 ). While traditional optimization methods, such as manual operation (MO), grid search (GS), and random search (RS), provide re - searchers flexibility in model tuning, they suffer from inefficiency, subjective bias, wasted computational resources, and inadequate opti - mization ( Victoria and Maragatham, 2021 ). In contrast, Bayesian opti - mization (BO) introduces a probabilistic acquisition function to efficiently explore the parameter space which significantly increases the likelihood of discovering the global optimum ( Chen et al., 2025 ). Therefore, to improve the prediction and control performance of data-driven methods, a hybrid BO-GCN-BiLSTM method is proposed. Additionally, to address the research gap in key construction pa - rameters for TFS during LDSS construction, this paper employs the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify and analyze these key parameters. By utilizing this method, it is possible to accurately deter - mine the construction parameters that significantly impact TFS ( Xu et al., 2026 ), thereby enabling precise control of TFS in LDSS construction. Data-driven methods can improve prediction and control perfor - mance but may lack reliability for practical engineering due to the absence of practical experience and expert knowledge ( Zhang et al., 2024a ). Therefore, a knowledge-data driven method integrating expert knowledge is more suitable for guiding TFS during LDSS construction. This approach can select input parameters and identify key influencing factors based on expert knowledge and literature ( Su et al., 2025 ). However, most existing research focuses on developing prediction and control algorithms, with limited efforts on creating a unified platform for integrating knowledge-driven and data-driven methods with phys - ical systems to enhance LDSS control and monitoring capabilities. Digital twin (DT) technology integrates construction site data with virtual dynamic models to create a digital simulation platform, showing superior capabilities in tunnel operation and management ( Boje et al., 2020 ; Yu et al., 2021 , 2023 ). Recent studies have applied the concept of DT to general shield tunnel excavation, enabling efficient remote interaction between physical and virtual entities by transforming physical equipment into digital models ( Pan and Zhang, 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2024b ). However, research on using DT platforms for data collection and TFS prediction and control in LDSS construction is limited. This paper proposes a DT platform that integrates data-driven methods (BO-GCN-BiLSTM and SHAP) with expert knowledge to form a knowledge-data driven method, enabling data acquisition and sup - porting TFS prediction and control in LDSS construction. In summary, the following issues motivate this paper: (1) how to analyze the spatiotemporal relationships among the various construc - tion parameters affecting the TFS during LDSS construction; (2) what construction parameters are the key factors affecting the TFS during LDSS construction; and (3) how to implement prediction and control of TFS in LDSS construction. Model interpretability analysis As a powerful tool, SHAP has gained attention for its ability to pro - vide interpretability of model predictions. Through this method, the reality service layer of the DT platform can not only identify the LDSS construction parameters that most significantly impact TFS but also understand how these parameters should be adjusted according to different geological types. This provides deeper insights into ensuring safety during LDSS construction. The SHAP results for each geological type are shown in Figs. 16 – 18 . As shown in Figs. 16 – 18 , the ranking of construction parameters significantly impacting TFS varies across different geological types. Specifically, the advance pressures A-E (x 13 -x 17 ) have a notably signif - icant impact on TFS in Geological Types 5 and 6. Particularly in Geological Type 5, construction parameters x 16 and x 13 both rank within the top five most influential construction parameters affecting TFS. Conversely, in Geological Types 1 and 2, the influence of these con - struction parameters is relatively minor, with lower rankings. Moreover, the in and out slurry pressure difference (x 6 ) exhibits higher influence rankings in Geological Types 1 and 3. However, in Geological Types 2, 4, 5, and 6, x 6 is identified as the most significant construction parameters impacting TFS. Meanwhile, some construction parameters maintain consistent rankings across all six geological types; for instance, Air cushion chamber pressure (x 1 ) consistently ranks within the top three in terms of its influence on TFS across all geological types, underscoring its consistent importance. Although SHAP can identify important construction parameters affecting TFS under different geological types, it is essential to distin - guish between active and passive parameters in LDSS construction. Active parameters can be directly adjusted by operators, while passive parameters, though identified as important, cannot be controlled. Additionally, some active parameters may rank lower but still require attention during operations. Therefore, based on SHAP and expert knowledge, the final selection of the top five parameters influencing TFS across various geological types are: x 1 , x 7 , x 9 , x 10 , and x 11 . The above analysis results also can be verified in similar studies in the past ( Chen et al., 2013 ; Shang et al., 2023 ; Weng et al., 2020 ). In this study, the interpretability of the BO-GCN-BiLSTM model is enhanced through the adoption of SHAP, revealing the complexity and geological dependency of how LDSS construction parameters influence TFS. This provides guidance for identifying key construction parameters affecting the entire well section. Furthermore, SHAP-based interpreta - tion of the data-driven method establishes a theoretical foundation for adaptive control strategies in LDSS operations under varying geological conditions. During construction, LDSS operators can leverage SHAP analysis results from the DT platform combined with integrated expert knowledge to adjust TFS control strategies specifically for different geological types, thereby achieving an adaptive closed-loop TFS control encompassing interpretation, decision-making, and execution. 4. Introduction In modern engineering, the characterization and estimation of both the seismic response and the performance of structural and non- structural systems have evolved through the incorporation of innova- tive technologies. These tools allow users to capture and integrate all building components into a virtual model through numerical models, which are meant to approximate the behavior of structures and on un- certainties on the level and frequency of extreme loadings. However, these representations are constrained by the scope of work, the reli- ability required for each particular project, the level of detailing of the model, and the capabilities/limitations of the software for the simula- tion. In addition, access to specific building features related to geometry, material properties, damage reports from past events, and interventions plays a key role when determining the model ’ s adequacy and accuracy in properly capturing the seismic response of the construction. Never- theless, this practice has nowadays been widely standardized, to some extent, in national and international codes and standards for common practice [1,2] , under what has been cataloged as seismic vulnerability assessment for existing buildings. Special attention has been given to buildings classified as heritage buildings, which present a unique structural behavior when subjected to external loadings. In fact, many seismic vulnerability studies have demonstrated that, despite having similar features from a geometric and material standpoint, their seismic response cannot be grouped into a generic category [3 – 7] . One of the main goals of SHM is the creation of systems capable of predicting damage levels. At this point, the Intelligent Circular Resilience (ICR) is proposed as a novel methodological framework aimed at improving DRR management by extending the concept of dynamic circular seismic resilience through the integration of SHM, real-time data analysis, and active public-private participation. The framework operates across three stages — understanding seismic risk, implementing mitigation strategies, and responding to seismic events — within a continuous, circular process that identifies blind spots in risk perception, institutional coordination, and infrastructure planning. ICR is designed to function on three inter- dependent levels: institutional expertise, infrastructure risk manage- ment, and real-time monitoring, each supported by the adoption of emerging technologies toward the development of intelligent and resilient urban environments [43] . On one hand, this approach addresses the challenges and limitations encountered in heritage buildings by identifying critical features such as geometry configuration, material properties, and available historical records. On the other hand, it deals with the duality of the physical and virtual world through the selection and installation of sensor devices and the devel- opment of a trustworthy DT model for continuous feedback. This research delves into the Building Virtualization and partially on Data Gathering and Analysis from Sensors phases outlined in Fig. 1 (i.e., steps 02 and 03, respectively). The broad development of the methodology will be complete in further publications, incorporating experimental models and sensor deployment, covering phases 3 – 6. In this study, the DT is defined as a dynamic numerical representa- tion of the heritage building that replicates its behavior under seismic loading, continuously updated and cross-validated through real-time data from strategically placed sensors. This virtual replica integrates geometric fidelity, material heterogeneity, historical performance data, and predictive deep learning (DL) algorithms, enabling proactive structural assessment and decision-making. Geometrical accuracy is critical in the development of DT models, especially when dealing with heritage buildings whose non-linear seismic behavior is highly sensitive to local geometric irregularities. Recent studies (e.g., [44,45] ) demon- strate the power of combining 3D scanning technologies and photo- grammetric methods to reconstruct detailed digital geometries suitable for structural analysis. In parallel, recent work by [46] outlines a sys- tematic pipeline for generating discrete element models (DEM) directly from imagery, reinforcing the potential of image-to-DEM workflows for data-driven seismic assessment. The analyzed phases were achieved through three main steps; the first provides guidance on the available and recommended field survey methods to characterize the geometric configuration and material properties using a Building Information Model (BIM). The second fo- cuses on the creation of the virtual model using a finite element method (FEM) model and covers key aspects such as software selection based on its features, capabilities, and limitations; the linking process between the BIM and FEM model, modeling techniques, and seismic hazard defini- tion are also well-thought-out . Finally, the last phase highlights the challenges that arise during the post-processing of results and highlights the available tools to analyze massive data by suggesting a novel idea that incorporates the intensity-duration-frequency plots for the engi- neering demand parameters (EDP). Therefore, all these steps lead to the integration process between the physical and virtual world under the ICR framework (see Fig. 2 ). The process deals with aspects related to the deployment of a reliable SHM while focusing on the Early-warning system (EWS) and its con- nectivity between both worlds in real-time. Digital twins, as virtual entities, are digital representations of physical entities for understanding, predicting, optimizing and controlling natural objects. The universality of its construction method directly affects the application effect of the digital twin so - lution. Colucci et al. investigated the interoperability of HBIM with 3D GIS, but their aim was limited to providing more comprehensive data documen - tation for heritage conservation [ 24 ]. It aims to compre - hensively capture data regarding the dynamic changes of architectural heritage over time and under various influences through multi- level real-time/quasi-real-time situational awareness and ultra-real-time virtual extrapolation, facilitating the transition from salvage to preventive conservation. Architectural heritage preventive conservation is a critical concern based on the notions above. Digital twin modeling Architectural heritage digital twin construction is the digital modeling of physical entities ’ properties, methods, behaviors, and other characteristics in a digital space to simulate and predict their state and behavior. In order to ensure that the twin is highly consistent with the physical entity, it needs to be modeled in a virtual environment from the fusion of four dimensions: geometry, physics, behavior and rules, as shown in Fig. 2 . (1) Geometric Modeling Geometric modeling ( M G ) is a digital representation of architectural heritage, detailing the geometric attributes of elements such as columns, beams, and sapphires. These define the heritage ’ s intricate internal and external structures and constitute the initial phase in creating a digital twin. Point cloud modeling and building information modeling are commonly utilized. Point cloud modeling uses laser scanning and other techniques to gather dense point cloud data, thereby capturing the intricate aspects of the item. The volume of data remains substantial and necessitates high-performance computing resources. This work employs a hybrid point cloud approach and building information modeling to ensure data consistency to reconstruct physical things ’ geometric form and non-graphical data, thereby establishing a foundation for future model analysis and interactive functions. (2) Physical Modeling Physical modeling ( M P ) is the addition of physical properties and constraints to a geometric model to simulate the dynamic response of built heritage under natural environments (e.g., wind, rain, earthquakes, etc.) or anthropogenic forces, such as stresses and deformations. Mathematical approximations are usually made using finite element modeling techniques to construct models reflecting the physical phenomena of built heritage at macro and micro scales. Training and optimizing the models with real-time data streams can achieve a high-level mapping of the built heritage ’ s physical entities. Conclusion and discussion This study focuses on architectural legacy as the subject of research and proposes the building of a five-dimensional digital twin model. The objective is to create a highly detailed digital replica that can accurately replicate and analyze the intricate features of architectural heritage. Furthermore, the practicality and applicability of the architectural heritage digital twin 5D model framework and the architectural heritage digital twin prototype are confirmed through the use of the Yingxian Wooden Pagoda as a case study. (1) Constructing geometric and physical models of wooden tower buildings based on historical building information modelling By integrating standard parametric models with irregular triangular mesh models, a detailed hybrid HBIM model of wooden tower buildings has been developed. However, the model sometimes tends to select the closest previous observation value as the current prediction value, leading to prediction delays. Second, the feature extraction method based on a sliding time window strategy has an inherent lag, making it unable to respond promptly to trend changes in the data sequence.
