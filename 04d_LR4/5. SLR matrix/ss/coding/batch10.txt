Introduction With the continuous expansion of global infrastructure networks and the growing number of structures that are reaching or exceeding their design service life, the operation and maintenance (O&M) of civil infrastructure (CI) is facing increasing complexity. Data from the New Climate Economy and McKinsey show that global infrastructure assets now exceed USD 100 trillion. Around 60% of bridges and roads are already in mid- or late-life stages and are entering periods of concentrated aging and performance decline [1]. According to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and the ASCE report based on the National Bridge Inventory (NBI), the United States has more than 619,000 bridges. Over 42% of these bridges have been in service for more than 50 years, and about 7.5% are classified as structurally deficient [2]. Studies by the OECD and the European Union further show that many key bridges and tunnels in the core Trans-European Transport Network (TEN-T) were built in the 1960s and 1970s and now face pressure from outdated functions and delayed rehabilitation [3]. Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 1848 https://doi.org/10.3390/app16041848 Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 1848 2 of 28 At the same time, the digitalization level of infrastructure O&M is rising worldwide. Smart monitoring devices, large-scale sensor networks, and automated inspection systems are driving exponential growth in data volume. In intelligent transportation systems and infrastructure IoT deployments, city- and nation-level networks rely on millions of sensor nodes, and a single large monitoring system can generate gigabytes to terabytes of high-frequency data each day [4,5]. According to the Ministry of Transport of China, the total length of rural roads exceeded 4.5 × 106 km by the end of 2023. In several provinces and cities, automated pavement condition surveys using inspection vehicles and digital platforms have achieved or nearly achieved a 70% automation rate, which demonstrates a clear improvement in the digitalization level of rural road O&M [ 6]. These trends indicate that modern CI has become a large-scale and highly complex system with multi- scale characteristics, wide spatial coverage, diverse structural forms, and heterogeneous data sources. Although many countries are promoting smart monitoring, health diagnosis, and digital management, current CI O&M systems are approaching the practical limits of purely data-driven automation when applied to infrastructure networks of this scale and complexity. This challenge highlights the need for digital twin (DT) frameworks that go beyond data aggregation and visualization, and that can explicitly incorporate human expertise, engineering knowledge, and explainable reasoning mechanisms. DTs have become an important technical pathway for CI O&M. A DT establishes a dynamic mapping among the physical structure, the virtual model, and real-time multi- source data. This mapping enables state visualization, behavior prediction, and quantitative decision support, which strengthens monitoring, diagnosis, and risk assessment. However, recent reviews in leading international journals show several major limitations in current DT applications for infrastructure: (1) high cost and complexity of multi-source data fusion and real-time updating; (2) weak integration between DT workflows and organizational or human decision processes; and (3) limited capability to formalize structural codes and engineering knowledge within DT systems. In parallel, deep learning and data- driven methods in Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) have achieved notable progress but still face key challenges, including incomplete data, weak explainability, poor scene generalization, and limited ability to absorb expert knowledge and engineering standards. Recent studies report that common SHM models for images and sensor data exhibit poor scalability, weak real-time performance, insufficient coupling of multi-variable features, and limited interpretability, which restrict real engineering deployment [7]. Similar conclusions have been repeatedly emphasized in multimodal SHM research. Compared with fully automated monitoring algorithms, traditional manual inspection and expert-based judgment retain strong advantages in understanding structural codes, recognizing subtle abnormalities, and handling complex field conditions. However, this human-centered approach faces severe scalability constraints when applied to large-scale infrastructure networks. According to the White Paper Development of China’s Rural Roads in the New Era issued by the State Council Information Office, China completed the rehabilitation of 58,000 unsafe rural bridges over the past decade. The proportion of Class I, II, and III rural road bridges increased from 83.2% to 98%, and the number of unsafe bridges declined each year [8]. This achievement required extensive human and material resources within a concentrated time period, showing that large-scale O&M relies heavily on massive resource input. Under routine O&M conditions, the rapidly increasing number of structures, the accelerating aging of bridges, and the combined impact of hidden damage and extreme events make it difficult for manual inspection and human-led decisions to provide full coverage in time, space, and precision. https://doi.org/10.3390/app16041848 Appl. To address this gap, this study proposes a human-AI-knowledge (HAK) integrated digital twin framework with explicit human-in-the-loop (HITL) interaction for CI O&M. The proposed framework combines human experience and cognition, structural codes, engineering knowledge, and explainable artificial intelligence within a unified DT envi- ronment. Through knowledge formalization, human experience modeling, explainable AI reasoning, and multi-source spatiotemporal data fusion, the framework supports intelli- gent recognition of structural changes, mechanism-based diagnosis, and risk prediction. This study does not aim to improve performance only through data-driven optimization. Instead, it focuses on improving engineering interpretability, diagnostic credibility, and practical feasibility in DT-based CI O&M systems. The HITL interaction links the device layer, the model layer, and the decision layer. In this way, the system becomes more reliable and more transparent, and it can be applied more easily in real projects. It also supports continuous development, joint participation, and long-term knowledge storage. In this study, we mainly show that the proposed framework is feasible in practice, logically consis- tent, and reasonable from an engineering point of view. We do not focus on quantitative performance comparison in this paper. This will be studied in future work. 2. Literature Review 2.1. Digital Twin Modeling DTs are a key technology in CI. They link physical structures with virtual models and support real-time reflection and prediction of structural conditions. From a system engineering point of view, a DT has continuous and two-way mapping between the physical entity, the virtual model, and related data streams. Because of this mapping, the digital model can change over time and follow the operating state of the infrastructure [9]. In construction and infrastructure projects, DT modeling usually uses geometric information, material parameters, and sensor networks to build accurate models of the physical system [10]. In the virtual domain, many studies use hybrid modeling methods. These methods combine mechanism-based, data-driven, and knowledge-based approaches. Abualdenien and Borrmann show that clearly defined meta-models and multi-level model structures are important for keeping consistency and allowing models to work at different levels of detail [ 11]. Chiachío et al. show that virtual models should be updated and checked continuously with multi-source monitoring data. In this way, a closed loop can be built between the physical system and the digital system [ 12]. Jiang et al. also propose a multi-domain DT framework. This framework organizes different types of data, models, and semantic information in one unified structure, so it can support complex infrastructure systems [13]. Recent studies show that DT development is changing. In the past, it mainly focused on a “physical-virtual” two-domain framework. Now, it is moving to a more integrated “human-machine-knowledge” framework. In this new view, human interaction and knowl- edge are treated as core parts of the DT system. They are not seen as external inputs anymore. A recent review on human-centered DTs shows that human-machine collab- oration and knowledge integration are important for reasoning and decision-making in complex and uncertain situations [14]. These studies show that DT modeling is changing from a data-centered approach to a knowledge-centered and human-centered approach. Because of this change, DTs can provide a more reliable foundation for intelligent O&M. https://doi.org/10.3390/app16041848 Appl. Sci. 2026, 16, 1848 4 of 28 2.2. Human Factors in Civil Infrastructure Operation and Maintenance (CI O&M) Human factors are very important in CI O&M. They directly affect monitoring quality, risk judgment, and decision reliability. Many studies show that structural inspection and defect detection still depend a lot on the experience and professional knowledge of field engineers. When compared with fully automated systems, humans are usually more sensitive to small abnormalities, complex environmental effects, and rare operating conditions. Reason’s classical work shows that human judgment and understanding of context are important safety barriers in complex systems [15]. Hollnagel also shows that situational awareness and adaptability are important for dealing with unexpected events in real operations [16]. In infrastructure monitoring practice, many studies show that automated sensing and robotic inspection technologies improve efficiency. But human expertise is still very important. Ahmed et al. show that non-destructive bridge evaluation now uses robotic platforms and advanced sensors more often. But experts are still needed to judge how serious the damage is and to connect inspection results with engineering knowledge [17]. Sun et al. show that structural health monitoring becomes more reliable and easier to understand when HITL mechanisms are used. This is important for damage interpretation, risk assessment, and decision checking in uncertain situations [18]. At the same time, sole reliance on manual inspection has clear limitations. Large-scale infrastructure networks and high-frequency monitoring tasks impose significant cogni- tive and physical burdens on inspectors. Al-Sabbag et al. show that human-machine collaboration, supported by interactive visualization and mixed reality, can enhance in- spection effectiveness by enabling engineers to verify and correct automated detection observations [19]. From a broader perspective, Asad et al. argue that human-centric DTs should explicitly incorporate human perception, interaction, and decision-making capa- bilities to support effective collaboration between engineers and intelligent systems [20]. Recent overviews of DT applications in structural health monitoring further confirm that human expertise remains necessary for model calibration, anomaly interpretation, and maintenance planning in complex operational environments [21]. Human factors are a strength of traditional CI O&M practices. They are also an important part that future DTs and intelligent monitoring systems must consider. When human experience, situational understanding, and decision-making processes are included, the system becomes stronger. This forms the basic foundation for the proposed HAK- integrated O&M framework. 2.3. AI for Civil Infrastructure Operation and Maintenance (CI O&M) AI is now an important tool in CI O&M. Abstract: With rapid urbanization, the utilization of underground space has become an important part of infrastructure. However, the stability of underground spaces such as large caverns remains a key challenge in civil engineering throughout the lifecycle of a project. Traditional methods of stability assessment rely on static models and periodic monitoring and often fail to capture real-time changes in rock behavior, leading to potential safety risks and, in severe cases, even the collapse of underground infrastructure. To address this challenge, this study introduces a digital twin (DT) framework to improve stability assessments and monitor deformations in underground structures. The framework enables the continuous monitoring and adaptive optimization of rock support systems by combining real-time sensor data with virtual simulations. A five-dimensional DT framework comprises physical objects, virtual objects, service systems, DT data, and their interconnections. It incorporates six key modules, which are structure, geology, material, behavior, performance, and environment, to enhance the understanding of cavern stability. The framework is based on Industry Foundation Classes standards to ensure seamless data exchange, interoperability, and the standardized representation of geotechnical and structural data. A seven-step methodology is developed for this framework, encompassing geological assessment, virtual modeling, Building Information Modeling (BIM)-based de- sign, construction processes, real-time monitoring, and optimization strategies. To evaluate its effectiveness, the framework is applied to a case study, demonstrating improvements in deformation monitoring and rock support efficiency. The findings highlight the poten- tial of integrating DT with BIM to enhance safety, reliability, and long-term stability in underground construction projects. Keywords: digital twin (DT); building information modeling (BIM); underground cavern stability; real-time monitoring; rock support optimization 1. Introduction Building and maintaining large underground caverns is an important part of modern infrastructure, especially for projects like hydroelectric power plants, storage facilities, and transportation tunnels [1]. These caverns face challenging conditions, such as changes in rock properties, ground stress, and water pressure, which can affect their stability and long- term safety [2]. Geological investigations play a critical role in the design of underground caverns, as they provide essential insights into the rock mass conditions that influence Appl. Sci. 2025, 15, 4481 https://doi.org/10.3390/app15084481 Appl. Sci. 2025, 15, 4481 2 of 29 stability and excavation performance [3,4]. These investigations typically involve a combi- nation of field and laboratory techniques, such as geological mapping, core drilling, logging, geophysical surveys, and the testing of rock samples to evaluate properties like uniaxial compressive strength, tensile strength, and elastic modulus [5,6]. Understanding features such as discontinuities, in situ stress regimes, and groundwater behavior is also vital to assessing construction feasibility and selecting suitable support systems [7,8]. Ensuring the stability of these structures is essential because any failure can lead to serious problems, like loss of life, economic damage, and harm to the environment [9,10]. Traditionally, engi- neers have used static models and regular monitoring to assess stability during the design, construction, and operation phases [11]. These methods may not detect real-time changes in rock behavior during or after construction [12]. To overcome similar problems in other infrastructure projects, new technologies like DT and BIM are being adopted [13,14]. These tools can also improve the accuracy and efficiency of stability management in underground projects [15]. Digital twin technology, which involves creating a dynamic virtual model of a phys- ical system continuously updated with real-time data, has emerged as a powerful tool in recent years [16,17]. It holds significant potential for enhancing decision making and predictive analysis across various engineering disciplines [18,19]. Recently, there has been a lot of effort in the use of DT in civil engineering and its application across a range of infrastructure projects, such as bridges, buildings, and transportation systems [20,21]. It plays a crucial role in managing construction projects by creating a dynamic and interactive model that reflects the actual physical structure [22]. This allows engineers to continuously monitor conditions and optimize the stability of infrastructure projects in real time [ 23]. When combined with BIM, it provides a detailed digital overview of a facility’s physical and functional elements; the integration forms a comprehensive framework [24,25]. This approach enhances the ability to simulate, monitor, and optimize the construction projects throughout their entire lifecycle [26]. The use of DT in underground engineering, espe- cially for large caverns, is still in its early stages [ 27]. A major challenge in this field is effectively integrating real-time data from various sources, including geological surveys, laboratory experiments, and construction site sensors, into a unified virtual model [ 28]. This model must be capable of accurately predicting and responding to changes in rock mass behavior [29]. This challenge becomes even more complex due to the necessity for interoperability among various underground construction activities and data formats [30]. Ensuring smooth data exchange and effective collaboration among project stakeholders is essential to addressing this issue. Recent developments in information technology and data analytics have made it more practical to adopt digital twin-based frameworks in underground engineering [31,32]. How- ever, traditionally, underground construction projects rely heavily on hardcopy records, paper-based reports, and flat-file storage systems (e.g., spreadsheets, PDFs, and basic CAD drawings) [33]. Currently, BIM is primarily used for design and construction, offering benefits such as 3D visualization, clash detection, and improved coordination among stake- holders [34]. Some projects have adopted 4D (time) and 5D (cost) BIM for scheduling and cost estimation, but the lack of real-time data integration limits its full potential [35]. Unlike above-ground structures, underground environments present unique challenges, including difficulty in deploying Internet of Things (IoT) sensors for real-time monitoring, the absence of standardized frameworks for merging BIM with DTs, and the computational demands of processing large-scale geotechnical and sensor data [36]. There has been an effort to design optimization and simulation of Tunnel Boring Machines (TBMs) by inte- grating DT with Virtual Reality (VR) to streamline the assembly process and performance evaluation of Tunnel Boring Machine (TBM) tunnelling operations [37,38]. Recent research Appl. Sci. 2025, 15, 4481 3 of 29 has introduced a digital twin-driven blast-induced vibration optimization method aimed at enhancing blasting operation [39]. A digital twin-enabled decision support method for the proactive maintenance of Tunnel Electro-Mechanical Equipment (TEE), integrating BIM, IoT, and Semantic Web technologies to enhance real-time fault detection and predictive maintenance was developed [40]. However, typical large underground cavern construction projects that use drill-and-blast tunnelling methods still need attention. With the current advancements in real-time sensor networks, AI-driven analytics, and high-performance computing, there is great potential for adopting DT in underground cav- ern construction [41,42]. It can provide significant benefits for optimizing the construction and stability of underground structures [43]. One major advantage is the ability to develop a virtual model that simulates rock mass behavior under different conditions, offering valuable insights into potential risks and challenges during construction and operation [44]. Additionally, continuously updating the real-time information from the construction site enables proactive decision making and risk management, helping to prevent unexpected failures or project delays [ 45]. Despite the increasing adoption of BIM in underground construction, its integration with DT technology remains largely unexplored [46]. Existing research has primarily focused on BIM applications in design and construction, with limited studies addressing its transition into an operational digital twin capable of continuous mon- itoring, analysis, and optimization. Furthermore, the lack of standardized methodologies for integrating sensor networks, geotechnical monitoring, and real-time performance data with BIM models presents a significant challenge. Additionally, underground construction has fewer case studies demonstrating successful BIM-enabled DT, further slowing adoption. Addressing these limitations is crucial to advancing underground construction towards data-driven, real-time decision making. Furthermore, the introduction of Industry Founda- tion Classes (IFC) standards in underground engineering has been under the process of development for data exchange and improving interoperability [47–49]. It is necessary to explore the potential of DT integration and key challenges for a structured approach for seamless data connectivity in underground cavern construction. This research study develops a novel DT-driven framework that provides real-time feedback from the real world to a virtual BIM-based environment, enabling real-time decision making for optimizing stability assessment and rock support strategies. The proposed approach follows a seven-step DT-driven framework to integrate BIM, sensor- driven feedback loops, and predictive modeling, enabling real-time excavation progress tracking, as-built information management, and convergence monitoring based on an IFC-based data structure. It begins with geological assessments and laboratory testing, followed by BIM-based virtual modeling and numerical analysis for excavation and rock support design. Real-time sensor deformation data are continuously integrated to update the digital twin. The proposed DT framework is validated through a real case study of the Neelum Jhelum Hydropower Project powerhouse cavern construction, demonstrating its effectiveness in real-world underground construction. This research study advances data- driven decision making in underground infrastructure, contributing to the development of intelligent, resilient, and sustainable underground spaces. This approach will ultimately enhance underground cavern construction management and lifecycle stability. Background BIM reconstructions of engineering slopes using LiDAR point cloud data involve two critical phases: initial geometric parameter extraction of drainage channels through point cloud segmentation, followed by parametric BIM reconstruction using specialized software. This two- phase method ensures accurate digital representation of structural elements. 2.1. Related research Accurately identifying object boundaries within point cloud data is typically achieved through two primary methods: (1) directly seg - menting 3D point clouds, and (2) mapping 2D segmentation results into 3D space [ 20 ]. Deep learning has emerged as a dominant approach for both 2D and 3D semantic segmentation, offering advanced capabilities for object recognition and boundary delineation. These segmentation results can then be utilized in parametric modeling methods to automate the reconstruction of BIM models. 2.1.1. 3D Point Clouds Segmentation While pioneering 3D methods like PointNet [ 24 ] introduced permutation-invariant networks through multi-layer perceptrons and max pooling, subsequent advancements such as PointNet ++ [ 25 ] improved local feature capture through set abstraction. Modern archi - tectures including PointWeb [ 26 ] and RandLA-Net [ 27 ] address com - plex scene processing through context-aware aggregation and efficient sampling. Large-scale benchmarks like SemanticKITTI [ 28 ] and KITTI360 [ 29 ] have significantly advanced data-driven segmentation, with recent supervised learning approaches achieving notable success in multi-object segmentation for indoor and autonomous driving scenarios [ 30 , 31 ]. However, challenges persist in processing large-scale, un - structured point clouds efficiently [ 21 ], particularly for extracting slender elements like drainage channels. The alternative 2D projection approach, which converts 3D data into 2D depth maps, leverages mature image segmentation techniques to significantly reduce computational loads compared with direct 3D processing. 2.1.2. 2D Image Segmentation The evolution of 2D segmentation architectures has progressed through three distinct generations: (1) Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs) [ 32 ] or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [ 33 – 38 ] excel - ling in texture pattern recognition, (2) Vision Transformers (ViTs) [ 39 , 40 ] that capture long-range dependencies, and (3) foundation models like Segment Anything Model (SAM) [ 41 , 42 ] that enables zero- shot adaptation for downstream tasks. These methods have been widely applied in tasks related to construction and infrastructure maintenance, such as crack segmentation [ 43 , 44 ], road network segmentation [ 45 , 46 ] and in-door pipeline segmentation [ 20 , 21 ]. To preserve the connectivity of linear objects, some studies focus on refining imperfect road seg - mentation results to maintain their inherent connectivity [ 47 ]. Notably, most existing models are trained on datasets, such as ImageNet [ 48 ] for general vision tasks and Chest X-ray8 [ 49 ] for medical imaging, which significantly differ from point cloud-derived imagery. 2.1.3. Parametric Modeling Grasshopper and Dynamo have become pivotal tools in parametric design and cross-platform collaboration within engineering sectors. Their integration of Visual Programming Languages (VPLs) enables automated workflows and complex geometric manipulation [ 50 – 54 ]. These tools seamlessly connect conceptual design with technical execution through computational logic, supporting applications from generative design to data-driven optimization. For instance, Grass - hopper facilitates AI-enhanced workflows, such as converting 2D sketches into 3D structural models via deep learning [ 51 ], while Dy - namo streamlines BIM data interoperability, enabling efficient data transfer between Revit and external platforms like Excel for documen - tation [ 54 ]. Their adaptability extends to advanced computational sce - narios, including LiDAR-based point cloud processing for scan to BIM [ 50 , 53 , 55 ] and reinforcement learning-driven spatial optimization [ 52 ]. Custom workflows are necessary to address slope-specific challenges, such as the integration of irregular geometries and domain-specific analysis. 2.2. First, computa - tional bottlenecks persist in processing city-scale LiDAR point clouds, where conventional 3D segmentation models struggle to efficiently extract narrow elements like drainage channels from millions of un - structured points. Methodology The proposed framework for digitizing massive engineered slopes comprises four principal components ( Fig. 2 ): (1) domain knowledge- incorporated image dataset generation, (2) deep learning-based image segmentation, (3) Grasshopper-based fully automated BIM reconstruc - tion, and (4) digital twin platform interactive visualization. This study employs an image-based deep learning method to delin - eate slope surface drainage channels. The initial phase involves gener - ating depth images from LiDAR point clouds, incorporating domain knowledge to optimally distinguish the characteristics of slope surface drainage channels. Slope surface drainage channels are then delineated manually as labels for segmentation. The segmentation phase implements customized CNN model Drain - Net to address the challenge of detecting local linear features while preserving global contextual awareness. DrainNet integrates directional convolution blocks (horizontal and vertical) for local feature extraction, dilated convolution blocks for expanded receptive fields, and channel attention modules for better generalization on small datasets. DrainNet achieves optimal performance in linear structure identification, partic - ularly for slope surface drainage channel delineation. Analysis of failure cases in Drainage Channel delineation As illustrated in Fig. 14 , failure cases in image segmentation fall into two main categories: overestimation and underestimation. Both occur in areas where the point cloud data is sparse or vacant, leading to unex - pected topographic variations that disrupt curvature-based analysis. This results in either false positives resembling drainage channels or large blanks in the image. From a model design perspective, such sparsity can amplify noise in feature extraction, causing the network to misinterpret subtle edges or fail to connect fragmented structures. To address these challenges, a twofold strategy is adopted. First, during dataset annotation, incomplete or discontinuous drainage channels are manually labeled as continuous structures to help the model recognize gaps due to data sparsity ( Fig. 14 ). Second, DrainNet with perpendicular 1D convolutions and channel attention is developed to better capture sparse drainage channel features and improve robustness against incomplete data. Nevertheless, data completeness remains crucial, as it fundamentally affects segmentation accuracy and subsequent modeling. 7.2. Limitations and future work The method proposed in this research is suitable for drainage chan - nels on slopes that are exposed and uncovered, which can be detected through surface curvature analysis and subsequently segmented using image-based methods. The available data from SIS provides only approximate cross-sectional dimensions, thus empirically determined parameters are applied uniformly for modeling purpose. Using the parametric modeling method, the creation of channel section can be easily generalized when detailed parameters are available. The current segmentation model has limitations in accurately resolving channel width parameters. Alternatively, channel geometry could be derived by converting deep learning segmentation results into shapefiles, though this approach requires further research. Notably, the application of closure operations for maintaining hydraulic connectivity is a simplified way. This post-processing step, while effective, is a heuristic simplifi - cation that operates under the assumption that all discontinuities are segmentation errors. Consequently, it may erroneously connect chan - nels that are geomorphologically distinct or introduce non-physical al - terations to the channel ’ s shape. The performance of this operation is also contingent upon a relatively complete initial segmentation. Future work should explore more sophisticated approach to establish connec - tivity, which would more robustly handle complex drainage networks and reduce the reliance on such geometric heuristics. Introduction According to the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, there are more than 6400 high-rise buildings over 150 m built worldwide by March 1, 2023 [1] . High-rise buildings serve crucial urban functions, including hospitals and office spaces. Any sudden occurrence of vibra - tion anomalies in these structures can impair their functionality, resulting in substantial direct and indirect economic losses [2] . Several high-rise buildings have experienced vibration anomalies that differ from those caused by typhoons or earthquakes. These vibra - tions are more sudden and usually cause widespread panic. It is usually impossible to find the cause of the vibration in the first place, and subsequent investigations result in prolonged closures of the buildings, leading to significant economic losses. For example, on May 18, 2021, the Saige Building ( Fig. 1 a) in Shenzhen encountered a vibration anomaly caused by wind-induced vortex resonance of the top-floor mast [3] . After nearly four months of closure, the building resumed operation, but the owner suffered a net profit loss of approximately 55 million RMB that year [4] . Similarly, on February 7, 2013, occupants on the 20th floor of the Information Building in Shanghai experienced intermittent abnormal shaking, prompting an emergency evacuation. Nonetheless, the cause of the building's shaking remains unknown [5] . Additionally, on September 5, 2013, a 50-story office building in Nanjing underwent several intense up-and-down shaking events within an hour due to construction work involving a road roller at a nearby construction site [6] . Moreover, on July 5, 2011, TechnoMart ( Fig. 1 b), a 39-story shopping mall building in Seoul, experienced abnormal vertical shaking lasting 10 min due to crowd movement inside the building, leading to a two-day evacuation of residents [7,8] . Lastly, on January 20, 2022, a 33-story high-rise residential building in Seoul encountered structural resonance caused by group dancing within the building after two months of investigation and lockdown [9] . High-rise buildings typically exhibit relatively low lateral stiffness and damping ratios, making them susceptible to external excitations such as wind loads, crowd activities, and mechanical vibrations. Struc - tural vibrations induced by earthquakes or typhoons exhibit distinct characteristics that can be quickly identified to prevent unnecessary panic. Vibrations caused by equipment operation or human activities within the 1 – 10 Hz frequency range not only coincide with many high- order vibration modes of tall buildings but also fall within the sensitive * Corresponding author. The inability to promptly determine the cause and location of such vibration sources often leads to public panic events. The aforementioned vibration anomaly events of high-rise buildings in Shenzhen, Seoul, and Nanjing demonstrate that vibration sources can be located at the top, middle, and base of the buildings. Yet, in each case, the inability to immediately identify the vibration source led to building evacuations and months-long investigations before resuming normal operations, resulting in substantial economic losses. If a vibration anomaly can be detected and localized while still weak (below the human perception threshold), timely elimination of the source can prevent such events from escalating into public incidents. In the field of structural health monitoring (SHM) for high-rise buildings, extensive research has been conducted on operational modal analysis (OMA) [11 – 14] and damage detection and localization [15 – 17] . However, limited research has been reported on the detection and localization of vibration anomalies specifically for high-rise build - ings. Existing research on vibration source localization primarily focuses on seismic localization, occupant localization, and pipeline leakage localization. For instance, travel-time inversion was proposed to deter - mine earthquake hypocenter coordinates [18] . The double-difference earthquake location method was proposed to minimize the residuals between observed and theoretical travel-time differences of seismic pairs at each station [19] . Schloemann et al. [20] introduced a time difference of arrival (TDoA)-based method to localize impact sources within buildings. Mirshekari et al. [21] proposed an occupant localiza - tion approach using TDoA and footstep-induced floor vibrations. Lah et al. [22] introduced a pipeline leakage localization method by esti - mating TDoA through vibration signal correlation. Wang and Zhu [23] developed a two-stage impact source localization method based on the time of arrival (ToA) of impact waves recorded from multiple vibration monitoring points. The localization accuracy was validated through numerical simulations and field experiments conducted at a construc - tion site with ongoing impact piling work. These algorithms were designed for transient pulse excitations, whereas high-rise building vibration anomalies are typically non-pulse- like and persistent, limiting the applicability of TDoA/ToA methods. Consequently, vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings remain underexplored. In most SHM applications, the SHM system is designed based on the cloud computing pattern, where all sensor data are transmitted to a cloud server for centralized processing [24,25] . While this pattern is feasible in wired SHM systems, it has been found to be challenging and inappropriate for wireless sensor networks (WSNs) when it comes to transmitting large time-history data [26] . On one hand, implementing a WSN based on the cloud computing pattern would result in high energy consumption and reduced battery life of sensor nodes due to the continuous wireless connection to the cloud server and the significant data transmission requirements for time-history vibration data. On the other hand, the cloud computing pattern is not suitable for real-time detection of vibration anomalies due to the inherent delay in data transmission and cloud analysis. Edge computing is a computing paradigm that performs computation at the network edge, which has gained significant research interest in the field of SHM. For instance, Wei et al. [27] proposed a multi-resource computing offload algorithm for optimizing energy consumption in mobile edge computing, resulting in more than 12 % of energy saving. Zonzini et al. [28] introduced an innovative extreme edge data compression strategy tailored for low-power and low-cost SHM appli - cations. Experimental results from wind turbine monitoring demon - strated that this strategy reduced the energy consumption of a sensor node by over 16 %. Additionally, Abner et al. [29] presented three strategies for conserving battery power in wireless monitoring sensors for infrastructures using edge computing, significantly enhancing the sensor's battery lifespan and reducing the frequency of on-site hardware maintenance. Based on the above research, edge computing shows promise in addressing challenges related to response time, battery life, and bandwidth costs [30] . However, due to limited computing and memory resources, as well as network bandwidth constraints, edge computing faces challenges in achieving satisfactory performance when dealing with complex problems. Owing to the complementary advantages and disadvantages of cloud computing and edge computing, an increasingly common approach is to integrate them in real-world applications [31] . This approach is known as edge-cloud collaboration (ECC), which combines the low latency characteristics of edge computing with the powerful computing capa - bilities of cloud computing [32] . Early explorations of ECC have been conducted in both academia and industry. For example, Yu et al. [33] designed an ECC framework for industrial anomaly detection, where cloud algorithms are responsible for extracting and optimizing anomaly diagnosis rules, while edge algorithms detect anomalies based on these rules. This method significantly reduces network load and computa - tional pressure on the cloud. Gang et al. [34] proposed an ECC frame - work for electric load monitoring, which performs processing at the edge and optimization in the cloud. Ding et al. [35] introduced an ECC framework for healthcare monitoring that enables lesion detection at the edge and high-performance modeling in the cloud. As observed, current ECC frameworks primarily operate in the mode of edge detection with cloud-based dataset training or modeling. However, no reports have been made on edge-cloud collaborative intelligence for anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings. The aforementioned edge and clouding computing can help to ac - quire, process and analysis data from physical world. When combined with more complicated simulation and visualization models as the dig - ital replica of the real world entities, the concept of digital twin can be involved. A digital twin represents a cyber-physical system that enables visualization, modeling, simulation, analysis, prediction, and optimi - zation [36] . It has found applications in various civil engineering fields, including buildings [37 – 40] , infrastructure [41,42] , and construction sites [43,44] . The digital twin is considered a promising technology with the potential to transform the construction industry and provide solu - tions to various challenges [45] . Furthermore, Jiang et al. [46] argued that technologies such as digital twin, building information modeling (BIM), and cyber-physical systems (CPS) are not mutually exclusive; instead, they can mutually reinforce civil engineering digitalization. Therefore, the digital twin represents a broader concept that can be integrated with edge-cloud collaborative computing, BIM models, and simulation models to establish a comprehensive digital system for enhanced management and disaster prevention of civil structures. Therefore, an edge-cloud collaborative digital twin framework is presented for the purpose of vibration anomaly detection and localiza - tion in high-rise buildings. Discussion and limitations This study presents an integrated wireless smart sensor system with edge-cloud computing algorithms for vibration monitoring in high-rise buildings, featuring three key innovations. First, we developed an edge-cloud collaborative framework that distributes vibration detection algorithms to edge nodes, which reduces cloud computing workload and data transmission requirements while enabling near real-time vibration source localization and visualization. Second, we designed a compact, highly integrated wireless sensor that combines high-precision vibration measurement (0.2 μ g/(Hz) 1/2 noise density) with onboard edge computing capabilities (Linux-based Python environment), achieves less than 20 μ s time synchronization through a novel LoRa-based chain network, and enables direct 4 G cloud connectivity without requiring gateway nodes. Third, we established a digital twin-based vibration source localization methodology employing an updated flexural-shear MDOF model for building simulation, performing dynamic analysis under floor-specific excitations, and implementing SAF vector matching between simulated and measured responses to identify vibration sources. This study demonstrates the feasibility of an edge-cloud collabora - tive framework and proposes a novel vibration source localization method. However, three key limitations warrant discussion to guide future research directions: (1) The current SAF vector comparison method achieves only zonal- level localization because SAF vectors exhibit similar patterns when excitations are applied to adjacent floors. This limitation underscores the need for more precise floor-specific identification techniques. (2) The framework currently supports only single-frequency steady- state excitations. Extending it to multi-source and non-stationary vi - bration scenarios will require further theoretical and experimental development. Fig. 23. F: Digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system for seamless worker-robot collaboration in construction Xiao Lin , Ziyang Guo , Xinxiang Jin , Hongling Guo * Department of Construction Management, Tsinghua University, Beijing, China ARTICLE INFO Keywords: Worker-robot collaboration Digital twin Safety monitoring Construction robot ABSTRACT Worker-robot collaboration (WRC) has emerged as a transformative approach to augmenting the productivity of the construction industry. However, the development of a safety monitoring method or system for stopping robot operations in emergency is imperative, especially for seamless WRC on site. This paper presents a digital twin- enabled safety monitoring system for seamless WRC on site, characterized by its comprehensive perception of dynamic entities and dynamic calculation of protective separation distances during seamless WRC. The effec - tiveness of the proposed system is substantiated through a series of experiments, the result demonstrates its proficiency in mitigating collisions during robot operation in both static and dynamic WRC scenarios. The system achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 frames per second, an average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and a positional perception error of 0.09 m. It not only provides a practical tool for the implementation of seamless WRC on site, but also offers valuable insights for future WRC research. 1. Introduction The construction industry has shown a keen interest in robotic technology to address issues such as labour shortage, low productivity, and poor working condition. Various automated construction robot systems have been developed [ 1 ], however, challenges arise when these systems are deployed in a highly dynamic and complex construction environment, leading to reduced precision and efficiency. Worker-robot collaboration (WRC) has been recognized as a promising method by leveraging the complementary skills of humans and machines to enhance the on-site usability of construction robots [ 2 ]. The predominant form of WRC in construction currently relies on remote control methods [ 3 ]. However, closer collaboration, both spatially and temporally, can transform the role of human workers from robot operators to teammates, enhancing the productivity and flexibility of on-site WRC [ 4 ]. Despite these benefits, safety concerns remain a significant barrier to seamless WRC in construction [ 5 ]. Since workers and construction robots share a same operational space in seamless WRC, physical risks arise due to planned or unexpected contact caused by the movement of either party [ 6 ]. When workers need to collaborate closely with robots to complete tasks, physical risks in WRC can further increase [ 7 ]. Therefore, an effective safety monitoring system is essen - tial for achieving seamless WRC in construction. Despite its critical importance, implementing a safety monitoring system on construction sites presents significant challenges. The un - structured and dynamic nature of construction scenarios may decrease the efficiency and precision of the safety monitoring system [ 8 ]. Traditional safety monitoring methods in industrial WRC, such as using physical or virtual fences to spatially separate robots and workers, are impractical. These methods tend to fragment a continuous workspace, reduce operational efficiency, and fail to adapt to rapidly changing construction environment. Therefore, there is a need for an advanced safety monitoring system with fine-grained monitoring capabilities and spatial flexibility that can be easily integrated into construction robots to support safe and seamless WRC on site. This research proposes a digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system (SMS) for seamless WRC on construction sites. The contributions of this research are two-fold. First, it introduces a digital twin (DT)- based system capable of comprehensively perceiving and integrating the spatial information of workers, construction robots and construction materials during WRC, and establishing their approximate geometric occupation representations for spatial collision monitoring. Second, it proposes a rapid separation distance (SD) and a dynamic protective separation distance (PSD) calculation method for real-time safety monitoring in seamless WRC. WRC in construction Combining the hierarchical structure of construction works proposed by Bernold [ 9 ], with the Level of Robotic Automation (LoRA) defined by Beer et al. [ 10 ], it establishes a robust theoretical foundation for cate - gorizing WRC in construction. Based on the work hierarchy at which WRC occurs, the collaborative system can be classified into three types: Division Collaboration (DC), Sequence Collaboration (SC), and Task Collaboration (TC) ( Table 1 ). Each collaboration approach involves construction robots with different activity capabilities and correspond - ing LoRA that align with their designated collaboration roles. DC focuses on appropriate labour division between workers and ro - bots, such as task allocation [ 11 , 12 ] and scheduling planning [ 13 , 14 ]. Construction robots in DC are usually assumed to have a high LoRA (Levels 7–10) and can autonomously execute assigned tasks consisting of complex processes similar to workers. Through adequate labour divi - sion, workers and robots perform tasks independently in terms of time or space, resulting in clear temporal and spatial boundaries regarding their operation space [ 8 ]. Correspondingly, safety risks are minimized because there is minimal shared workspace between humans and robots, as well as physical risks. SC involves a series of subtasks executed in a predetermined process to achieve the collaboration objective, with each subtask requiring specific skills from either workers or robots [ 15 ]. In SC, human workers typically handle the more fragile and dynamic subtasks, while robots undertake those with high physical demands. Related research focuses on designing optimal processes to maximize the full potential of human- robot collaboration. For example, MitterBerger et al. [ 2 ] developed a wood framework construction method, where robots hold and position wooden beams and workers then tie them together with ropes. Con - struction activities involving the installation and delivery of construc - tion materials are a key focus of SC research [ 16 ]. For instance, Wu et al. [ 17 ] analyzed the efficiency of bricklaying teams, where robots lay bricks supplied by workers. In SC, robots’ actions may need to be planned or authorized by human workers [ 18 ], but be executed auton - omously by robots, demonstrating a middle LoRA in Levels 4–6. Nevertheless, the autonomy of robots and complex spatial interaction increases physical risks in WRC. In TC, human workers and construction robots jointly complete a single challenging task, which involves the highest level of human-robot interaction (HRI). The task is typically characterized by the high de - mand of both physical and cognitive efforts, exceeding the sense and plan capabilities of current construction robots as well as the physical ability of human workers. Therefore, human workers usually act as operators and control construction robots manually or remotely through HRI devices, such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR) and force interaction devices [ 19 ]. For example, Chen et al. [ 20 ] presented a WRC approach for the sorting of construction wastes, where workers use AR devices to guide a robot based on their judgement of potential waste materials. Given the low LoRA (Levels 1–3) of construction robots in TC, physical risks are inherently controlled as robots are under human supervision. In practical applications, WRC is usually based on hybrid configu - rations. For instance, a robot only possessing motion capability might initially collaborate with a worker to form a human-robot team for TC, thus engaging with another worker in process-level SC. Such hybrid WRC strategies become particularly prevalent when construction ro - bots’ LoRA remains insufficient to support their independent partici - pation in higher-level collaborative activities. The operational compromise enables robotic systems to contribute to advanced con - struction operations while compensating for current technological lim - itations. However, the hybridity inevitably introduces systemic safety concerns across robotic applications - irrespective of their designated roles in WRC or attained LoRA levels. This further underscores the critical need for developing adaptive safety monitoring systems in WRC. 2.2. Safety monitoring method for WRC Safety monitoring methods are pivotal in enhancing robotic safety [ 16 ]. Unlike industrial robots that are typically mounted on static bases, construction robots are usually mobile, comprising mobile platforms, kinematic structure, task-specific end-effectors, and integrated sensors [ 1 ]. The foundational architecture, first established by Shimizu Site Robot [ 21 ], remains fundamentally retained by modern construction robots. While dedicated safety standards for construction robots remain under development, ANSI/RIA R15.08 [ 22 ] provides critical normative guidance as the first safety standard for industrial mobile robots (IMR). Particularly, its classification of Type C IMR, defined as “mobile plat - forms integrated with manipulator attachments”, is a configuration structurally analogous to modern construction robots. The standard mandates that manipulators mounted on mobile platforms comply with ISO 10218 [ 23 ] requirements, which specifies four principal collabo - rative methodologies: Safety-Related Monitoring Stop (SRMS), Hand Guidance (HG), Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM), and Power and Force Limiting (PFL). The subsequent publication of ISO 15066 [ 24 ] significantly advances WRC safety frameworks by providing detailed design guidelines for safe WRC methods. Therefore, different safety monitoring methods can be designed based on WRC methods adapted in production. SRMS demands that robotic operations are conducted at a pre - determined distance from human workers, commonly achieved through the implementation of physical or virtual barriers to demarcate zones separating humans and robots. While effective and widely used in in - dustrial environments, the method has been criticized for creating spatial segmentation and may potentially undermine on-site produc - tivity [ 8 ]. The HG method facilitates teaching and programming robots through direct physical interaction [ 25 ], however, SRMS must be the prerequisite for HG. SSM defines separation distance (SD) and protective separation distance (PSD) based on the speed of humans and robots, dictating that robotic actions are permissible when SD exceeds PSD. Since SSM requires simultaneous detection of the spatial position and speed of both humans and robots, the corresponding safety monitoring method requires higher sensing resources. PFL safeguards human workers from physical harm by limiting the force output of robots during contact [ 26 ]. Therefore, SSM stands out as the most appropriate and adaptable method for ensuring safe WRC on site. With the development of sensing technologies, SSM is becoming increasingly cost-effective and feasible. The key of SSM is to calculate SD and PSD according to the spatial motion information of both robots and collaborating workers, which requires advanced robot intelligence. For monitoring human bodies within collaborative workspace, sensors with three-dimensional perception capabilities, such as light detection and ranging (LiDAR) [ 27 ] and depth cameras [ 28 ], are commonly employed. For seamless WRC, limb-level monitoring becomes imperative for accurate SD sensing. The effectiveness of SD sensing is heavily influenced by how obstacles are represented. For instance, Lucci et al. [ 26 ] utilized depth cameras for the limb-level detection of human workers, but the collision representation relied on point clouds, which are less versatile than surface-based representations like triangle meshes. Similarly, Rose - nstrauch et al. [ 29 ] used Kinect V2 for human body detection, with a simplified representation consisting of joint keypoints without collision volumes. Li et al. [ 30 ] also adopted joint keypoints for SD calculations. However, in close WRC scenarios, keypoints may not always represent the closest points on a worker ’ s body relative to a robot, potentially leading to inaccurate estimation for SD. Surface-based representations for both workers and robots can provide more accurate SD measure - ments. Nevertheless, overly complex representations, such as the Skin - ned Multi-Person Linear (SMPL) model [ 25 ], need substantial computing resources, thus hindering real-time performance. Simplified geometric representations, including spheres, boxes, and capsules [ 31 , 32 ], offer a viable alternative, but the level of detail (LoD) must be carefully designed to strike a balance between computational efficiency and representation accuracy. While ISO 15066 provides calculation methods for PSD, obtaining necessary value for calculation, particularly the speed of workers and robots, renders it challenging. A common approach is to set a constant value for workers ’ speed to estimate PSD [ 33 – 35 ], where a maximum speed assumption for workers is often established for safety. However, the assumption also constrains robots ’ workspace, potentially reducing the efficiency and flexibility of WRC [ 36 ]. Besides, current research for safety monitoring concentrates on WRC in manufacturing, where robots usually operate on a tabletop scale, using stationary arms to handle small objects. However, in construction sites, components and objects such as drywall panels manipulated by robots may have a large size and should not be overlooked [ 18 ]. Conclusion Seamless WRC has considerable potential for enhancing productivity in construction but must be supported by adequate safety monitoring methods or systems. Although SSM is deemed suitable for WRC on site, the practicality of existing methods is limited due to that they are designed especially for industrial WRC that is less dynamic and complex. To bridge the gap, this paper introduced a digital twin-enabled safety monitoring system. The proposed SMS offers the following contribu - tions: 1) a comprehensive perception method for WRC entities, including construction workers, robots, and materials; 2) a geometric solid-based virtual representation method for WRC entities that balances sensing accuracy and efficiency; 3) a rapid calculation method for SD, and 4) a dynamic calculation method for PSD. The above-mentioned physical entities, their virtual twins, and relevant functions are inte - grated within a digital twin system. Experiments have shown that the proposed SMS achieves an average monitoring rate of 9.8 FPS, an average reaction latency of 0.177 s, and an SD perceiving error of 0.09 m. This system has been validated in both static and dynamic-conflict scenarios, demonstrating its safety-guarding capabilities. The future directions for further enhancing the proposed SMS are discussed as follows. First, this system will be validated on a broader range of construction robots beyond the mobile manipulator configu - ration, such as humanoid robots. Second, it will continuously benefit from more advanced perception methods for human workers and con - struction materials with less latency and higher accuracy. Finally, the incorporation of predictive methods for workers ’ and robots ’ motion will further enhance the performance of PSD calculation, empowering a more robust and flexible WRC on site. Introduction As a disruptive innovation, Modular Integrated Construction (MiC) is increasingly replacing traditional methods, offering benefits such as improved quality, simplified management, and shorter project du- rations (Pan and Hon, 2020; Zheng et al., 2020). During the MiC construction process, efficient module stacking is critical for timely assembly, high construction quality, and safety (Shan et al., 2019; Zhai et al., 2019). In practice, module stacking still depends on manual ob- servation and the expertise of crane operators (Zhu et al., 2022), which gives rise to several critical challenges. First, crane operators often exercise delayed control due to insufficient real-time information for accurate perception of module status, thereby increasing the likelihood of safety incidents, stacking errors, and module collisions (Kim and Chi, 2021). Second, the lack of timely and integrated information sharing among stakeholders causes delays and fragmentation, which hinder coordination and perception across the project team (Zhao et al., 2022). These limitations further result in module accumulation in buffer zones, thereby disrupting upstream production and timely delivery (Yue Zhai and Huang, 2017; Zhong et al., 2017). Thus, these challenges raise higher requirements to facilitate module stacking precision, including but not limited to: (1) comprehensive module stacking perception to replace workers’ observation; (2) efficient information transmission and sharing among stakeholders to avoid information silos; (3) pre- dictive stacking guidance to refrain from delayed control of crane operators (Yue Zhai and Huang, 2017; Salama et al., 2017). Collectively, these requirements highlight a central need: enabling stakeholders to both perceive ongoing conditions and anticipate forth- coming events. Therefore, global perceptual monitoring supported by real-time simulation of the stacking process and trajectory prediction is required. Precise simulation of the module stacking process demands real-time data collection and processing, highly accurate dynamic mod- eling, and extensive information sharing and collaboration. In contrast, Digital Twin (DT) technology provides a robust solution by replicating physical processes in a virtual environment and integrating sensors for real-time monitoring and prediction (Zhou et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Within the DT framework, module stacking is redefined to include real-time visibility, cyber–physical connectivity, and traceability. Furthermore, a DT is incorporated into collaborative platforms that facilitate access to and sharing of module information among stakeholders, enhancing information synchroniza- tion. Consequently, this paper aims to leverage the advantages of DT to achieve comprehensive visibility and traceability at module stacking sites through real-time data collection and positioning. Nevertheless, addressing delayed control and achieving predictive guidance requires further development of precise methods for module pose estimation and trajectory prediction within the DT framework. Computer vision-based methods have been proposed by researchers and practitioners (Zhang et al., 2019). Yang et al. (2023) suggested surveillance cameras for environmental perception and worker trajec- tory prediction to boost construction safety. Similarly, Cheng et al. (2022) introduced a stereo-vision technique for aligning precast con- crete bridge components. Considering that vision-based methods often fail to capture accurate 3D spatial relationships, Xie et al. (2025) proposed a framework that leverages point cloud data to detect human– object interactions on construction sites. However, on-site practices re- veal that these methods struggle in dynamic and variable construction environments, poorly addressing challenges like cluttered backgrounds, wind, vibrations, fluctuating lighting, and limited camera angles (Kop- sida and Brilakis, 2020; Omar and Nehdi, 2016; Liu et al., 2023). In contrast, Internet of Things (IoT) sensors can capture high precision, multisource stacking data in real-time, offering superior environmental adaptability and reliability. Combined with IoT devices, high fidelity virtual models in the DT framework can reflect the dynamic state, be- havior, and changes of the physical stacking system in a comprehensive perspective (Zhou et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Current methods for predicting trajectories with IoT sensors pri- marily use temporal models to analyze historical motion data se- quences (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023; Zhang and Ge, 2022). These sequential relationships expose the temporal continuity and directional trends of motions, enabling more reliable future trajectory predictions. However, in complex spatial movements like module ro- tation or flipping, reliance solely on temporal relationships for the module trajectory prediction proves challenging. This is mainly because such movements entail multi-dimensional displacement and spatial pose adjustments. This difficulty often requires deep learning networks with advanced capabilities for handling complex nonlinear problems. Recently, deep learning models have achieved outstanding perfor- mance in multiple domains (Shang et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). Specifically, in tasks that involve extracting complex spatiotemporal features, Zhang et al. (2024) designed a multi-attention network based on spatio-temporal feature fusion to predict pedestrian crossing intention. Ali et al. (2025) proposed a spatio-temporal graph convolutional recurrent network with multi-scale feature extraction and dual attention mechanisms, achieving superior long-term urban traffic prediction on real-world datasets. Wang et al. (2025) recently proposed a large language model (LLM)-driven probabilistic trajectory prediction framework, which integrates spatio-temporal encoding with normalizing flows to model uncertainty and complex motion dynamics. Although these methods struggle to represent the complex spatial posture changes during the module installation process, they show that accurate pose prediction of modules depends crucially on effective modeling of spatial relationships and temporal dependencies among trajectory points, particularly in complex movements. Spatial structures elucidate the distribution and interrelation of points in space, vital for deciphering movement patterns. Consequently, this paper introduces a DT-enabled module stacking system (DTPTS) with a novel network, PosTraFormer, which enhances controllability by modeling the com- plex spatial posture changes during the module installation process through multi-layer graph convolutional networks (GCNs), and by estimating posture and predicting trajectories through the integration of spatiotemporal modeling. The lifecycle of MiC is illustrated in Fig. 1. Initially, dynamic spatio- temporal data from assets involved in the stacking process (such as cranes, workers, and modules) are captured using Ultra Wide Bands (UWBs) and Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) for enhanced position- ing accuracy in MiC environments. All real-time data and intrinsic characteristics, including geometry and semantics, are updated to the Smart Industry Foundation Classes (SIFCs) via the Mobile Gateway Operating System (MGOS) for efficient data management. DTPTS en- ables real-time automatic mapping of stacking activities and unified information sharing among stakeholders, while PosTraFormer analyzes key stacking factors for enhanced trajectory prediction. Our primary contributions are as follows: (1) A DT platform based on IoT is proposed for real-time perception, pose estimation, and bidirectional interoperability between the cyber and physical modules in MiC stacking scenarios. (2) A novel network called PosTraFormer is proposed to model the spatial–temporal structure of trajectory points to predict future motion trajectories, which is embedded into the DT platform to prevent potential structural collision and misaligned stacking. (3) Comparative studies with the current state-of-the-art trajectory prediction models are conducted to validate the feasibility of the system and the effectiveness of the proposed method. The setup comprises a physical stacking site, a digital stacking site, and an intelligent data management system. The physical stacking site mainly includes a buffer zone for storing modules, a building under construction, cranes, mod- ules, workers, gateway, and base stations. The buffer zone measures 0.7 m in length and 0.35 m in width, capable of storing up to 16 modules. The building model follows the mainstream Podium System construction method, standing 1.35 m tall with 15 floors, including a podium section at the bottom and a framework section above. Base stations are strategically deployed around the building. Each room is modular and demountable, closely resembling real construction instal- lation scenarios. Additionally, every module embeds intelligent RFID tags, UWBs, and IMUs. The smart crane has an operating radius of 1 m, a maximum payload of 10 kg, and a lifting speed of 0.1 m/s. A gateway is mounted on the crane hook to capture real-time data from modules and transmit control commands to the tower crane. The digital stacking site is developed by WebGL, which serves as the DT of the aforementioned physical assets. To facilitate unified management of di- verse structural data, a ‘‘Trinity’’ platform is developed, encompassing intelligent modules, workers, and cranes. The entire trajectory from hoisting the module in the buffer zone to placing it at the designated position is sampled at 2 Hz. A sliding window of fixed length is used to trim the trajectory starting from every alternate time step of the original trajectory (specifically, 5 s, 10 frames). Each trimmed trajectory is divided into two parts: the historical trajectory (input) and the predicted trajectory (output or la- bel), totaling 1200 trimmed trajectories. In the experiment, the dataset is split into training and evaluation sets in a 7:3 ratio. The training set is utilized to optimize the parameters of the trajectory prediction model, while the evaluation set monitors the convergence of the model. Additionally, the module storage and movement status, construction progress of each building layer, and occupancy status of the tower crane are automatically updated at the same timestamp during this process. Dynamic activity data of workers, such as their movement paths and actions on the construction site, are captured via smart wristbands and helmets. All the aforementioned data is integrated into the SIFC database and subjected to data calibration and synchronization to ensure consistency and accuracy among various data sources. To address possible jitter and anomalies in the collected data, Kalman filtering is employed for smoothing and noise removal. Polynomial interpolation is utilized to handle missing values. Finally, to standardize the scale of position and orientation data for model training, the data is normalized. 5.1.2. Data processing In this section, we describe the process of augmenting the lifting dataset through data augmentation techniques to ensure that various possible lifting scenarios are covered during model training, including normal lifting trajectories as well as anomalous situations (e.g., sen- sor malfunctions). As a result, the dataset size was expanded from a few hundred trajectories to 6000 trajectories. The data augmentation methods primarily include two techniques: using Time-Series Genera- tive Adversarial Network (TimeGAN) to simulate real lifting data and injecting anomalous trajectory points into certain trajectory data using noise. Real normal stacking data simulation. TimeGAN is used to gen- erate samples that resemble the distribution of real lifting data. The generator of TimeGAN learns the temporal patterns of the real data, thus simulating new trajectory data. This approach effectively enhances the diversity of the dataset, especially when the original data is limited. Specifically, we input real lifting data into the TimeGAN model and train the generator to produce trajectory point sequences similar to the real data. After several iterations, the generator can output a large amount of new data that is temporally similar to the real data. To validate the effectiveness of the generated data, we compared the distribution of the generated data with the real data using principal component analysis. Fig. 8 demonstrates that the generated data’s dis- tribution in the two-dimensional space closely matches the distribution of the real data. These generated data not only increase the sample size but also enhance the diversity of the dataset, helping to improve the model’s generalization ability, particularly when dealing with unknown lifting situations.
