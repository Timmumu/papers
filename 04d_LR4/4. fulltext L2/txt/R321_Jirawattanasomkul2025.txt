This study developed a digital twin (DT) and structural health monitoring (SHM) system for a balanced can- 
tilever bridge, utilizing advanced measurement techniques to enhance accuracy. Vibration and dynamic strain 
measurements were obtained using accelerometers and piezo-resistive strain gauges, capturing low-magnitude 
dynamic strains during operational vibrations. 3D-LiDAR scanning and Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity (UPV) tests cap- 
tured the bridge’s as-is geometry and modulus of elasticity. The resulting detailed 3D point cloud model revealed 
the structure’s true state and highlighted discrepancies between the as-designed and as-built conditions. Dynamic 
properties, including modal frequencies and shapes, were extracted from the strain and acceleration measure- 
ments, providing critical insights into the bridge’s structural behavior. The neutral axis depth, indicating stress 
distribution and potential damage, was accurately determined. Good agreement between vibration measurement 
data and the as-is model results validated the reliability of the digital twin model. Dynamic strain patterns and 
neutral axis parameters showed strong correlation with model predictions, serving as sensitive indicators of local 
damage. The baseline digital twin model and measurement results establish a foundation for future bridge in- 
spections and investigations. This study demonstrates the eﬀectiveness of combining digital twin technology with 
ﬁeld measurements for real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance, ensuring the sustainability and safety 
of the bridge infrastructure, thereby enhancing its overall resilience to operational and environmental stressors. 
1. Introduction 
Ensuring the safety, longevity, and functionality of bridges, partic- 
ularly as they age within crucial transportation networks, is a critical 
aspect of infrastructure management focused on enhancing their overall 
resilience to various stressors [ 1 ]. Structural health monitoring (SHM) 
plays a key role in achieving this by continuously assessing structural 
integrity and performance, enabling early detection of issues such as 
cracks, corrosion, and unusual vibrations that could lead to catastrophic 
failures [ 2–4 ]. A proactive SHM approach facilitates timely maintenance 
and repairs, mitigating risks associated with accidents, downtime, and 
excessive costs. However, traditional inspection methods —such as vi- 
sual surveys and single-point measurements —often fail to capture the 
high-resolution, real-time data required for eﬀective long-term monitor-

simulations to create a dynamic, continuously updated representation of

DT models eﬀectively requires tailoring them to diﬀerent bridge types, 
each of which presents unique structural complexities and monitor- 
ing challenges. Various structural systems —such as balanced cantilever,

Balanced cantilever bridges are widely used for spanning large dis- 
tances due to their eﬃcient use of materials and construction tech- 
niques. They are often deployed in environments where conventional 
bridge construction methods may not be feasible. Despite these ad- 
vantages, they pose signiﬁcant monitoring challenges due to their seg- 
mental construction, variations in cross-sectional geometry, and sus- 
ceptibility to dynamic loads [ 11 , 12 ]. Their structural behavior un- 
der diﬀerent loading and environmental conditions is highly complex, 
making real-time data integration essential for reliable SHM. How- 
ever, traditional inspection approaches often lack the capacity to cap- 
ture the time-sensitive, high-resolution data required for eﬀective long- 
term monitoring. While digital twins oﬀer a promising solution, fully 
capitalizing on a DT model that accurately integrates real-time ge- 
ometric changes and evolving material properties remains an open 
challenge. 
Addressing this research gap requires a robust approach that inte- 
grates advanced measurement techniques with high-ﬁdelity numerical 
modeling to improve the accuracy and reliability of balanced cantilever 
bridge monitoring. The challenge lies not only in real-time data inte- 
gration but also in reﬁning DT frameworks to accommodate the unique 
geometric and dynamic complexities of these structures. Successfully 
overcoming these challenges will enhance predictive maintenance, im- 
prove structural assessments, and ensure the long-term safety of bal- 
anced cantilever bridges. 
This study aims to address these challenges by developing a DT-based 
SHM system speciﬁcally for balanced cantilever bridges, leveraging ad- 
vanced measurement techniques and digital modeling to enhance accu- 
racy and reliability, thereby fostering a more resilient and sustainably 
managed infrastructure asset [ 13 ]. A comprehensive digital twin model 
requires precise measurements of the bridge’s dynamic behavior and 
structural responses. To achieve this, the study utilizes vibration and dy- 
namic strain measurements obtained through accelerometers and piezo- 
resistive strain gauges [ 14 , 15 ]. Piezo-resistive strain gauges, which can 
capture low-magnitude dynamic strains during operational vibrations, 
provide critical data for model calibration. By integrating these detailed 
measurements into the digital twin model, this study seeks to develop a 
more accurate representation of the bridge’s real-time behavior, thereby 
enabling a more eﬀective SHM strategy [ 16 , 17 ]. 
The incorporation of 3D-LiDAR scanning and Ultrasonic Pulse Veloc- 
ity (UPV) tests marks a signiﬁcant advancement in capturing the as-is 
geometry and material properties of the bridge [ 18 , 19 ]. The 3D point 
cloud model generated from the LiDAR scan oﬀers a comprehensive and 
detailed visualization of the bridge, surpassing the capabilities of con- 
ventional visual inspections, especially in terms of time eﬃciency, ac- 
cessibility, and detail [ 20 ]. These advanced techniques ensure that the 
digital twin model reﬂects the true state of the structure, highlighting 
discrepancies between as-designed and as-built conditions, and empha- 
sizing the importance of updating structural models throughout the ser- 
vice life of the infrastructure [ 21 ]. 
Dynamic properties derived from strain and acceleration measure- 
ments provide crucial insights into the structural behavior under opera- 
tional conditions [ 22 , 23 ]. Strain gauges capture localized deformation, 
while accelerometers provide global response characteristics, such as 
modal frequencies and shapes. The neutral axis depth is a critical param- 
eter, indicating the stress distribution and potential damage within the 
structure [ 24 , 25 ]. Discrepancies between the as-is measurements and 
as-designed drawings highlight the importance of accurate, real-time 
data in maintaining structural integrity. These discrepancies may arise 
from material degradation, construction deviations, or unforeseen load- 
ing conditions, necessitating regular updates to the digital twin model 
for reliable SHM and predictive maintenance. 
Furthermore, the validation of the digital twin model through com- 
parison with ﬁeld measurement data is a critical step in ensuring its ac- 
curacy [ 26 , 27 ]. This study conducts extensive vibration measurements 
to extract modal properties, such as natural frequencies and bending 
mode shapes, which are then compared with the numerical model re- 
sults [ 28 , 29 ]. Good agreement between the measurement data and the 
as-is model results underscores the reliability of the digital twin ap- 
proach. The dynamic strain patterns and neutral axis parameters, sensi- 
tive indicators of local damage, are also analyzed and found to correlate 
well with the model predictions [ 30 , 31 ]. These ﬁndings support the use 
of digital twin models and ﬁeld measurements as baseline data for fu- 
ture bridge inspections and investigations, while also identifying areas 
for further reﬁnement, particularly at span join locations [ 32 , 33 ]. 
2. Digital twin-based structural health monitoring 
Fig. 1 presents the framework for a combined Digital Twin (DT) and 
structural health monitoring system designed for the maintenance of a 
cantilever bridge. The left side outlines the DT model, which is sepa- 
rated into two segments: the As-designed and the As-is models. The As- 
designed model is based on the bridge’s initial design details, including 
a 3D Finite Element Model (3D-FEM) and its initial dynamic properties. 
On the other hand, the As-is model updates the FEM to reﬂect real-time 
conditions using data such as the concrete’s modulus of elasticity ob- 
tained through UPV testing and the bridge’s geometry captured by 3D- 
LiDAR scans. This results in updated dynamic properties of the bridge, 
which are essential for accurate assessment and ongoing monitoring. 
Concerning the structural health monitoring component (see the 
right side of Fig. 1 ), it uses dynamic measurements like acceleration 
and dynamic strain, obtained through accelerometers and piezoresis- 
tive strain gauges, respectively. The data collected from these devices 
are then processed for data acquisition purposes. These experimental 
measurements validate and update the dynamic properties in the digital 
twin model. The footnote clariﬁes that the dynamic properties include 
frequency, mode shapes, normalized modal strain (NMS), and neutral 
axis depth. The integration of the digital twin model with experimen- 
tal data from structural health monitoring allows for a comprehensive 
assessment of the bridge’s condition and serves as a baseline for future 
comparisons, ensuring an informed approach to maintenance and man- 
agement. 
While the individual components of this process, such as numerical 
modeling and structural measurements, are well-established techniques, 
their integration within a digital twin framework —which inherently in- 
volves an interplay between the virtual model and the physical asset to 
build resilience [ 34 ] —enables a more powerful, data-driven approach to 
continuous model updating, performance tracking, and predictive main- 
tenance aligning with advancements in DT modeling that emphasize 
comprehensive data integration [ 35 , 36 ]. The DT framework is partic- 
ularly valuable when construction quality is uncertain or when devia- 
tions from design speciﬁcations may exist, as it provides a dynamic and 
traceable baseline throughout the service life of the infrastructure. Tran- 
sitioning from the theoretical framework to practical application, the 
integration of the DT model with real-time data from structural health 
monitoring, as described in this section, sets the stage for its deployment 
on actual infrastructure. The subsequent section focuses on a case study 
involving the targeted balanced cantilever bridge in Thailand, highlight- 
ing the real-world utility of the technology.

3.4. Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test 
In this study, the UPV technique (see Fig. 1 ) was employed to de- 
termine the current properties of the concrete, which will subsequently 
be incorporated into the 3D-FEM. This technique is a non-destructive 
testing method that evaluates the quality, integrity, and uniformity of 
concrete, masonry, and other construction materials. By measuring the 
time, it takes for ultrasonic pulses to travel through a material, UPV can 
determine its properties and identify defects, voids, or signs of deteri- 
oration without inﬂicting any damage. It should be noted that the tar- 
geted bridge, which has been in service for 10 years, may have material 
properties that deviate from the original design values over its service 
life [ 12 , 39 ]. Notably, the concrete’s modulus of elasticity, a critical and 
time-dependent parameter, plays a signiﬁcant role in determining the 
structural capacity and modal characteristics of the bridge.

3.5. 3D-FEM in the DT model of the bridge 
To investigate the modal properties of the bridge, the 3D-FEM for 
both the as-designed and as-is conditions was simulated using modal 
analysis with SAP2000 [ 38 ]. The as-designed FEM was developed based 
on geometric and concrete properties speciﬁed during the design stage. 
In contrast, the as-is FEM incorporated geometric details captured from 
the 3D-LiDAR scan and the concrete’s elastic modulus from UPV to rea- 
sonably depict the current condition. In the 3D-FEM, the segmental box 
girders and walkway were modeled using shell elements, while the mid- 
span piers were represented by solid elements, as depicted in Fig. 9(a) . 
The shell elements are four-node area objects that reﬂect plate-bending 
behavior with assigned edge constraints, illustrated in Fig. 9(b) . Mean- 
while, the solid elements are deﬁned as eight-node objects, each hav- 
ing six quadrilateral faces with a joint at each corner, as presented in 
Fig. 9(c) . The model was set with a mesh size of 250 mm. Structural con- 
straints were assigned to the pier-to-deck joint to ensure equal rotation 
and translation of connecting regions. 
For both the as-designed and as-is conditions, roller systems were 
employed at both ends for support, and lateral springs were installed at 
the pier ends of the mid-span to represent the lateral and vertical stiﬀ- 
ness of the piles. In the as-designed model, the modulus of elasticity of 
concrete for the segmental box girders and piers were 29,436 MPa and 
27,534 MPa, respectively. Since the UPV test results showed that the 
modulus of elasticity for each box segment varied between 30,385 MPa 
to 45,433 MPa, the concrete’s modulus of elasticity at each box seg- 
ment was updated in the as-is model. Then, the models generated up- 
dated dynamic properties such as natural frequencies, mode shapes, and

4. SHM using dynamic measurements 
To assess the dynamic characteristics of the targeted bridge, a SHM 
system, equipped with accelerometers and piezoresistive strain gauges 
for measuring acceleration and dynamic strain (see SHM in Fig. 1 ), was 
installed along the length of the box girder. The dynamic measurements 
of the bridge under ambient vibration and during regular operation were 
conducted to identify the frequencies, mode shapes, modal strain, and 
neutral axis depth. A frequency-domain technique was employed to ex- 
tract these modal properties from the measurement data. The measure- 
ment results were then compared with both the as-designed and as-is 
models to validate and reﬁne the baseline DT model of the bridge. 
4.1. Measurement of acceleration under ambient vibration 
To identify the natural frequencies and mode shapes of the bridge, 
acceleration-based measurements were conducted using two sets of uni- 
axial accelerometers in the vertical direction, as shown in Fig. 10(a) . One 
set was positioned 88.7 m from the bridge’s left side and designated as 
the reference station, indicated by a blue cross in Fig. 10(a) . This loca- 
tion was chosen both for its ability to capture the 1st fundamental mode 
shapes of a three-span bridge and for its reduced sensitivity to local 
defects in the structure, thus ensuring consistent acceleration measure- 
ments. The second set of accelerometers was utilized at moving stations, 
consisting of 25 locations strategically placed with intervals of 7.5 m and 
10 m in the side and middle spans, respectively. These moving stations 
are marked by red circles in Fig. 10(a) . To record acceleration data, two 
data acquisition systems were set up at both the reference and moving 
stations. A voltage pulse signal generator was employed to ensure time 
synchronization among the data acquisition units [ 41 ]. 
The accelerometer at the reference station features an acceleration 
measurement capacity of ± 0.5 g, whereas the accelerometers at the mov- 
ing stations have a capacity of ± 0.25 g. Each measurement set at the 
various moving stations lasted 5 min with a sampling rate of 100 Hz. 
Under the service response, the natural frequency of the bridge at each 
location was calculated using the Pwelch windowing function in MAT- 
LAB software [ 42 ]. Mode shapes were determined by normalizing the 
peak magnitudes of Fourier Response Functions between the moving 
and reference acceleration data. 
4.2. Measurement of dynamic strains under ambient vibration 
Segmental box girders in a balanced cantilever system are prone to 
mid-span cracking and deﬂection due to environmental factors, materi- 
als aging, or overloading, all of which can signiﬁcantly impacting the 
bridge’s ultimate ﬂexural capacity [ 43–46 ]. Therefore, the neutral axis 
(NA), inﬂuenced by material and geometric properties and loading con- 
ditions, is critical in assessing the stiﬀness and ﬂexural performance 
necessary for structural integrity evaluations [ 24 , 47 ]. To monitor the

sents an enhancement 60 times greater than that of traditional metal foil 
gauges, as noted in previous studies [ 48 , 49 ]. The high resolution of these 
gauges enabled precise capture of sub-micron strains during operational 
vibrations. In total, 336 piezo-resistive strain gauges were installed at 
42 locations, illustrated in Fig. 10(b) . To systematically record the lin- 
early distributed strain across each cross-section, eight strain gauges, la-

validating the bridge’s structural behavior under the operational load.

damage-sensitive information, it is inﬂuenced by unknown or variable 
excitation levels in ambient conditions. NMS addresses this challenge 
by normalizing the frequency-domain strain data, emphasizing the rel-

highlights deviations due to structural irregularities. However, this nor- 
malization inherently ﬁlters out amplitude-based diﬀerences that could 
indicate the severity of localized stiﬀness loss. Therefore, while NMS is 
eﬀective for spatial pattern recognition and structural consistency as- 
sessment, it should be complemented by absolute strain analysis or cal- 
ibrated damage indices in future work to improve sensitivity to minor 
or incipient damage.

5.4. Shifting of NA depth 
The NA depth serves as a critical parameter for evaluating the ﬂex- 
ural behavior of the bridge and assessing potential structural changes 
over time. The NA depth was determined from NMS measurements at 
the top and bottom of the cross-sections for diﬀerent bending modes. 
The results for the ﬁrst two vertical bending modes ( f = 1.635 Hz and 
f = 2.468 Hz) are presented in Fig. 17 , illustrating comparisons between 
measured values and predictions from both the as-designed and as-is 
ﬁnite element models. 
The measured NA depths exhibit a stable pattern along the bridge 
length, closely following the trends predicted by the numerical models. 
Variations in NA depth are observed at inﬂection points, where mo- 
ment redistribution occurs, as expected. The as-is model demonstrates 
stronger agreement with the measured data compared to the as-designed 
model, particularly at midspans and inﬂection zones. This improved cor- 
relation suggests that updating the ﬁnite element model with geometric 
and material properties derived from 3D-LiDAR scans and UPV tests en- 
hances its predictive accuracy. 
It is also important to note that the NA positions diﬀer between the 
1st and 2nd bending modes due to the fundamental diﬀerences in their 
modal shapes and deformation patterns. The 1st bending mode, char- 
acterized by a single curvature over the span, leads to more uniform 
strain distributions and relatively consistent NA positions. In contrast, 
the 2nd bending mode introduces multiple inﬂection points, resulting 
in more localized curvature changes and dynamic strain concentration 
in diﬀerent regions of the girder. These higher-mode eﬀects tend to am- 
plify diﬀerences in NA depth, especially in structurally complex or less 
stiﬀened sections. Thus, variations in NA depth across modes are not 
anomalies but rather theoretical outcomes of diﬀering modal behavior. 
Additionally, the observed ﬂuctuations in NA depth align with strain 
distribution trends discussed in Section 5.3 , reinforcing the consistency 
of the measured response. Importantly, no signiﬁcant shifts in the NA 
position are detected, indicating that the overall ﬂexural behavior of 
the bridge remains consistent with design expectations. The absence of 
abrupt deviations suggests that no major structural damage, such as ex- 
cessive cracking or material degradation, has compromised the bridge’s 
stiﬀness or load-carrying capacity. 
The agreement between the measured NA depths and the as-is model 
validates the digital twin framework for this structure. By integrating 
real-time monitoring data with numerical modeling, the updated model 
provides a reliable baseline for ongoing SHM and facilitates early de- 
tection of potential structural changes. However, for future structural 
health monitoring and damage identiﬁcation, the measured NA posi- 
tions should serve as the primary benchmark. Unlike numerical predic- 
tions, ﬁeld measurements capture the actual structural behavior under 
real operational and environmental conditions, including the eﬀects of 
damage, construction variability, and material aging. While the digi- 
tal twin model is invaluable for comparative analysis and forecasting, 
the real-time measurements provide the most accurate baseline against 
which changes and anomalies can be detected over time. Continuous 
monitoring of NA variations over time will be essential for tracking 
long-term performance and identifying emerging structural concerns, 
particularly in regions susceptible to environmental eﬀects or high traf- 
ﬁc loads. 
6. Discussion 
Accurately capturing the current state of the balanced cantilever 
bridge was critical for developing the DT-based SHM system in this 
study. 3D-LiDAR scanning eﬀectively provided the as-is geometry, while 
UPV tests were essential for determining the in-situ concrete modulus 
of elasticity. The resulting detailed 3D point cloud model of the bridge’s 
box girder demonstrated considerable advantages over conventional vi- 
sual inspections regarding time eﬃciency, accessibility, and the level of 
detail achieved, aﬃrming the value of these non-destructive techniques 
for creating robust "as-is" DT models. 
A signiﬁcant ﬁnding was the observed discrepancy between the mea- 
sured as-is geometry and concrete modulus of elasticity compared to the 
original as-designed details. This highlights the imperative to develop 
DT models that reﬂect real-time structural behavior, necessitating up- 
dates after construction and throughout the service life of civil infras- 
tructure to ensure the accuracy of structural assessments. 
Validation of the "as-is" analytical model against ﬁeld measurements 
was a cornerstone of this research. Modal properties extracted from 
vibration measurements, speciﬁcally natural frequencies and bending 
mode shapes, showed good agreement with the "as-is" model results. 
This synergy between as-is geometric and material data, integrated with 
experimental results, is crucial for validating numerical FE models in- 
tended for reliable bridge assessment. 
Dynamic modal strain patterns and NA depth, derived by normaliz- 
ing dynamic strain to acceleration data, emerged as sensitive indicators 
of structural behavior and correlated well with "as-is" model predictions. 
This conﬁrms their utility for establishing a comprehensive baseline for 
future bridge inspections. However, some observable diﬀerences in NMS 
and NA magnitude persisted between experimental and numerical re- 
sults, particularly at span join locations. These discrepancies may arise 
from uncaptured material defects or the complexities of modeling time- 
dependent eﬀects from various construction stages —challenges inher- 
ent in numerical simulations. Such localized variations identify areas 
for further reﬁnement in DT modeling, especially in the detailed repre- 
sentation of joints and material non-uniformities.

is ”DT model, coupled with the sensitivity of parameters like NMS and
