ABSTRACT
Predictive maintenance is a key enabler of automated Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) for safety-critical 
infrastructure, supporting reliable operation under increasing service demands. This paper presents an inte -
grated Digital Twin – Artificial Intelligence (DT – AI) framework for the automated condition assessment of bonded 
Insulated Rail Joints (IRJs) and adjacent sleepers. A high-fidelity Digital Twin is developed to simulate joint 
behaviour under varying preload and support degradation scenarios, generating physically consistent datasets 
across representative loading conditions. Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) and traditional Machine 
Learning (ML) classifiers are trained on time – frequency representations of selected predictive indicators to 
identify and classify degradation states. Results demonstrate that the proposed physics-informed DT-driven 
framework achieves high diagnostic accuracy and strong generalization capability, with DCNNs models out -
performing conventional classifiers. By integrating physics-based simulation with automated data analytics, the 
proposed framework advances scalable, data-driven SHM and predictive maintenance strategies, for the intel -
ligent automation of railway infrastructure management.
1. Introduction
Modern railway networks are advanced technological systems that 
underpin sustainable mobility, fostering social cohesion, economic 
growth, and regional integration [1] . By enhancing safety and capacity 
while promoting a modal shift from road to rail, efficient rail systems 
contribute to reduced congestion and greenhouse gas emissions, sup -
porting circular economy objectives [2] . Achieving these goals requires 
not only infrastructure expansion but also improved reliability, resil -
ience, and optimized lifecycle management of critical assets.
In this context, the digitalization of railway infrastructure, through 
Digital Twins (DTs), the Internet of Things (IoT), and Artificial Intelli -
gence (AI)-based predictive tools, represents a transformative paradigm 
for asset management [3] . These technologies enable real-time moni -
toring, automated anomaly detection, and Predictive Maintenance 
(PdM), moving beyond traditional time-based scheduling. Their stra -
tegic importance is reflected in large-scale national and European ini -
tiatives, such as the Italian project MOST — Centro Nazionale per la 
Mobilit `a Sostenibile [4] , Spoke 4 Railway Transport, funded under the 
National Recovery and Resilience Plan (NRRP) and the Next Generation 
EU (NGEU) programme [5] , which promote advanced digital solutions 
for sustainable railway transport.
Among safety-critical railway components, bonded Insulated Rail 
Joints (IRJs) remain essential in conventional signalling systems until 
the transition to European Rail Traffic Management System/European 
Train Control System (ERTMS/ETCS) and Radio Block Center (RBC) 
technologies, is fully completed. While ensuring electrical insulation 
and mechanical continuity, IRJs introduce structural discontinuities 
relative to continuous welded rail, making them inherently susceptible 
to degradation. Failures may compromise signalling integrity, reduce 
track stiffness, and adversely affect operational safety. Periodic 
inspection-based maintenance is increasingly inadequate, as IRJ dete -
rioration results from complex interactions among cyclic loading, bolt 
preload loss, environmental effects, and support degradation [6] .
Despite robust design, bonded IRJs are prone to progressive bolt 
loosening, cracking, and wear. Loss of bolt preload increases vertical 
deflection and may induce bolt – hole clearance undetectable until 
cracking occurs [7] . Their reduced bending stiffness amplifies defor -
mation under train passage, accelerating deterioration and increasing 
maintenance costs [8,9] . Adjacent sleepers also play a critical role: 
stiffness discontinuities around joints intensify impact loads and stress 
concentrations [10] , contributing to ballast degradation [11] and

Physics-based Digital Twins integrated with data-driven models offer 
a promising alternative for predictive maintenance [17,18] . While DTs 
have been applied to railway bridges, switches, and rolling stock 
[19,20] , and previous studies have examined bolt degradation, IRJ 
behaviour, or sleeper stiffness separately, no work has systematically 
integrated their coupled interaction within a unified DT – AI framework 
for automated diagnostics. Traditional methods, such as isolated com -
puter vision or conventional sensors, often rely on heuristic thresholds 
and manual inspections, limiting scalability and real-time applicability.
To address these limitations, the present study develops a high- 
fidelity, physics-informed Digital Twin that reproduces the coupled 
mechanical behaviour of IRJs and adjacent sleepers under both nominal 
and controlled degradation scenarios that cannot be safely reproduced 
in the field. DT-generated signals, specifically vertical displacement and 
gap value. i.e. the longitudinal displacement between adjacent rails, are 
transformed into time – frequency representations and analysed using 
Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (DCNNs) and traditional Machine 
Learning (ML) classifiers for automated condition assessment.
The main contributions of this work are: 
• A unified physics-informed Digital Twin reproducing the coupled me -
chanical behaviour of bonded IRJs and adjacent sleepers under 
varying bolt-preload and support-stiffness conditions.
• A structured definition of structural health classes grounded in me -
chanically interpretable degradation mechanisms.
• A systematic comparative assessment of time – frequency-based deep 
learning models (STFT-CNN and WT-CNN) and a traditional ML 
decision-tree classifier across six predictive maintenance scenarios.
• A methodology for quantifying the detectability limits of various 
degradation modes, linking classification performance to underlying 
mechanical phenomena and providing a foundation for future 
maintenance prioritization.
From an automation perspective, the proposed framework estab -
lishes a closed-loop, decision-oriented monitoring workflow in which 
DT simulations generate labelled degradation scenarios that are mapped 
to measurable indicators through AI-based classifiers. By embedding 
mechanical knowledge within the diagnostic logic, the methodology 
reduces reliance on manual inspections and heuristic thresholds, 
enabling scalable predictive maintenance aligned with digital trans -
formation objectives. The decision-oriented monitoring workflow re -
duces human dependency by automating the classification of 
degradation states, thus enabling data-driven maintenance decisions 
and mitigating operational bottlenecks throughout the infrastructure 
lifecycle.

1.1. Digital twins and AI-driven predictive maintenance in railway 
infrastructure
Digital Twin technology is rapidly advancing in the Architecture, 
Engineering and Construction (AEC) sector [21] , enabling high-fidelity 
virtual representations of infrastructure under realistic operational 
conditions. In railway systems, DTs support lifecycle optimization, sce -
nario analysis, and risk assessment [17 – 20] . However, their full PdM 
potential emerges when integrated with ML and DL techniques capable 
of extracting patterns from large and heterogeneous datasets [22] . This 
study introduces a novel integration of a physics-informed Digital Twin 
with deep learning-based diagnostics, which offers a significant 
advancement over prior work by addressing the coupled interaction of 
IRJs and adjacent sleepers.
A key advantage of physics-informed DTs is their ability to generate 
controlled degradation scenarios that cannot be safely reproduced in the 
field. Railway datasets are inherently unbalanced: healthy-condition 
measurements are abundant, whereas severe or progressive damage 
data are scarce. DT simulations enable systematic generation of labelled, 
mechanically consistent degradation states, producing training datasets 
with meaningful predictive content more efficiently than extensive 
experimental campaigns. When combined with AI models, this capa -
bility supports automated scalable SHM workflows while complement -
ing conventional sensor- and vision-based approaches.
AI techniques have demonstrated strong predictive performance 
across multiple domains [23,24] and are increasingly adopted in railway 
monitoring [25,26] . While interpretable ML methods provide structured 
modelling of multimodal sensor data, CNN-based DL approaches enable 
automatic feature extraction from high-dimensional signals, improving 
scalability and reducing manual feature engineering. To ensure inter -
pretability and operational reliability, we embed mechanical knowledge 
into the DCNN framework, guiding the network with physically mean -
ingful features.
Large-scale implementations further demonstrate the potential of AI- 
enhanced DTs in rail systems. For example, Siemens-supported projects 
in China's high-speed rail network employed ML-augmented DT 
frameworks to improve reliability and maintenance efficiency [27] . 
Other studies explored Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) and Interfero -
metric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) data integration for predictive 
maintenance [28] , HybridGAN architectures for defect detection under 
low-quality imagery [29] , and UAV-based monitoring for safety

1.2. Degradation mechanisms and monitoring of bonded IRJs and sleepers
Historically, maintenance of bonded IRJs has relied on periodic in -
spections and preventive routines [14] . However, IRJs remain vulner -
able to bolt loosening, cracking, and material degradation. Reduced 
bending stiffness amplifies dynamic response under loading [8,9] , while 
preload loss increases vertical displacement and gap variation preceding 
visible damage [7] . Stiffness discontinuities at joints intensify impact 
forces on adjacent sleepers [10] , contributing to ballast degradation 
[11] , increased rail-seat pressures, and potential cracking of prestressed 
concrete sleepers [12,13] . The coupled interaction between joint flexi -
bility and sleeper support conditions critically affects track durability 
and geometry preservation.
Modern monitoring methods, including axlebox acceleration-based 
drive-by monitoring [31] , Fibre Bragg Grating (FBG)-based curvature 
sensing [11] , acoustic emission techniques [32] , and non-destructive 
evaluation approaches such as ultrasonics, infrared thermography, and 
ML-enhanced vision systems [12,33] , provide valuable diagnostic in -
formation. However, these techniques require extensive field instru -
mentation and do not allow controlled reproduction of severe 
degradation states for algorithm training.
1.3. Time – frequency representations and CNN-based diagnostics
Transforming time-series signals into time – frequency representa -
tions, such as spectrograms (via Short-Time Fourier Transform, STFT) 
and scalograms (via Wavelet Transform, WT), enables mapping struc -
tural responses into image-like formats suitable for CNN analysis.
Spectrogram-based CNNs have outperformed traditional feature- 
engineering methods in bearing diagnostics [34] , pavement crack 
detection [35] , and railway axle fault identification [36] . These ap -
proaches demonstrate that time – frequency imaging allows CNNs to 
capture subtle spectral signatures associated with incipient damage.
For bonded IRJs, indicators such as vertical displacement and gap 
value, i.e. the longitudinal displacement between adjacent rails, previ -
ously identified as predictive of structural degradation [16,37] , exhibit 
transient spectral characteristics during wheel passage. Converting these 
signals into high-resolution time – frequency maps enables automated 
extraction of degradation patterns that may remain undetectable in raw 
time-domain analysis.
1.4. Research gap and positioning of the present study
DT modelling, FE simulation, and AI-based diagnostics are estab -
lished in SHM [35,38] . In railway engineering, DT and AI approaches 
are increasingly applied to individual subsystems [19,20] . Recent work 
has proposed DT-based frameworks for enhancing the resilience of 
cyber-physical systems [22] . Systematic reviews have synthesised ML 
methods for predictive maintenance applications [23] , outlined the 
foundations of AI for engineering practice [24] , and highlighted 
emerging ML-driven approaches for SHM under high-dimensional data 
environments [25] . More recently, railway-specific reviews have docu -
mented the transition from conventional monitoring techniques toward 
predictive maintenance strategies [26] . However, the coupled interac -
tion between bonded IRJs and adjacent sleepers has not been investi -
gated within a unified DT – AI framework designed for automated 
condition assessment.
Building on our previous work [16] , which combined a DT with a 
decision-tree classifier under simplified assumptions, this study ad -
vances modelling fidelity and automation by capturing IRJ – sleeper 
coupling and exploiting full time-series signals transformed into 
time – frequency representations for DCNN-based diagnostics.
The proposed framework is designed as a physics-informed DT, 
capable of generating mechanically consistent and systematically 
labelled degradation states to address the scarcity of real failure data. 
While the current validation is conducted on synthetic datasets, the 
architecture is conceived for future integration with laboratory and field 
measurements through transfer learning and hybrid virtual – physical 
updating strategies. Unlike traditional FE models, this DT-oriented 
framework is intended to integrate real-time data streams and provide 
dynamic feedback for decision-making, bridging the gap between nu -
merical simulation and operational predictive maintenance.
The primary objective is the development and validation of a 
coherent DT – AI synergy framework for automated predictive mainte -
nance. The comparative evaluation of deep learning and traditional ML 
classifiers serves to assess the robustness and scalability of this inte -
grated architecture rather than to perform isolated algorithm bench -
marking. The novelty lies in the systematic integration of validated 
modelling and AI tools into a physics-informed, decision-oriented 
workflow that supports scalable automation and future alignment be -
tween virtual simulations and real-world railway operation.
2. Materials and methods
This section presents the integrated methodology underpinning the 
proposed DT – AI framework, combining a physics-informed Digital Twin 
for modelling the coupled behaviour of bonded IRJs and adjacent 
sleepers ( Section 2.1 ) with DCNN-based time – frequency analysis for 
automated predictive maintenance and condition classification ( Section 
2.2 ).
2.1. Digital Twin framework for bonded IRJ and sleeper modelling
The proposed Digital Twin (DT) reproduces the coupled mechanical 
behaviour of bonded Insulated Rail Joints (IRJs) and adjacent sleepers 
under controlled degradation scenarios, including bolt-preload loss and 
sleeper stiffness reduction. It generates physically consistent synthetic 
data suitable for AI-driven diagnostics, supporting predictive mainte -
nance without requiring exhaustive field measurements in the early 
development stages. Previous work demonstrated that classifiers trained 
on synthetic DT-generated signals generalize effectively to safe-field 
experimental measurements, providing evidence that the Digital Twin 
reproduces mechanically relevant responses that reliably enable AI- 
driven diagnostics [16] .
2.1.1. Geometrical configuration and modelling assumptions
The DT of the suspended bonded IRJ was developed using the FE 
platform Abaqus/CAE. The model provides a geometrically simplified

Electrical insulation between the rails is represented by a 5 mm end

response. The adopted configuration therefore preserves structural fi -
delity while ensuring numerical robustness.
The passage of two train wheels (diameter 920 mm, wheelbase 2700 
mm) is simulated following established IRJ methodologies [39] . Wheel 
loads are applied vertically and symmetrically along the rail axis, 
consistent with straight-track conditions. Minor-axis bending and 
torsional effects are neglected, as previous IRJ studies identified vertical 
load-transfer mechanisms as the dominant contributors to gap formation 
and rail-end displacement [40,41] .
2.1.2. Material modelling, contact formulation, and loading strategy
All structural components, rails, fishplates, bolts, wheels, and end 
post, are explicitly modelled and assembled ( Fig. 2 ). Material properties 
and cross-sectional parameters are derived from literature values for 
standard rail steels and commonly adopted insulating materials (nylon, 
polyurethane, epoxy – fibreglass composites) [42,43] .
The analysis adopts a quasi-static formulation with geometric 
nonlinearity enabled (Nlgeom option). Bolt preload and wheel loads are 
applied incrementally to ensure numerical stability. Wheel passage is 
simulated through prescribed translational motion at a temporal reso -
lution equivalent to 1000 Hz.
Rail – end post interfaces are defined via tie constraints [44] , while all 
remaining interactions are governed by a general contact formulation 
with a tangential penalty coefficient of 0.6, representative of dry 
wheel – rail contact conditions [16,45] . This configuration captures the 
dominant nonlinear contact phenomena influencing joint stiffness and 
gap evolution.
A constant wheel speed of 18 m/s ( ≈ 65 km/h) is adopted, repre -
sentative of regional and suburban operating regimes in which bonded 
IRJs remain widely deployed [37,46] . Within this moderate-speed 
range, quasi-static modelling reproduces the primary stiffness- 
governed response with limited deviation from fully dynamic peak in -
dicators [47] , while maintaining computational efficiency compatible 
with large parametric campaigns. Extension to variable-speed and dy -
namic analyses is identified as a natural progression of the framework.

2.1.4. Bolt preload modelling and structural health states
Bolt assemblies are represented through a simplified but mechani -
cally consistent modelling strategy in which the bolt – nut – washer system 
is treated as a unified preloaded component [40,43] . This approach 
captures the dominant influence of clamping force on joint flexural 
stiffness and contact pressure redistribution without introducing un -
necessary local nonlinearities.
A nominal preload of 75 kN represents standard assembly conditions. 
Reduced preloads of 50 kN and 25 kN simulate progressive clamping- 
force relaxation due to cyclic loading, embedment, and micro-slip phe -
nomena observed in service conditions [7] . Missing-bolt configurations 
represent extreme loss of mechanical continuity.
These preload levels are not arbitrarily selected numerical partitions; 
they correspond to mechanically distinct regimes characterised by 
monotonic amplification of gap values and rail-end vertical displace -
ment. Structural health states are therefore defined according to tran -
sitions in load-transfer behaviour rather than abstract signal thresholds.
2.1.5. Sensitivity-informed parameter selection
The degradation parameters adopted in the Digital Twin are defined 
following a sensitivity-informed strategy aimed at ensuring mechanical 
representativeness and robustness of the diagnostic framework. 
Parameter ranges are selected to span distinct regimes of load-transfer 
behaviour, enabling clear discrimination of degradation-induced struc -
tural responses rather than reproducing a single calibrated condition.
The selected bolt preload levels (75 – 25 kN) reflect progressive 
clamping-force loss mechanisms reported in bonded IRJs [16] . Simi -
larly, the 50% reduction in sleeper support stiffness is consistent with 
degradation scenarios associated with ballast fouling and void devel -
opment [11] in railway track systems [12,13] . These values are there -
fore interpreted as physically meaningful degradation states rather than 
prescriptive thresholds.
Targeted sensitivity checks indicate that the primary diagnostic in -
dicators, namely gap value and vertical displacement, vary mono -
tonically with stiffness degradation, while secondary modelling 
parameters exert limited influence on global response patterns. This 
behaviour is consistent with established vehicle – track interaction 
studies, which identify support stiffness as the dominant factor gov -
erning track response [50] . Consequently, class separability is driven by 
intrinsic mechanical contrasts rather than specific parameter tuning.
This parameterization provides a robust basis for DT-generated 
training datasets, supporting future integration with experimental data 
within hybrid virtual – physical monitoring frameworks.
2.1.6. Numerical verification and experimental benchmarking
A mesh convergence study was conducted by refining discretisation 
in critical regions (rail ends, bolt interfaces, fishplate contacts) and 
monitoring peak gap and vertical displacement under a 60 kN load. 
Variations below 2% beyond the adopted C3D8R mesh confirmed 
discretisation-independent global response. Reduced-integration 8-node 
brick elements provide an effective balance between accuracy and 
computational cost.
The modelling framework builds upon established finite-element 
formulations for bonded and mechanical rail joints, including analyses 
of IRJ deflection behaviour under wheel loading [39,43] , bolt-preload 
effects and stress concentrations in rail joints [40,41] , and train – track 
interaction mechanisms associated with insulated joints [48] . Experi -
mental validation of IRJ response through dedicated field measurement

2.1.7. Synthetic datasets as a structured baseline for automation
The DT configuration remains consistent across all case studies 
presented in Section 3 ; only degradation parameters (bolt preload and 
sleeper stiffness) are varied. Simulation outputs, specifically gap value 
and rail-end vertical displacement extracted at probe locations near the 
joint and critical sleepers ( Fig. 3 ), are compiled into labelled datasets for 
AI training and testing.
The reliance on synthetic data is a deliberate methodological choice. 
Severe degradation scenarios are inherently scarce, unsafe to reproduce 
experimentally, and typically imbalanced relative to healthy conditions. 
The Digital Twin provides a controlled, mechanically consistent envi -
ronment in which primary degradation mechanisms can be systemati -
cally isolated and parameterised.
The present validation therefore establishes a physics-informed 
upper-bound benchmark under controlled conditions. Environmental 
variability, measurement noise, and operational uncertainty are not 
explicitly modelled at this stage but are identified as part of ongoing 
hybrid virtual – physical updating strategies aimed at progressively 
enhancing robustness under real-world deployment conditions.
In this context, the use of synthetic data is not intended to approxi -
mate full operational complexity, but to establish a controlled, mecha -
nistically interpretable learning baseline from which model behaviour 
can be rigorously assessed before progressive exposure to real-world 
variability.
2.2. DCNN with time – frequency analysis
Recent advances in AI-driven railway monitoring have demonstrated 
the value of combining high-fidelity datasets with data-driven models 
for damage detection and prognosis [25,26] . Similar trends have also 
emerged in broader SHM applications [51,52] . Within this context, DTs 
are particularly effective for SHM, as they provide physically consistent 
data under both nominal and systematically degraded conditions. Since 
no single AI technique is universally optimal, this study adopts and 
compares multiple learning strategies to evaluate their suitability for 
automated health assessment of bonded IRJs.
Building on previous Machine Learning (ML) investigations on bolt 
preload degradation in IRJs [16] , the present work extends the frame -
work to Deep Learning (DL) approaches inspired by recent fault- 
classification methodologies based on time – frequency imaging 
[34 – 36] . The proposed methodology consists of three sequential stages: 
(i) transformation of DT-generated time-domain signals into time -
– frequency – energy representations, (ii) training of Convolutional Neu -
ral Networks (CNNs) on the resulting image-based datasets, and (iii) 
performance testing for automated classification of IRJ structural health 
states.
2.2.1. Training data and time – frequency feature construction
Vertical displacement and gap value, defined as the relative longi -
tudinal displacement between adjacent rails, were selected as primary 
diagnostic variables. These indicators exhibit strong mechanical sensi -
tivity to bolt preload loss and sleeper stiffness degradation, enabling 
physically interpretable definition of structural health classes. Their 
relevance is supported by previous numerical and experimental in -
vestigations [15,16] and further demonstrated in Section 3 . Moreover, 
both quantities are measurable in practice using modern sensing tech -
nologies [53] , reinforcing the translational potential of the proposed 
framework.
For each of the six predictive maintenance case studies, DT outputs 
were organised into labelled datasets representing discrete degradation 
states. Each sample includes the corresponding wheel load, which is 
explicitly incorporated into the neural architecture. This multimodal 
configuration enables the classifier to distinguish load-induced varia -
tions from degradation-induced effects, reducing the risk of mis -
classifying high-load healthy states or low-load damaged states. Explicit 
inclusion of operational load information avoids the need for substan -
tially larger datasets to implicitly learn load effects from image features 
alone.
Each simulation covers 0.8 s, corresponding to wheel traversal of the 
joint at 18 m/s. Signals were recorded at 1000 Hz, resulting in 800 time 
samples per simulation. Time-domain responses were transformed into 
time – frequency representations using both the Short-Time Fourier 
Transform (STFT) and the Wavelet Transform (WT), generating spec -
trograms and scalograms, respectively. Spectrograms provide fixed 
resolution suitable for quasi-stationary components, while scalograms 
exploit multi-resolution analysis to capture localized non-stationary 
phenomena. These representations allow CNNs to automatically 
extract degradation signatures without manual feature engineering.
The STFT implementation employed a Hamming window of 
approximately one quarter of the signal length with 50% overlap. The 
FFT length was defined as the maximum between 256 and the next 
power of two of the window size, ensuring adequate frequency resolu -
tion while limiting spectral leakage. This configuration provided a 
balanced compromise between temporal localisation of wheel – joint

For the WT-based scalograms, a filter bank was constructed for the

Preliminary sensitivity checks were conducted on a reduced subset of 
simulations by varying window length, overlap ratio, and analytic 
wavelet selection within the same family. These variations did not 
qualitatively alter class separability or relative model ranking, con -
firming that diagnostic performance is not an artefact of singular 
parameter tuning. Since the dataset is synthetically generated in a 
controlled numerical environment without measurement noise, time -
– frequency representations reflect exclusively the mechanically 
modelled degradation mechanisms. The objective of this study is 
therefore not optimisation of imaging parameters, but validation of a 
physics-informed DT – AI integration framework. Extended sensitivity 
analyses will become particularly relevant when synchronizing the DT 
with laboratory and field measurements incorporating operational 
variability.
An 80/20 stratified split between training and testing datasets was 
adopted for each case study, ensuring balanced representation of all 
health classes. The total number of samples per scenario is reported in 
Table 1 ( Section 3 ). The 20% hold-out subset was reserved exclusively 
for evaluation on previously unseen data.
The STFT and WT parameters were selected based on established 
time – frequency analysis methodologies for non-stationary structural 
signals [34 – 36] , balancing temporal and frequency resolution to capture 
localized degradation effects while maintaining computational effi -
ciency [54,55] . Preliminary sensitivity analyses confirmed that moder -
ate variations in window length, overlap, and wavelet choice did not 
materially affect class separability, consistent with prior studies on 
vibration-based damage detection in railway components [56] .
2.2.2. AI classification models
All models were developed within the MATLAB ® environment, 
which integrates signal processing, image analysis, and machine 
learning toolboxes within a unified SHM-oriented workflow [57,58] . DT 
simulation outputs were transformed into image-based representations 
and processed using Deep CNN (DCNN) architectures designed to 
accommodate both image inputs and operational features.
The adopted network consists of an image input layer matching 
spectrogram or scalogram dimensions, followed by a convolutional layer 
with 5 × 5 kernels and 32 filters. Batch normalisation and a rectified 
linear unit (ReLU) activation are applied to enhance convergence sta -
bility. A fully connected layer with 50 neurons produces a compact 
embedding of extracted features. The resulting feature vector, originally 
in “ SSCB ” (spatial, spatial, channel, batch) format, is flattened, into “ CB ” 
(channel, batch) format, and concatenated with the numerical wheel- 
load input along the channel dimension. Final classification is per -
formed through a fully connected layer with output size equal to the 
number of health states, followed by a softmax layer to generate class 
probabilities. A schematic representation is provided in Fig. 4 .
Training was conducted using Stochastic Gradient Descent with 
Momentum (SGDM) and cross-entropy loss. The training configuration 
includes an initial learning rate of 1 × 10
  4
, mini-batch size of 15, and a 
maximum of 20 epochs. Convergence stability was monitored during 
training, and overfitting was mitigated through conservative hyper -
parameter selection and balanced dataset partitioning. No adaptive 
learning-rate scheduling was applied, as stable convergence was 
consistently achieved under these settings.
The architectural depth was intentionally limited. The objective of 
this work is not benchmarking increasingly complex deep networks, but 
validating a deployable DT – AI framework suitable for predictive 
maintenance automation. Excessively deep architectures may increase 
computational burden and reduce interpretability without proportion -
ate diagnostic gains under mechanically structured datasets. The 
selected configuration provides sufficient representational capacity to 
capture localized degradation patterns while remaining computation -
ally efficient and compatible with edge- or cloud-based monitoring 
infrastructures.
Two DL models were implemented using this architecture: STFT- 
CNN and WT-CNN. In parallel, a supervised ML model was developed 
using time-domain DT outputs. A medium-depth Decision Tree classifier 
was selected as a transparent and computationally efficient baseline. 
Because ML approaches require explicit feature engineering, gap value 
and vertical displacement were directly used as input features. This 
structured comparison enables assessment of feature-learning DL versus 
feature-engineered ML within the same physics-informed dataset, sup -
porting evaluation of scalability, interpretability, and automation 
readiness rather than exhaustive classifier benchmarking.
To further corroborate the robustness of the proposed framework, an 
additional reference model based on a Support Vector Machine with 
radial basis function (RBF) kernel was also implemented. The corre -
sponding results are reported in Section 2 of Supplementary Material.

Overfitting was mitigated through complementary strategies. For the 
DCNN, a lightweight architecture was adopted (32 convolutional filters, 
fully connected layer of size 50, MaxEpochs = 20, mini-batch size 15) to 
balance representational capacity and computational efficiency. During 
training, convergence behaviour was monitored using MATLAB's Vali -
dationFrequency option, which evaluates model performance at fixed 
intervals of 10 iterations (mini-batch updates). Importantly, these 
evaluations are performed on mini-batches drawn from the training data 
stream and are used exclusively for training diagnostics (i.e., tracking 
loss and accuracy evolution). They do not constitute an independent 
validation dataset and are not used for hyperparameter tuning or model 
selection.
Under this configuration, generalization is assessed through two 
complementary mechanisms: (i) iterative monitoring of training dy -
namics (loss and accuracy trends across mini-batches), ensuring stable 
convergence without divergence or memorisation effects; and (ii) final 
evaluation on the independent 20% hold-out test set, which provides an 
unbiased estimate of predictive performance.
For the decision-tree model, 5-fold cross-validation, consistent with 
the approach adopted in [16] , was additionally implemented within 
MATLAB's Classification Learner app to provide a standard robustness 
check.
A separate static validation set was not introduced as an additional 
partition. In deep learning workflows, validation sets are primarily 
required for hyperparameter optimisation and model selection [55,59] . 
In the present study, model architecture and training parameters are 
defined a priori and not iteratively tuned against validation perfor -
mance. Moreover, the datasets are synthetically generated, physics- 
informed, and noise-free, resulting in deterministic class structure and 
reduced variance compared with typical data-driven applications. 
Under these conditions, a two-way hold-out strategy provides a statis -
tically consistent and computationally efficient framework for assessing 
generalization without introducing unnecessary data fragmentation.
The adopted 80/20 split remains consistent with established ma -
chine learning practice for controlled datasets and provides an effective 
balance between training sufficiency and unbiased evaluation [60,61] .
Because the datasets are synthetically generated and noise-free, the 
reported performance represents an upper-bound benchmark of intrinsic 
mechanical separability. Equivalent performance under real operating 
conditions cannot be assumed and will require progressive integration of 
noise-contaminated and field-acquired data through hybrid virtual – -
physical updating strategies.
2.2.4. Performance testing
Model performance was evaluated using standard classification 
metrics, including accuracy (A), precision (P), recall (R), and F1-score 
(F1), as defined in [16] . Accuracy quantifies overall correctness, preci -
sion assesses reliability of positive predictions by limiting false positives, 
recall measures detection completeness by minimizing false negatives, 
and F1-score balances precision and recall.
Performance assessment was further supported by confusion 
matrices and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. ROC 
analysis illustrates the trade-off between true positive rate (TPR) and 
false positive rate (FPR) across decision thresholds, while the area under 
the curve (AUC) provides a scalar measure of discrimination capability, 
with values approaching unity indicating superior separability [16] . 
These evaluation criteria are widely adopted in SHM and related diag -
nostic domains [35] .
The integrated MATLAB ® workflow enables consistent preprocess -
ing, training, and evaluation within the same computational environ -
ment, ensuring methodological reproducibility and transparent 
comparison between DL and ML approaches.
3. Results
The six case studies presented below progressively address 
increasing levels of diagnostic complexity, from global bolt preload 
assessment to coupled bolt – sleeper degradation and remote support 
monitoring. The 6 predictive maintenance (PdM) strategies investi -
gated, are summarized in Table 1 and detailed in the following sub -
sections. They differ primarily in their PdM objectives: assessing joint 
integrity under varying bolt preload conditions and evaluating sleeper- 
stiffness degradation near the rail joint. Cases 3.1 and 3.2, which address 
the PdM of bonded IRJs under variable bolt-preload conditions, 
constitute a natural extension of the SHM framework originally intro -
duced in [16] . Unlike the earlier formulation, where the DT was coupled 
solely with a ML algorithm, these cases broaden the framework to 
accommodate more complex operational scenarios.
Following the development of the DT of the bonded IRJ ( Section 2.1 ), 
FE simulations are performed to generate the datasets used in each 
predictive approach. The number of simulations per model is reported in 
Table 1 , with additional comments provided where relevant. Each 
dataset is split into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets, ensuring 
balanced class distributions and wheel-load levels to support robust AI- 
model training. In Cases 3.1 – 3.4, gap values and local vertical dis -
placements near the joint serve as predictive indicators of structural 
condition. In contrast, Cases 3.5 – 3.6 employ alternative monitoring 
strategies that rely solely on vertical displacement.
The resulting data are processed for labelling and conversion into 
time-frequency representations, spectrograms and scalograms, used to 
train and test the STFT-CNN and WT-CNN, respectively, or for pre -
processing prior to input to the ML classifier. Table 1 also lists the 
number of predictive classes and corresponding labels, which are fully 
described in the individual subsections. Briefly, the safe class represents 
IRJs with correct bolt preload and nominal sleeper conditions; classes 
marked B* identify loosened-bolt configurations, while classes marked 
S* denote sleepers with stiffness degradation ( Fig. 3 ).
Model performance is evaluated using the metrics defined in Section 
2.2.3 . Across all applications, the predictive models demonstrate high 
accuracy, confirming both the maturity and practical relevance of the 
proposed methodologies. These results further emphasize the strategic 
importance of advancing digitalization and smart maintenance strate -
gies for railway infrastructure. A comprehensive discussion of the results 
is presented in Section 4 .

reported in Section 2 of Supplementary Material for completeness.

3.1. Bolt preload assessment under multiple wheel loads
This case study investigates the capability of the proposed DT – AI 
framework to identify progressive bolt-preload degradation in bonded 
IRJs under varying operational loading conditions.
3.1.1. Predictive objective and dataset preparation
The model evaluates the structural health of bonded IRJs by ana -
lysing bolt preload conditions under three wheel-loads (60, 90, and 120 
kN). Digital Twin simulations yielded a balanced dataset of 360 samples 
based on two predictive indicators: gap values and vertical displace -
ments near the IRJ.
Four structural health classes were defined based on mechanically 
interpretable degradation states derived from the physics-informed 
Digital Twin, consistent with the framework introduced in [16] . Class 
boundaries were established by progressively reducing bolt preload and 
analysing the resulting changes in joint stiffness, gap value, and vertical 
displacement under wheel passage. The safe class corresponds to the 
nominal assembly condition (75 kN preload per bolt). The warning and 
risk classes represent controlled reductions to approximately 66.6% and 
33.3% of nominal preload, respectively, modelling progressive 
clamping-force loss and the associated reduction in joint flexural stiff -
ness. The fault class represents severe degradation with two missing 
bolts, producing marked alterations in load-transfer mechanisms.
Multiple bolt layouts were analysed (e.g., central bolts B23, outer 
bolts B14, alternating pairs B13/B24, individual-rail connections B12/ 
B34, and the full set B1234). As supported by Table 3 , Figs. S1 – S2, and 
the findings of [16] , these configurations produce similar feature sig -
natures within each class, justifying their grouping. A more granular 
classifier could detect specific loosened configurations but would 
require additional training data, as explored in Section 3.2 .
Digital Twin simulations revealed monotonic, mechanically 
consistent amplification of gap values and vertical displacement with 
decreasing preload, confirming progressive reduction of joint bending 
stiffness and altered load-transfer mechanisms. These distinct response 
patterns provide a robust basis for defining discrete structural health 
classes suitable for supervised learning.
Figs. 5 a and 6 a show representative gap and vertical displacement 
responses under a 60 kN wheel load. Fig. 5 b – e presents spectrograms 
and scalograms of gap values for safe and risk conditions, while Fig. 6 b – c 
shows time – frequency analyses of vertical displacement in the safe case. 
Numerical values at four critical wheel positions, P1 (first wheel on the 
joint), P2 (wheels symmetric about the joint centreline), P3 (second 
wheel on the joint), and P4 (residual displacement after both wheels at 
0.7 s), are reported in Table 2 a – c and were used as ML training inputs 
[16] . Both gap and vertical displacement increase monotonically with 
decreasing preload, with the largest deviations occurring at P1 and P3 
during direct wheel – joint interaction. At P2, the fault condition exhibits 
distinct amplification relative to other classes.
It is emphasized that the purpose of this classification is not to 
establish field-ready maintenance thresholds, which would require 
extensive experimental calibration and regulatory validation, but rather 
to provide a structured and physically grounded representation of pro -
gressive degradation states for DT – AI framework development.

3.1.3. Misclassification analysis and physical interpretation
A qualitative analysis of classification errors confirms that mis -
classifications arise from physically meaningful sources rather than 
algorithmic deficiencies. Adjacent-class errors reflect the subtle impact 
of partial bolt loosening and amplitude differences between neigh -
bouring degradation levels. The Digital Twin generates mechanically 
ordered degradation regimes, enabling classifiers to establish physically 
interpretable decision boundaries.
From a safety perspective, the error structure is predominantly 
conservative, with rare underestimation of severe damage. False posi -
tives (overestimating degradation) trigger precautionary inspection, 
while false negatives (underestimating damage) are minimal. This 
conservative bias ensures the framework's suitability for automated 
predictive maintenance in safety-critical infrastructure, maintaining a 
clear prioritization of operational safety over occasional inspection 
redundancy.
3.1.4. Transferability and robustness under real-world uncertainty
The near-perfect classification performance reported in Sections 
3.1.2 – 3.1.3 is an upper-bound benchmark, reflecting mechanically 
consistent, noise-free Digital Twin simulations. Real-world variability, 
including sensor noise, placement tolerances, material heterogeneity, 
environmental effects, and vehicle – track interaction fluctuations, may 
partially reduce feature separability and increase misclassification rates, 
particularly for adjacent degradation states (e.g., safe vs. early- 
warning).
Nevertheless, the primary degradation mechanisms, bolt preload loss 
and associated stiffness reduction, are well-established drivers of IRJ 
deterioration and are accurately reproduced by the validated Digital 
Twin. Prior experimental studies [16] demonstrate that classifiers 
trained on such synthetic data retain predictive relevance under real 
measurements, providing initial evidence of transferability.
The Digital Twin thus serves as a structured baseline for physically 
interpretable, systematically labelled training, enabling robust feature 
learning before exposure to operational uncertainties. Future de -
velopments will integrate laboratory and field measurements via hybrid 
virtual – physical updating and transfer-learning strategies, progressively 
introducing noise and environmental variability. This staged approach is 
expected to enhance model generalization while preserving mechanical 
interpretability.
Accordingly, the results presented in this section demonstrate 
intrinsic separability of degradation states under controlled conditions, 
providing a reproducible benchmark for subsequent field validation and 
operational deployment.
