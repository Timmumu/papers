Abstract 18 
Aging tower cranes face an elevated risk of failure, primarily due to structural fatigue and 19 
deterioration. Surprisingly, the degradation of aging-induced lifting capacity (LC) remains an 20 
unexplored domain. In response to this research gap, this paper introduces a digital twin-driven 21 
(DTD) framework and model to predict the degraded LC of aging tower cranes . This 22 
framework combines theoretical and numerical analysis of fatigue and degradation behavior in 23 
tower cranes with real-time vibration data obtained during cyclic load scenarios on the actual 24 
cranes. Machine learning (ML) techniques are employed to develop a model that accurately 25 
predicts the degraded LC caused by aging. A scaled-down tower crane prototype is adopted as 26 
a demonstrative case to illustrate the feasibility and effectiveness of the DTD framework. The 27 
DTD model predicts the degraded LC of the prototype with high accuracy, achieving a mean-28 
square error (MSE) of 0.2253 and a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.9973. The predicted 29 
degraded load charts of the tested tower crane for each decade of usage from 0 to 70 years are 30 
also presented to assist crane operators in applying safe loads, preventing unexpected failures 31 
and damages, and enhancing workplace monitoring and safety. This study helps monitor the 32 
safety conditions of tower cranes that are aging and susceptible to structural fatigue and 33 
deterioration, facilitates the prediction of the deterioration of complex machines and systems 34 
in the construction industry with real-time data, and highlights the potential of DTD approaches 35 
in improving efficiency, safety, and decision-making. 36

1. Introduction 39 
Tower cranes are essential in modern construction projects, facilitating the lifting and 40 
moving of hefty modules, equipment, and tools from lifting points to target locations on 41 
construction sites [1]. Over prolonged periods of use, however, aging tower cranes may 42 
experience structural fatigue, corrosion, and deterioration due to exposure to high -cycle and 43 
variable-amplitude loads, harsh working environments, and improper operation and 44 
maintenance, leading to degraded lifting capacity (LC) [2–8]. Such degradation can increase 45 
the risk of accidents and jeopardize the safety of workers and nearby properties, resulting in 46 
severe injuries or fatalities [9]. For instance, in 2022, the tower crane at the "S au Mau Ping" 47 
construction site in Hong Kong collapsed and killed three workers, where the operator set the 48 
manufacturer-specified load without considering the deteriorated load and /or capacity [10]. 49 
Similar incidents have occurred worldwide, including a crane collapse that killed 18 people in 50 
2016 in Dongguan, China [11], and another crane collapse that killed seven people and injured 51 
dozens more in 2008 in New York City in the United States [12]. Thus, there is still a practical 52 
need for real-time safety monitoring systems to detect and prevent accidents caused by aging 53 
tower cranes with degraded LC. 54 
Various safety monitoring systems have been developed and adopted in the construction 55 
industry to address crane safety, including load moment indicators (LMIs) [13–16], anti-two-56 
blocking (ATB) systems [17, 18], and crane cameras [19, 20]. LMIs measure the load on the 57 
crane and provide a warning when the load is near or exceeds the crane's max imum LC [13]. 58 
Anti-two-block systems prevent the crane from collapsing by limiting its operation when the 59 
hook block is too close to the boom tip [18]. Crane cameras monitor the crane's movement and 60 
detect unsafe behavior by recording a visual perspective [20]. However, while these systems 61 
can improve safety on construction sites, they are generally not designed to address the specific 62 
issue of aging tower cranes with degraded LC. For instance, LMIs, ATB systems , and crane 63 
cameras do not consider the effects of wear and tear on crane components or the health level 64 
of the crane and, thus, fail to predict the internal degradation in LC, which can result in 65 
unexpected accidents due to unintentional over-loading. Furthermore, due to the unavailability 66 
of historical data on crane operations, the complexity of the deterioration mechanism itself, and 67 
the unpredictability of external deterioration-related factors, it remains an open question of how 68 
to estimate and update the aging-induced LC degradation. 69 
Many studies have been conducted to tackle the issues of data availability and deterioration 70 
mechanism in understanding the degraded LC of tower cranes, which can be categorized into 71 
two groups: (1) data-driven methods (DDM) and (2) model-based methods (MBM). DDM uses 72 
real-time, historical, and maintenance data [21] to predict deterioration patterns [22]. Several 73 
studies have used DDM to predict performance degradation in tower cranes [23]. However, 74 
DDM is only effective when data are abundant, and without proper domain knowledge of the 75 
deterioration mechanisms, it may be less reliable when applied practically. On the other hand, 76 
MBM requires an understanding of failure mechanisms such as cracks, wear, deflection, 77 
friction, and stress. One can estimate performance degradation by developing a mathematical 78 
model that quantitatively describes the crane ’s degradation. However, extensive experiments 79 
are required for parameter identification, varying from case to case. Due to the complex nature 80 
3 
 
of tower cranes, MBM may be less efficient. To overcome the limitations of DDM and MBM, 81 
Liu et al. [24] proposed a hybrid approach that combines both methods (DDM -MBM). This 82 
approach collects data from mu ltiple sensors since a single sensor cannot capture all the 83 
relevant parameters that affect degradation. The use of multiple sensors may increase 84 
complexity, but it results in more accurate predictions. Additionally, the mechanical model 85 
used in MBM can simulate the structural performance of the tower crane, which increases the 86 
interpretability of DDM. Therefore, a hybrid approach that integrates multi -sensor data with 87 
an interpretable model could be a possible solution to enable real -time monitoring and 88 
prediction of tower cranes’ safety conditions considering degraded LC. 89 
As an instance of hybrid DDM -MBM methods, the digital twin-driven (DTD) approach 90 
creates a replica of a physical system , aiming to enable real-time data acquisition, response 91 
prediction, and human-machine interaction (HMI) [25]. The DTD approach has been widely 92 
utilized across various studies f or various applications, including product design, 93 
manufacturing, planning, maintenance, management, control, traceability, anomaly detection, 94 
operational errors, and safety analysis [26–36]. For instance, in a study by Yang et al. [37], a 95 
hybrid DTD approach was used to predict the performance degradation in the transmission unit 96 
of CNC machines by incorporating damage indices with wear data from Achard wear theory. 97 
Similarly, Tran et al. [23] predicted machine degradatio n by considering both the present 98 
conditions of the machine and degradation indices based on a year of data. However, these 99 
methods are time-consuming and primarily applicable only to those tools. Despite the extensive 100 
use of DT in the degradation field, there is limited research exploring the potential of the DTD 101 
approach in predicting the deterioration of complex machines and systems in the construction 102 
industry using real -time data. Nevertheless, the mentioned examples from adjacent fields 103 
highlight the flexibility and potential of the DTD approach in improving efficiency, safety, and 104 
decision-making in various applications. 105 
Aiming to address the real-time safety monitoring and alerting of aging tower cranes, this 106 
study develops a DTD framework and model to predict degraded LC after different usage 107 
periods. The framework integrates theoretical and numerical analyses of tower crane fatigue 108 
and degradation behavior with real -time vibration data obtained during cyclic load scenarios 109 
on the physical tower crane. Machine learning (ML) models, including Random Forest (RF) 110 
and Support Vector Machine (SVM) , are employed for feature selection and LC prediction . 111 
Specifically, a scaled -down tower crane prototype is adopted as a demonstrative case to 112 
illustrate the feasibility and effectiveness of the DTD framework. The predicted degraded load 113 
charts of the prototype for each decade of usage from 0 to 70 years are presented for validation 114 
purposes. The proposed DTD framework allows for continuous monitoring of the crane's safety 115 
performance considering the degradation in LC with real-time data and numerical model, 116 
which is expected to significantly improve safety in construction sites with aging tower cranes. 117

existing research and studies related to the topic ; Section 3 presents a DTD framework for 119 
monitoring machine deterioration , explicitly focusing on the theoretical background of DTD 120 
approaches and numerical analysis of tower crane fatigue accumulation and degradation in LC; 121 
and Section 4 outlines the process of developing a DTD model for tower crane degradation 122

2. Literature review 127 
2.1. Crane safety in construction projects 128 
Construction sites are widely recognized as challenging environments due to the 129 
simultaneous and complex nature of multiple hazardous activities. These activities involve the 130 
movement of laborers, heavy machinery, and construction materials, thereby amplifying the 131 
associated risks [38]. Cranes, being a crucial piece of equipment in construction, not only play 132 
a significant role but also pose substantial health and safety concerns for their operators. 133 
Previous research has focused on understanding the causes of injuries and fatalities related 134 
to various crane activities, including layout planning, installation/dismantling, erection, and 135 
operations. These studies aimed to identify the key factors contributing to crane accidents. For 136 
instance, Sulankivi et al. [39] investigated safety issues in crane layout planning, whereas Shin 137 
[40] identified the primary factors influencing crane safety during installation and dismantling 138 
processes. Chang et al. [41] used 3D simulation and visualization techniques to improve safety 139 
during crane erection. Sadeghi et al. [42] developed a framework for ensemble risk analysis to 140 
enhance the safety of crane operations. Shapira et al. [43] identified 20 factors associated with 141 
crane safety, including project conditions, environmental factors, human factors, and safety 142 
management. Finally, Lee et al. [9] found that most crane accidents occur because of human 143 
error and crane-related issues. Crane-related problems include structural fatigue, wear and tear, 144 
stress, strain, cracks, corrosion, deterioration, and the risk of structural collapse. These issues 145 
arise from exposure to cyclic and varying loads, challenging working conditions, and 146 
inadequate operation and maintenance practices. As a result, the LC of the cranes decreased 147 
over time. Research studies are essential to identify and quantify the degradation of LC caused 148 
by the aging of these cranes. 149 
2.2. Analytical approach for crane degradation 150 
Analytical methods use tools, models, mathematical equations, and formulas to address 151 
complex problems. In crane assessment, these techniques are applied to evaluate and predict 152 
factors such as structural integrity, LC, and overall safety. This approach involves 153 
understanding failure mechanisms such as cracks, wear and tear, deflection, friction, and stress, 154 
identifying critical components, considering environmental conditions, and analyzing 155 
historical data. Relevant information regarding crane usage, environmental factors, and 156 
material properties is collected and organized for analysis. Monitoring indicators of early 157 
degradation throughout the crane's operational lifespan can help detect potential issues in 158 
advance. 159 
Researchers have studied various critical components of the crane to investigate specific 160 
failure mechanisms. For instance, Vukelic et al. [44] investigated the failure mechanism of 161 
crane gear damage, while Guerra -Fuentes et al. [45] studied the failure analysis of steel wire 162 
ropes in overhead crane systems. Das et al. [7] reviewed the failure analysis of hooks, and Wu 163 
5 
 
et al. [46] developed models for fatigue damage accumulation in critical components exposed 164 
to moving loads. Experimental and numerical methods are employed to examine component 165 
behavior and identify the ro ot causes of failure. Experimental techniques involve visual 166 
inspections, chemical composition analysis, mechanical properties, and hardness tests . 167 
Numerical methods, such as finite element analysis (FEA), are utilized to calculate stress/strain 168 
and load/deflection, providing insights into the structural performance of crane components. 169 
However, it is essential to acknowledge that the analytical method has limitations. It requires 170 
a comprehensive understanding of each parameter used in the model and extens ive 171 
experimental work to comprehend the failure mechanisms of individual components. 172 
Therefore, this method may be less effective for complex machines like cranes , as it relies on 173 
assumptions and suppositions regarding various parameters. 174 
2.3. Data-driven method for crane degradation 175 
A data-driven method (DDM) analyzes and interprets data to address problems and make 176 
informed decisions. It utilizes statistical, machine learning (ML), or artificial intelligence (AI) 177 
techniques to collect and scrutinize relevant data related to the degradation of tower cranes. 178 
The main goals of this approach are to monitor the operational state and performance of the 179 
cranes, predict potential issues or failures, and optimize maintenance and operational strategies. 180 
Various types of data are considered, including operational data ( e.g., load, usage, and 181 
environmental conditions), sensor data (e.g., vibration and temperature), maintenance records, 182 
and historical failure data. A thorough analysis of this data is conducted to identify patterns 183 
and trends. 184 
For example, Tran et al. [23] conducted a study where they employed a support vector 185 
machine (SVM) to predict machine degradation and estimate the remaining useful life (RUL) 186 
of machines. They incorporated monitoring and operational data, along with degradation 187 
indices, to establish a failure threshold for decision -making. However, it is important to note 188 
that the effectiveness of the DD M approach relies on having sufficient data available, and 189 
without a solid understanding of the specific domain's deterioration mechanisms, its practical 190 
application may be less reliable. Additionally, the data in this particular study was sourced 191 
from only one or two sensors installed over the course of a year. 192 
To overcome this issue, Roman et al. [22] introduced a hybrid data -driven fuzzy active 193 
disturbance rejection control system for tower cranes. Similarly, Liu et al. [24] utilized hybrid 194 
deep neura l networks to assess machine performance based on fatigue data. The hybrid 195 
approach involves collecting data from multiple sensors, but it can result in complex and 196 
computationally intensive modeling due to the increased data volume. This is where the digital 197 
twin-driven (DTD) method comes into play, as it allows for real -time data acquisition, 198 
prediction of responses, and interaction between humans and machines. 199 
2.4. Digital twin-driven method for crane degradation 200 
A digital twin -driven (DTD) approach utilizes digital twin technology to monitor the 201 
operational condition, performance, and potential degradation of a complex physical system, 202 
such as tower cranes. A digital twin is a virtual replica of the physical object created through 203 
advanced data analytics, simulation, and modeling techniques. Researchers have applied DTD 204 
6 
 
technology to address various machine performance degradation problems. For instance, 205 
Deebak et al. [26] used the DTD method for fault diagnosis in machining tool conditions . In 206 
contrast, Zhu et al. [32] used it to control the machining process for thin -walled part 207 
manufacturing. Zhang et al. [47] utilized DTD for surface roughness prediction, and Liu et al. 208 
[29] employed it for traceability and dynamic control of machine processes. Zhuang et al. [48] 209 
utilized DTD to predict wear in the turning process of machines. Jiang et al. [25] focused on 210 
the stability analysis of the tower crane hoisting process by examining stress conditions during 211 
various hoisting scenarios. Finally, Yang et al. [37] used DTD for performance degradation in 212 
the transmission unit of CNC machine tools. However, their approach involved incorporating 213 
damage indices with wear data from a specific model, making it time-consuming, complex, 214 
and applicable only to that particular tool. Therefore, this stud y proposes a method for 215 
predicting degradation in aging tower cranes based on real-time data, offering a more efficient 216 
and versatile approach. 217 
3. Methodology 218 
3.1. A digital twin-driven framework for degradation in machines 219 
The primary goal of this study is to use a DTD approach to predict degradation in machines, 220 
such as tower cranes. A DTD framework is formulated based on the mapping between physical 221 
and virtual machines, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This framework aims to (1) provide background 222 
information, (2) highlight issues, (3) define data requirements, (4) simplify mapping problems 223 
between virtual-physical spaces, (5) establish two-way communications between all parts, and 224 
(6) make decisions and feedback for improvement. A DTD approach for degradation must have 225 
five parts: physical space, virtual space, IoT-based connection, real-time database, and control 226 
system [49–51]. Machine degradation is predicted by mapping or fusing the DTD components 227

3.1.1. Physical space 231 
The physical space module encompasses all the physical activities, operational processes, 232 
and work environments contributing to degradation monitoring [25]. To enhance the accuracy 233

Once the necessary data from both the physical and virtual space is collected and a two -way 242

monitoring, control, safety, and improvement. The physical space module represents the 244 
environment in which the tower crane operates, encompassing the crane structure, components, 245

role in capturing real-time operational data and physical conditions. 247

3.1.3. IoT-based connection 263 
In accordance with ISO/OSI communication standards, the communication system for the 264 
DTD model can be implemented using either wired or wireless technologies [56]. To establish 265 
real-time data transfer and parsing, an IoT-based connection is utilized. This connection serves 266 
as a crucial link that facilitates two-way communication between physical and virtual spaces. 267 
It ensures that all components of the DTD model are integrated and appropriately mapped to 268 
detect and respond to real-time changes in the physical space, as well as to receive feedback 269

transmission between the physical machine and the virtual machine in both directions. Its 271 
significance lies in establishing connectivity between the physical and virtual spaces, thereby 272 
enabling seamless data flow and real -time synchronization between the physical tower crane 273 
and its digital twin. 274 
3.1.4. Real-time digital twin database 275 
Real-time data from the digital twin is utilized to integrate the multi-dimensional and multi-276 
scale virtual simulation model with the physical model and control system . Data serves as the 277 
foundational concept of the DTD model, enabling the virtual model to execute operations and 278 
make informed decisions. This module is crucial in interpreting, classifying, storing, pre -279 
processing, maintaining, and testing data [26]. This module is responsible for storing and 280 
managing the data collected from both the physical crane and its digital twin. The module ’s 281 
significance has been detailed, highlighting its role in storing historical data, enabling 282 
comparison and analysis, and suppor ting predictive maintenance decision -making. The DTD 283 
model can effectively access and utilize the collected data for various tasks and processes by 284 
leveraging this module. 285

The control system within the DTD model plays a vital role in regulating and predicting 287

analyzing, visualizing, making decisions, and providing feedback for improvement [47]. It 290 
consists of two main components : (1) data pre-processing and (2) a functional module. The 291

3.2. Fatigue behavior and deterioration in cranes 304 
Tower cranes are considered to be structures prone to fatigue and are at risk of collapsing 305 
during operation [24, 59]. In terms of fatigue behavior , tower cranes are classified as high-306 
cycle and super-high-cycle fatigue structures based on the S-N curve, also known as the fatigue 307 
curve or Wohler curve [60]. The fatigue curve represents the relationship between the cyclic 308 
stress amplitude and the number of cycles until failure. To analyze fatigue behavior in tower 309 
cranes, various approaches can be employed, including stress-life, strain-life, non-destructive, 310 
fracture mechanics, and damage mechanics. 311 
The stress-life approach involves plotting stress against the number of cycles until failure 312 
[61]. However, this approach requires extensive experimental data, and a single stress-life plot 313 
has limitations when it comes to predicting plasticity, deformation, and mean stress. Similarly, 314 
the strain-life method entails plotting strain against the number of cycles until failure [62]. This 315 
approach requires the stress-strain curve and consideration of plasticity nucleation to determine 316 
the transition life between plastic and elastic regions under fatigue loading . Therefore, it is 317 
mainly applicable when determining the crack initiation life of the crane structure. Non-318 
destructive techniques can be used to study fatigue by measuring and analyzing inherent 319 
properties such as cracks, strength reduction, and stiffness degradation [63]. However, this 320 
method necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the relevant parameters and can be 321 
challenging to cover all aspects [24]. In fracture mechanics, the focus is on pre-existing flaws 322 
and micro-cracks in the structure [64]. The degradation rate is determined using a crack growth 323 
law and stress intensity factor as a function of crack depth, shape factor, and stress range . 324 
However, our study doesn't consider crane sections with pre-existing defects. Finally, damage 325 
mechanics use appropriate assessment models, laws, and patterns to characterize damages, 326 
residual stresses, and strain localization [60, 64–66]. Damage mechanics is a suitable approach 327 
for addressing tower crane degradation as it encompasses critical factors that directly and 328 
indirectly influence crane degradation. For example, micro-plastic strain is often overlooked in 329 
low-cycle fatigue problems, but it must be considered in the case of high-cycle or super-high-330 
cycle fatigue damage within the elastic range, as is the case with tower cranes. Macro-plastic 331 
strain can result from such scenarios, leading to structural deterioration under long -term 332 
operational loading. 333 
10 
 
While steel structures can have an infinite design life if the stress range is low, tower cranes 334 
are prone to fatigue due to the cyclic and varied stress amplitudes experienced by their 335 
components during operation. For instance, when a tower crane lifts a heavy load at full stretch 336 
and lifting radius, it experiences a higher stress range. Additionally, the loads exerted on the 337 
crane are cyclic and have varying amplitudes. As a result, e lastic deformation occurs in the 338 
crane structure, generating stress and accumulat ing residual stresses over time. When the 339 
residual stresses reach a critical threshold, cracks form, posing a collapse risk for the crane . 340 
Therefore, measuring the loads and the associated deflection, deformation, and vibration of the 341 
physical tower crane is crucial to predicting degradation accurately. 342 
3.2.1. Fatigue damage accumulation analysis for cranes 343 
There are two main approaches for evaluating fatigue damage: (1) constant amplitude 344 
fatigue loading and (2) variable amplitude fatigue loading . In the case of a tower crane, the 345 
loads it experiences are stochastic and cyclic, causing fatigue damage accumulation to vary 346 
with each loading cycle. As a result, the traditional linear cumulative damage criterion is not 347 
applicable in the context of tower cranes [67]. Instead, non-linear and double-linear damage 348 
rules are used to account for variable amplitude loads [68]. 349 
During the progression of variable amplitude fatigue loading, the tower crane structure 350 
undergoes i rreversible deformation s and accumulates residual stress es. These factors 351 
contribute to the initiation and propagation of cracks within the structure. The presence of 352 
residual stresses causes cracks to propagate along a particular plane once initiated. These 353 
irreversible local deformations and crack propagation significantly impact the overall stability 354 
and reliability of the tower crane. 355 
Various parameters can be employed to analyze the damage occurring in the tower crane 356 
structure, including total strain, residual stress, stiffness degradation, strength degradation, heat 357 
dissipation resulting from micro -cracking, crack propagation, and the speed of sound. In our 358 
study, we simulate the mechanical response of the crane structure by considering stress, 359 
displacement, and damage index while applying a moving load with variable amplitude. 360 
Existing literature [24, 46, 60, 64–71] indicates that the damage rate in the tower crane is 361

In previous studies, Yang et al. [37] and Tran et al. [23] employed a hybrid model (DDM 391

models, physical scale model s allow for experimental testing of the crane’s operation and 445 
control process. This enables easier detection of errors and deviations under various working 446 
conditions. He et al. [52] suggested that integrated modeling, simulation, and scale models can 447 
improve the structural analysis of tower cranes, predict failure modes, dynamic analysis, and 448 
operation processes. This previous research serves as the basis for the proposed DTD model, 449

4.1.1. Fundamental data from the physical tower crane 468 
Data is the most essential and fundamental concept in developing a DTD model. In addition 469 
to sensors, other data types could be included to enhance the quality of a DT D model, 470 
particularly in the degradation scenario . As illustrated in Fig. 6, the tower crane generates 471 
multi-source heterogeneous data classified into six categories based on their type and nature: 472 
static, dynamic, specification, historical, environmental, and comprehensive data. Static data 473 
(SD) is data that does not change with time. Although SD does not directly influence 474 
degradation, it can affect quality if altered. In contrast, dynamic data (DD) varies with time. 475 
DD provides information about the crane's dynamics, lifting operation, degradation, and real -476 
time operation status . DD is more important than other data types for developing the DT D 477 
model. As a result, it requires high accuracy, quality, and timeliness. DD consists of sensors 478 
and built-in data. Furthermore, specification data (Sp-D) includes knowledge, standards, and 479 
load charts. Historical data (HD) refers to data from inspections, monitoring, and maintenance 480 
reports during the service life. Besides this, environmental data (ED) refers to external working 481 
conditions, such as temperature, wind, and humidity. Finally, comprehensive data (CD) is 482 
additional data other than the previously mentioned data that provides essential and specific 483 
information to the DTD model for enhancing prediction and decision-making. In addition, 484 
simulation data is required for the DTD model, which we will explain in Section 4.2. Please 485 
note that w e have developed a DTD model that utilizes real-time sensor data (DD data) and 486 
incorporates other data types in specific tasks . This integration enhances the quality and 487 
accuracy of assessing the degradation of tower cranes. 488

4.2. Virtual tower crane 491 
The virtual model of the tower crane serves as a simulation platform for analyzing its 492 
structure and operation s. It accurately replicates the lifting operations of the actual crane, 493 
considering factors such as deterioration over time. The ABAQUS software is utilized to create 494 
a FEM model of the tower crane, enabling effective numerical simulations to determine the 495 
capacity of its structural components in terms of load and deflection. The analysis takes into 496 
account the working conditions of a scaled -down model, considering static, dynamic, and 497 
moving mass on the crane. All factors relevant to the lifting operations and degradation are 498 
incorporated as real-time external data in the virtual model. 499 
The tower crane is connected to its foundation through a baseplate and anchor bolts, which 500 
bear the bending moment and res trict movement in four nod al points and six degrees of 501 
freedom (DOF). The connection between the mast and jib section is assumed to be rigid, while 502 
the junction between the mast, tower cap, and pivoting support is consolidated. The jib and 503 
counter jib are hinged to the mast. Finite element analysis (FEA) is performed using various 504 
loads, including dead, service, wind, and concentrated loads. This analysis helps determine the 505 
LC of the tower crane by examining a force/deflection or stress/strain curve. Additionally, a 506 
fatigue damage accumulation analysis is conducted to assess the rate of damage and 507 
degradation over a 70-year period. The ABAQUS software stores all relevant information 508 
about the lifting operation, including component geometry, mechanical data, operation al 509 
process, and environmental conditions. Finally, the degradation rate is integrated and used in 510 
conjunction with real -time sensor data in the control system of the DT D model to predict 511 
degradation in the LC of a tower crane, as explained in Section 3.3. 512
