 A B S T R A C T
Historic urban centers face unique evacuation challenges due to dense morphology, limited network re-
dundancy, and cultural heritage constraints that restrict infrastructural modification. This study presents an 
exploratory, decision-support analysis of evacuation dynamics in the walled Old Town of Nicosia, Cyprus, 
using a high-fidelity digital twin and a hybrid agent-based simulation framework integrating pedestrian and 
vehicular movement. Rather than modeling a specific hazard, the study evaluates how structural stressors 
commonly produced by disasters, including exit closures, road blockages, and shelter saturation, influence 
evacuation clearance, congestion formation, and overall system robustness. Controlled simulation experiments 
examine the sensitivity of evacuation performance to network disruptions, the effectiveness of existing and 
expanded shelter configurations, and the role of population familiarity and guidance in constrained historic 
environments. Results show that evacuation outcomes are highly sensitive to a small number of critical exits 
and that uncoordinated increases in shelter capacity, without adaptive guidance, may increase congestion 
and clearance times. Findings also reveal nonlinear congestion dynamics and long-tailed evacuation delays 
that persist under idealized conditions. The study demonstrates the value of high-resolution digital twins 
as exploratory tools for identifying structural vulnerabilities, evaluating mitigation strategies, and supporting 
risk-informed evacuation planning in historic districts exposed to multiple hazards.
1. Introduction
Historic urban centers embody a persistent tension between cultural 
heritage preservation and urban vulnerability. Dense morphology, lim-
ited network redundancy, and regulatory constraints on structural mod-
ification often restrict the range of feasible risk-reduction interventions. 
As a result, evacuation planning in such environments must operate 
within spatial and institutional limitations that differ fundamentally 
from those of modern cities.
This tension is particularly evident in the walled Old Town of 
Nicosia, Cyprus. Enclosed by 16th-century Venetian fortifications and 
partially bordered by a militarized buffer zone, the historic core is 
accessible through only six operational gates. Its irregular medieval 
street network, narrow corridors, pedestrian-only segments, and one-
way constraints create asymmetric flow patterns and limited alternative 
routing options. While these features contribute to the city’s heritage 
value, they also introduce structural bottlenecks that can severely 
constrain evacuation under emergency conditions.

high-resolution, data-driven evacuation modeling at city scale. Pre-

pedestrian–vehicle interactions [1]. Consequently, a gap persists in 
understanding how historic morphology, limited exit redundancy, and 
real-time decision-making jointly influence evacuation performance in 
constrained heritage environments.
More broadly, while urban evacuation modeling has advanced sub-

of fortification-imposed exits, one-way circulation, a militarized zone

1.1. Multi-hazard evacuation challenges in historic cities
Historic districts are rarely exposed to a single hazard type, and 
risk often emerges from interacting or cascading events [1,4]. Earth-
quakes may generate debris-induced road blockages and façade col-
lapses, while floods introduce depth-dependent walkability constraints 
and progressive route loss [5]. In morphologically constrained his-
toric environments, such hazard-specific effects interact with limited 
network redundancy, potentially producing nonlinear congestion and 
long-tailed evacuation delays.
Although the present study does not simulate a specific hazard 
process, it focuses on structural stressors that commonly arise across 
hazard types: gate closures, road blockages, and shelter saturation. By 
abstracting from hazard triggers while retaining their systemic conse-
quences, the analysis provides hazard-agnostic insights into evacuation 
vulnerability in historic environments. The framework is intentionally 
extensible, enabling the integration of debris fields, flood extents, or 
fire spread models in future work.
Different hazards can create different evacuation conditions in 
dense historic environments. Earthquakes can cause sudden infrastruc-
ture damage and debris that block narrow streets. Floods gradually 
reduce road accessibility as water levels rise. Urban fires, in contrast, 
require rapid evacuation under strong time pressure and may lead to 
more chaotic and panic-driven movement patterns. Previous studies in 
historic city centers such as Florence (Italy) [1], Dubrovnik (Croatia) 
under seismic risk [6], and Beirut (Lebanon) in earthquake evacu-
ation simulations [7], as well as other Mediterranean heritage con-
texts [5], highlight how warning time, infrastructure disruption, and 
population behavioral responses vary, shaping evacuation dynamics 
and congestion formation.
1.2. Research questions
This study addresses the following research questions:
RQ1: How do limited exit availability and constrained street mor-
phology influence evacuation clearance times and congestion 
patterns in a historic walled city?
RQ2: How do shelter capacity and spatial distribution affect evacua-
tion performance under conditions of network stress?
RQ3: To what extent does population familiarity and guidance modify 
evacuation outcomes in dense heritage environments?
Rather than predicting evacuation outcomes for a specific disas-
ter scenario, the objective is to identify structural vulnerabilities and 
decision-relevant sensitivities that can inform risk-informed planning.
1.3. Study approach and contributions
To investigate these questions, we employ a high-fidelity, geo-
referenced 3D digital twin of Nicosia’s Old Town.1 The simulation 
framework integrates detailed spatial data with a hybrid agent-based 
model capable of representing both pedestrian and vehicular move-
ment within the same constrained environment. Pedestrian and vehicle 
agents operate on layered navigation meshes that encode one-way 
restrictions, pedestrian-only segments, and exit constraints. Behavioral 
parameters are calibrated using available traffic counts and density 
observations to approximate realistic movement dynamics.
A series of controlled simulation experiments was conducted un-
der generalized stress conditions, including selective gate closures, 
road blockages, and alternative shelter configurations. These scenarios 
isolate the effects of exit redundancy, network asymmetry, and informa-
tion availability on clearance performance and congestion formation. 
By integrating physically realistic agent behavior with accurate topo-
graphical data, we produced actionable insights for urban planners, 
emergency services, and policy-makers. Our simulation experiments 
reveal that evacuating Nicosia’s semicircular walled core can become 
significantly delayed under certain conditions, and that adding infras-
tructure, without first providing clear guidance to residents, fails to 
improve evacuation outcomes. The resulting simulation dataset offers 
a valuable foundation for future academic research in crowd dynamics 
and evacuation modeling. The contributions of this study are threefold:
• Development of a high-resolution digital twin of Nicosia’s Old 
Town that integrates geo-referenced road network data with a 
hybrid pedestrian–vehicle agent-based simulation framework.
• Systematic evaluation of evacuation performance under con-
trolled stress conditions, quantifying clearance times, congestion 
hotspots, and shelter saturation effects.
• Identification of structural sensitivities in historic evacuation 
topology, demonstrating that performance is dominated by a 
small number of critical exits and strongly mediated by guidance 
and information availability.
By positioning evacuation as a systemic interaction between urban 
morphology, infrastructure constraints, and behavioral adaptation, this 
work advances the use of digital twins as exploratory decision-support 
tools for disaster risk reduction in heritage cities.
2. Literature review and background
Evacuation planning in dense, historic urban environments presents 
distinct challenges that transcend conventional transportation and civil 
engineering paradigms. In particular, the irregular morphology, re-
stricted egress points, and mixed traffic patterns characteristic of her-
itage city centers demand a multidisciplinary approach.

In general, agent-based frameworks such as those presented in [8] 
and [9] have significantly advanced the realism of crowd movement 
by integrating psychological and perceptual models. These systems 
support dynamic obstacle avoidance, group behavior, and real-time 
replanning, key capabilities when simulating evacuation through con-
strained, evolving spaces. Narain et al. [10] and Karamouzas et al. [11] 
introduced data-driven and velocity-based crowd models, respectively, 
enabling smoother, more predictive simulations that scale efficiently to 
dense urban populations.
In terms of city evacuations scenarios, early contributions to pedes-
trian dynamics include the work of Helbing et al. [12], who introduced 
the Social Force Model (SFM), a continuous micro-simulation frame-
work that has since been widely adapted for emergency scenarios. 
Extensions of this model by Yang et al. [3] introduced behavioral 
heuristics, including panic escalation and adaptive exit selection, to 
better reflect decision-making in disaster contexts. Bernardini et al. 
[5] further coupled pedestrian exit models with flood simulations, 
demonstrating that historical morphologies can produce localized de-
viations in evacuation times exceeding 15%. Finally, works like [13] 
presents an agent-based simulation model that incorporates the con-
cept of affordance to analyze pedestrian evacuation behavior during 
emergencies.
Multi-modal evacuation, where both pedestrian and vehicular flows 
must be considered, introduces additional complexity. Zhang and Chang 
[14] proposed a dynamic network flow model that jointly optimizes 
evacuation routes while resolving pedestrian–vehicle conflicts at inter-
sections. Ronchi and Nilsson [15] integrated vehicle dynamics into the 
FDS+Evac environment, revealing that spontaneous mode-switching 
behaviors (e.g., abandoning vehicles mid-evacuation) can have major 
implications for system-wide throughput. Additional work by van den 
Berg et al. [16] in reciprocal collision avoidance has informed many 
graphics-based simulations where pedestrians and vehicles share space 
under time pressure.
The growing maturity of digital twin technologies has opened new 
possibilities for urban DRR. Frameworks such as [17] and [18] high-
light the role of 3D urban models and sensor feedback in resilience 
planning. From a participatory planning perspective, Wang et al. [19] 
integrates GIS data with public engagement tools, though few mod-
els achieve centimeter-scale resolution or simulate full-scale evacua-
tions. Notably, graphics platforms like Unity and Unreal Engine have 
been adapted to support high-fidelity, agent-based urban simulation, 
as demonstrated in [20] and [21], offering both visual realism and 
behavioral plausibility.
Our work builds on these foundations by integrating high-resolution 
spatial data, scenarios with pedestrian and vehicle simulations, and be-
havioral calibration into a real-time, interactive digital twin of Nicosia’s 
Old Town. This allows for the testing of multi-hazard evacuation sce-
narios under realistic constraints, such as blocked gates or roads, 
narrow corridors, etc. providing actionable insights for heritage city 
DRR planning.
3. Methodology
This section outlines the development and implementation of our 
simulation framework. We describe the 3D environment setup, network 
reconstruction, shelter modeling, agent behavior design, and traffic 
dynamics.
3.1. Simulation environment
All simulations were developed in Unity, which offers an integrated 
3-D renderer, physics solver, and AI navigation toolkit. The digital twin 
of Nicosia’s Old Town, taken from i-Nicosia, was imported as textured 
meshes, providing geo-referenced buildings, terrain, and infrastructure 
elements such as walls and street furniture. This model forms the spatial 
foundation for evacuation scenario testing.
3.2. Network reconstruction and nav. Layer
The road network was reconstructed using EasyRoads3D, manually 
aligning each segment with the twin geometry to preserve geometric ac-
curacy. The six historic gates were defined as the only legal exit points, 
reflecting the constraints imposed by the 16th-century fortifications. 
Due to the military buffer zone in the north, vehicles and pedestrians 
are restricted from crossing that boundary (as seen in Fig. 1).
To enforce one-way street constraints and prevent unrealistic rout-
ing, we used Unity’s AI Navigation system to build a detailed navigation 
mesh (nav-mesh), overlaid with directional off-mesh links that encode 
one-way restrictions. Note that, pedestrian-only paths were excluded 
from the vehicle navigation layer.
3.3. Shelter modeling
Shelters were represented as dynamic nodes within the simulation. 
Consultation with Cyprus Civil Defense confirmed two active shelters 
(with capacities of 180 and 200), and two additional sites pending 
activation. Each shelter was parameterized with its capacity, and agents 
were programmed to select the nearest shelter but redirect dynamically 
if capacity was exceeded. This redirection logic allowed for realistic 
modeling of queuing, spillover flows, and localized congestion effects.
3.4. Agent modeling
To simulate realistic evacuation behavior, we modeled two main 
agent types: pedestrians and vehicles. Each type is governed by distinct 
motion dynamics, perception rules, and decision-making processes. 
Both agent systems operate in the same navigable environment but 
respond differently to congestion, routing, and environmental cues.
3.4.1. Pedestrians
Pedestrian agents used Unity’s nav-mesh for global path planning 
and a lightweight motion controller for local steering and skeletal 
animation. Parameters such as acceleration, avoidance radii, and stop-
ping distance were manually calibrated to mimic real-world walking 
dynamics. To model varied familiarity with the city layout, and rep-
resent heterogeneous knowledge, we implemented a leader–follower 
mechanism: these agents follow a designated leader until nearing a 
gate or shelter, then switched to individual pathfinding. This behav-
ior generated emergent platoon dynamics, similar to tourist or group 
evacuation behavior.
3.4.2. Vehicles
Vehicle behavior posed unique challenges. Unity’s default nav-agent 
was initially adapted for cars, but this yielded unrealistic motion. 
Instead, we developed a custom CarController script. Each vehicle 
still used the nav-agent path for navigation, but actual motion was 
computed by applying physical forces and torques to a rigid body, 
producing more lifelike acceleration, deceleration, and turning. To 
avoid collisions, we combined Unity’s Reciprocal Velocity Obstacles 
(RVO) module with three front-facing ray-casts (Fig. 2), which detect 
obstructions and modulate throttle in real time.
Navigation decisions were made using a ‘‘fewest-corners’’ heuris-
tic rather than Euclidean distance, reflecting the complexity of one-
way grids. After running a number of simulations, we observed that 
strictly following these precomputed routes created unrealistic behav-
ior, as real drivers will often detour when they spot heavy traffic. 
To model real-time driver adaptation, we implemented congestion 
triggers: each street segment monitored its vehicle density and, if 
overloaded, increased its nav-mesh cost. Therefore, all streets have 
their own Navmesh area and a congestion threshold customized to 
its width and position. Approximately 25% of agents responded by 
recalculating their route, producing a plausible mix of compliant and 
opportunistic drivers.

3.5. Speed calibration
Pedestrian agents were assigned walking speeds from a normal 
distribution  (𝜇 = 8 km∕h , 𝜎 = 1 .5), ensuring diversity in crowd 
dynamics. This distribution was selected based on publicly available 
data on average adult running speeds over short distances (≤ 600
m), which corresponds to the mean evacuation distance required for 
agents to reach nearby exits. Vehicle agents were assigned a maximum 
velocity of 30 km/h, consistent with the speed limit within the Old City 
boundaries, with modest tail-end overruns permitted on uninterrupted 
segments to reflect realistic driving variability. These parameter choices 
were validated through repeated simulation trials to ensure consistency 
with observed pedestrian and vehicular behaviors.
4. Results
4.1. Implementation details
All experiments were implemented in Unity 6 Preview (6000.0.19f1, 
DX11). Pedestrian and vehicle routing used the Unity AI Navigation 
package (v2.0.4), and the road network geometry was generated with 
EasyRoads3D Free v3 (v3.2.4f3).
4.2. Experimental design
To examine evacuation vulnerability in Nicosia’s walled Old Town 
under generalized stress conditions, we conducted controlled simula-
tions aligned with the three research questions (RQ1–RQ3). Scenarios 
were executed with fixed time horizons (10 min for pedestrian-only 
trials and 15 min for vehicle trials) to enable consistent comparisons. 
The baseline demand consisted of 3000 pedestrian agents and 700 
vehicles, representing peak activity within the city walls. Population 
levels were informed by the Census of Population and Housing 2021 [22]. 
Agents were initialized on the street network and remained stationary 
until the evacuation signal was issued; the system was closed, with no 
new entries during each run.
To represent heterogeneity in route knowledge (RQ3), pedestrian 
scenarios were repeated across five leader-to-follower ratios (80:20, 
60:40, 50:50, 40:60, 20:80). Leaders represent informed agents capable 
of independent navigation, whereas followers rely on nearby leaders for 
guidance. Metrics reported below are averaged across runs within each 
scenario suite.

This section reports simulation outputs describing evacuation clear-

line and disruption scenarios. Interpretative implications are presented 
in Section 5.
Evacuation outcomes were dominated by the availability of a small 
subset of critical gates. When all exits were available, both pedestrian 
and vehicle evacuations progressed steadily, but clearance times exhib-
ited long-tailed distributions, indicating that a non-trivial portion of 
agents required substantially longer travel times to reach safety (Fig. 
3).

100–400 m and cleared within 100–200 s (Figs. 3(a)–3(b)). Leader

contact under congestion. Exit-use patterns were strongly skewed, with

5. Discussion
The results highlight structural evacuation sensitivities that are 
characteristic of walled historic districts: asymmetric street topology, 
limited exit redundancy, and strong dependence on information flow. 
Rather than reflecting average performance under ideal conditions, 
the experiments emphasize how localized failures (e.g., a single gate 
closure) can trigger disproportionate, system-wide delays, and how 
shelter provision can unintentionally reshape flows in ways that in-
crease congestion. These effects are also reflected in the long-tailed

5.1. Exit dominance and nonlinear sensitivity
A central finding is that evacuation performance is dominated by a 
small number of critical gates. Both pedestrian and vehicle experiments 
show that closing a high-demand exit produces nonlinear degradation: 
rerouted flows rapidly saturate adjacent corridors, creating secondary 
bottlenecks that cannot be offset by nominal network connectivity. By 
contrast, many individual road disruptions yield modest system-level 
effects unless they interact with one-way constraints that trap agents. 
From a planning standpoint, this indicates that protecting and main-
taining operability of key exits is more consequential than marginal, 
network-wide upgrades in constrained heritage fabrics.
Consistent with the exit-use concentration observed in the Results 
section, these findings suggest that limited exit redundancy in walled 
historic districts creates a small set of operational ‘‘single points of 
failure’’. In such settings, the practical objective is not only to maximize 
nominal connectivity, but to preserve functionality at dominant gates 
and manage diversion demand so that secondary corridors do not 
saturate under stress.
In historic districts where structural modification is limited, this 
may involve temporary traffic control measures, reversible street man-
agement strategies, or the pre-positioning of emergency personnel at 
critical gates rather than permanent infrastructure changes.

5.2. Shelters as dynamic network nodes
Shelter expansion altered evacuation dynamics in ways that were 
not uniformly beneficial. While additional shelters reduced travel dis-
tance for nearby agents, the emergence of bimodal clearance distribu-
tions indicates that some agents experienced longer routes due to local 
saturation and subsequent rerouting. This supports a key implication 
of the study: shelters should be treated as dynamic elements of the 
evacuation system rather than static endpoints. Capacity increases are 
most effective when coupled with load balancing and timely guidance 
that prevents late-stage redirection and unnecessary circulation.
Accordingly, shelter planning in constrained street fabrics should 
account for how added destinations reshape flow patterns, potentially 
increasing internal circulation when capacity, accessibility, and guid-
ance are misaligned. Operationally, this points to the value of coordi-
nated shelter assignment (or steering), monitoring of local overload, 
and contingency options when nearby shelters become unavailable.
5.3. Guidance and familiarity as capacity multipliers
A central finding of this study is that evacuation performance is 
highly sensitive to population familiarity and guidance. Differences 
between leader and follower outcomes demonstrate that evacuation 
capacity is not purely infrastructural. Under congestion and partial 
network failure, informed agents maintain higher success, while unin-
formed agents are more likely to stall, disperse inefficiently, or fail to 
clear within the simulation window.
Taken together with the leader-follower patterns reported in the 
Results section, the simulations indicate that information availability

5.4. Relevance for multi-hazard and heritage contexts
Although this study does not model a specific hazard, the stress 
conditions examined — exit loss, route blockage, and shelter overload 
— are common consequences of earthquakes, fires, floods, and extreme 
weather events. The results thus provide hazard-agnostic insights into 
the structural evacuation vulnerability of historic cities. For heritage 
districts where preservation constraints limit physical intervention, 
decision-support tools such as digital twins offer a viable means to 
explore mitigation strategies before emergencies occur.
5.5. Implications for risk-informed planning in heritage contexts
The asymmetric congestion patterns and rerouting delays uncovered 
by the model underscore the need for adaptive evacuation strategies. 
Thus, the study suggests three actionable priorities for DRR in historic 
walled cities:
1. Prioritize critical exits: harden, monitor, and ensure operabil-
ity of dominant gates under stress.
2. Enable adaptive flow management: deploy mechanisms (e.g.,
dynamic signage, real-time routing directives) that redistribute 
flows before congestion saturates alternate corridors.
3. Integrate shelters with guidance: treat shelter deployment as 
a coordinated, load-balanced system rather than a standalone 
capacity expansion.
5.6. Simulation context and planning interpretation
Beyond revealing structural sensitivities, the digital twin functions 
as a controlled analytical laboratory for stress-testing mitigation strate-
gies before real-world implementation. By enabling systematic manip-
ulation of exits, road segments, shelter configurations, and information 
conditions, it provides a rigorous platform for evaluating alternative 
routing policies, load-balancing strategies, and infrastructure protec-
tion measures in heritage environments where physical intervention is 
constrained.
To contextualize these findings, we compared the simulation as-
sumptions with Cyprus’s existing disaster management framework, in-
cluding the Public Warning System (PWS) and the Polyvios Plan, 
under which evacuation orders are disseminated through SMS alerts, 
sirens, and local coordination mechanisms. In practice, warning prop-
agation and public response introduce temporal delays and behavioral 
variability that are not explicitly modeled here.
Our simulations assume idealized informational conditions: agents 
are already outdoors and respond immediately upon alarm activation. 
Notably, structural bottlenecks, nonlinear congestion, and clearance 
delays emerge even under these optimistic assumptions. In real-world 
evacuations, where communication is staggered, compliance is imper-
fect, and movement is less coordinated, such vulnerabilities would 
likely be amplified.
Thus, the digital twin should be interpreted not as a predictive 
forecasting tool, but as a decision-support and exploratory planning 
environment. Its value lies in identifying structurally critical exits, 
congestion-prone corridors, and shelter-induced rerouting cascades, 
thereby informing risk-informed planning and adaptive management 
strategies for historic urban areas.
6. Conclusions
This study presented a high-fidelity, hybrid agent-based evacu-
ation analysis of the historic walled Old Town of Nicosia using a 
geo-referenced 3D digital twin. Rather than predicting evacuation out-
comes for a specific hazard event, the objective was to identify struc-
tural vulnerabilities and planning sensitivities arising from interactions 
between historic urban morphology, constrained infrastructure, and 
human decision-making.
The results demonstrate that evacuation performance in morpholog-
ically constrained heritage districts is highly sensitive to exit availabil-
ity, network redundancy, and the coordination of shelter use. A small 
number of critical gates dominate system-wide performance, and their 
disruption produces disproportionate delays and cascading congestion 
effects. Even under idealized assumptions, where all agents are already 
outdoors and respond immediately to the evacuation signal, clearance 
times exhibit nonlinear patterns and long-tailed delays. These findings 
indicate that structural bottlenecks embedded in legacy urban layouts 
amplify vulnerability during emergency conditions.
The analysis further shows that capacity-based interventions alone 
are insufficient. While additional shelters reduce travel distance for 
some agents, uncoordinated expansion without adaptive guidance can 
generate rerouting cascades, localized saturation, and increased overall 
clearance times. Population familiarity and routing guidance function 
as capacity multipliers: informed agents consistently achieve higher 
success rates and reduce congestion propagation. Together, these find-
ings highlight that evacuation in historic districts must be approached 
as a systemic process shaped by infrastructure constraints, spatial con-
figuration, and information flow.
From a disaster risk reduction perspective, three strategic priori-
ties emerge: (i) safeguarding and ensuring the operability of critical 
exit gates, (ii) integrating real-time routing and communication mech-
anisms to mitigate emerging bottlenecks, and (iii) aligning shelter 
planning with dynamic load-balancing strategies rather than static 
capacity expansion. The digital twin developed in this work provides a 
robust decision-support platform for testing such interventions prior to 
real-world implementation and for facilitating stakeholder engagement 
in resilience planning.
Limitations: Despite the spatial resolution of the digital twin and 
the explicit modeling of pedestrian–vehicle interactions, several limi-
tations remain. First, agents are assumed to start outdoors and receive 
evacuation signals simultaneously, omitting building exit delays, staged 
warnings, and communication bottlenecks that characterize real events. 
Second, behavioral representation is simplified: agents comply with 
evacuation intent and do not exhibit panic, hesitation, social attach-
ment (e.g., family groups), mobility impairments, or heterogeneous 
cognitive responses, all of which can significantly affect evacuation 
dynamics in historic districts with elderly residents and tourists. Third, 
hazard evolution is not explicitly modeled. Debris generation, smoke 
propagation, flood depths, earthquake risk, and time-dependent route 
degradation are not incorporated, and their inclusion could substan-
tially alter network availability and clearance dynamics. Finally, results 
reflect the specific morphological and institutional characteristics of 
Nicosia’s Old Town and should not be generalized to other historic 
cities without contextual adaptation. These limitations position the 
model as an exploratory and decision-support tool rather than a pre-
dictive forecasting system. Its primary contribution lies in revealing 
structural sensitivities and nonlinear congestion patterns that may not 
be apparent through schematic planning approaches.
Future Work: We plan to address these limitations by introducing 
hazard overlays (e.g., debris fields, flood extents, fire spread), dynamic 
alarm propagation, and heterogeneous agent profiles informed by de-
mographic data. Planned extensions include group-based behaviors, 
adaptive traffic management strategies, heterogeneous vehicle fleets, 
and integration with external traffic simulators such as SUMO. These 
enhancements will enable more realistic multi-hazard scenario analysis 
while preserving the framework’s utility for risk-informed planning in 
heritage urban environments.
