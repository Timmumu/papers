ABSTRACT
Preservation of Venice's unique cultural identity requires innovative approaches to safeguard its fragile archi -
tectural heritage. This study explores the potential of drone-based surveying and Historical Digital Twin meth -
odologies to assess the vulnerability of city's façade systems. Focusing on emblematic case studies, high- 
resolution drone imagery was employed to generate detailed 3D digital models of the façades and their statu -
ary elements. Models served as base for structural vulnerability analyses, enabling precise monitoring, mainte -
nance planning, and safety evaluations. The research demonstrates how the integration of advanced digital 
surveying techniques with structural engineering expertise can enhance understanding and management of risks 
affecting Venice's cultural heritage. This research integrates advanced information technologies into the broader 
context of design, engineering, and maintenance of artistic buildings and assets, demonstrating how digital tools 
can revolutionize cultural heritage conservation. The findings offer a replicable framework that bridges tradi -
tional preservation methods with cutting-edge digital modeling techniques to enhance the management of risks.
1. Introduction
Since the second half of the last century, the need and urgency for the 
affirmation and recovery of the places' own identity through the care, 
preservation and enhancement of the Cultural Heritage, has been 
recognized nationally and internationally. Venice represents an exem -
plary case due to the exceptional concentration of historic architecture, 
the close relationship between buildings and the lagoon environment, 
and the specific vulnerabilities affecting its built heritage. The pecu -
liarities and intrinsic critical issues of this city require specific choices to 
be made for its preservation. The subject of this work regards the 
monumental façades, which represent a significant case of how much 
their protection and enhancement affects the perpetuation of the city's 
identity.
The Venetian façade system thus represents one of the main identity 
features of the lagoon city and, at the same time, it constitutes an 
extremely fragile ensemble, especially because of its structural and 
material characters in relation to the lagoon environment ( Settis, 2016 ; 
Di Sipio and Zezza, 2011 ). The present research work constitutes a first 
stage of a more ambitious project that promises to map the Venetian 
façade system through its most representative examples. A mapping of 
the main Venetian façade systems that, by means of a digital survey both 
overall and in detail, will constitute a real atlas capable of documenting 
both their historical-artistic values and their construction peculiarities, 
highlighting their fragility and conditions of degradation.
In particular, the paper will focus on two case studies: the façades of 
church Santa Maria di Nazareth and church San Francesco della Vigna, 
considered by the authors to be emblematic for several reasons and, 
above all, for the construction characteristics that make them particu -
larly representative of two cases of strong vulnerability in the panorama 
of the Venetian façade system. For both case studies, the digital survey 
was carried out with a well-established methodology: the use of 
photogrammetry, combining drone with terrestrial tools, and laser 
scanning ( Gonizzi Barsanti et al., 2022 ; Gonizzi Barsanti and Guidi, 
2018 ). In particular, digital images acquirement by means of drones for 
the survey of buildings or sites of cultural heritage and related digital 
model development by “ Structure-from-Motion ” (SfM) techniques 
gained more and more importance in the last years and an extensive 
body of literature has developed on the subject ( Azzola et al., 2019 ).
Drones (or unmanned aerial vehicles - UAVs) have proven to be 
invaluable in the fields of archaeology and cultural heritage, as they 
provide a noninvasive, time- and cost-efficient way to document and

the technological development that in recent years has allowed a 
considerable advance in the digitization systems of architectural assets

As a starting point for defining appropriate working methodologies, 
Federman's studies ( Federman et al., 2017 ) describe a procedure for 
acquiring data useful to SfM techniques elaboration. For the direct geo 
referencing of pictures by drone's sensors, Gabrlik's work can be 
mentioned ( Gabrlik, 2015 ). A comparison is made between traditional 
aerial photogrammetry and photogrammetry by drones by Bolognesi, as 
well as how they can be integrated ( Bolognesi et al., 2014 ). Among the 
research works that focus the investigation specifically on drone survey 
of cultural heritage assets, Barrile's paper describes the adopted meth -
odology for elaborating a 3D model of the Greek Walls of Reggio Cala -
bria starting from the images acquired by drones ( Barrile et al., 2017 ), 
Azzola uses UAV photogrammetry and consequent modeling for the 
preservation and mapping of venetian walls of Bergamo ( Azzola et al., 
2019 ), Themistocleous uses its potentials for Asinou church in Cyprus 
( Themistocleous et al., 2015 ) and more recently Banfi for Appia Antica 
Archaeological Park ( Banfi et al., 2023 ).
In both the selected case studies, given the construction specifics of 
the two façades, it was necessary to use the drone for filming the upper 
register of the façades. In fact, a high-precision survey, both overall and 
in detail, of the upper tier was a prerequisite for the success of the 
research work, for problems otherwise of strong distortions and ap -
proximations. The statues and decorative apparatus placed in the higher 
parts of the structures are very articulated and, at the same time, 
represent the most fragile and vulnerable elements because they are 
most exposed to kinetic stresses of various kinds, and it is precisely for 
this reason that the evaluation of their vulnerability is the subject of the 
research work. This process leading to the vulnerability assessments of 
cultural heritage assets (here San Francesco della Vigna and Santa Maria 
di Nazareth façades of Venice city) is a topic still little treated in the 
literature.
2. Adopted methodology: from historical digital twin to 
vulnerability assessment
This study employs a survey methodology based on drone flights to 
create historical digital twins of constructions within Cultural Heritage, 
with a specific focus on the city of Venice, an iconic site in the Italian 
landscape. These digital models are designed to provide valuable tools 
for Cultural Heritage management by administrative authorities, as well 
as to support the development of structural models for assessing the 
vulnerability of these assets to various potential hazards. These include 
seismic events and the effects of high tides (“acque alte”) that frequently 
impact Venice—a challenge further intensified by climate change.
The first phase of the procedure involves planning the drone survey 
operations, including defining flight trajectories and assessing re -
strictions imposed by no-fly zones in certain airspaces. Following the 
drone survey, the results are integrated with a supplementary phase of 
ground-based surveys and processing, utilizing more traditional 
methods (laser scanning and static photogrammetry).
Upon completion of the survey, the integrated workflow moves to 
data processing, and then to the development of a three-dimensional 
solid digital model, hereinafter referred to as Historical Digital Twin 
(HDT) of the asset of Cultural Heritage under study ( Marra et al., 2021 ). 
This particular application builds upon the Digital Twin concept, which 
was first conceptualized by ( Glaessgen and Stargel, 2012 ) and was 
subsequently applied to built structures by [ ( Angjeliu et al., 2020 ; 
Xiaozhu and Xingwang, 2018 ; Jouan and Hallot, 2019 ), amongst others] 
as a digital counterpart of their physical reality. This stage involves a 
multi-faceted approach, encompassing SfM techniques, automated 
reconstruction processes (including alignment, dense point cloud and 
meshing generation), and manual reconstruction, complemented by the 
utilization of reverse engineering software. The procedure, theorized by 
the authors in ( Rocca et al., 2024 ), is here outlined in Section 4 .
Having a digital model (HDT) provides multiple opportunities, from 
asset management and enhancement through the implementation of 
detailed historical, critical, and material analyses, to the development of 
structural models supporting vulnerability assessments. Unlike standard 
3D models, the HDT serves as an interoperable platform that merges 
geometric precision with historical stratigraphy and material decay 
data. Indeed, the issue of deterioration can be addressed through map -
ping and analyses, which are subsequently incorporated into the graphic 
restitution of the asset. Moreover, the methodology hopes to bridge the 
gap between geomatics and structural engineering through a specialized 
reverse engineering phase. By transitioning from discrete mesh data to 
continuous NURBS surfaces, the study then enables the generation of 
accurate solid Finite Element models (FEM), minimizing geometric un -
certainties that typically affect the structural simulation of complex 
Cultural Heritage shapes.
Of particular significance, the proposed procedure is applicable to 
any scale of Cultural Heritage constructions, ranging from entire 
buildings to specific components (e.g., a monumental façade) or even 
individual objects (such as individual high-relief statues or movable 
artistic assets). A specific novelty of this research is indeed the focus on 
the seismic vulnerability of projecting decorative elements, a segment of 
cultural heritage that is typically under-researched from a structural 
safety perspective. The methodology can address themes as behaviour 
under self-weight loads, out-of-plane phenomena and the amplification 
of seismic action at different heights of structures, directly linking high- 
resolution surveying with kinematic and structural analyses.
3. Venice case study: UAV photogrammetric survey of church 
monumental façades and statues
As an example of applying the proposed methodology for the con -
struction of historical digital twins at two scales—building element and 
artistic element—this work focuses on the monumental façades of two 
Venetian churches and some statues that characterize them, positioned 
at various heights: the church of “Santa Maria di Nazareth” (vulgo degli 
Scalzi), Fig. 1 and the church of “San Francesco della Vigna”, Fig. 2 .
Due to the nature of the objects under study, constructing models 
that both accurately represent a digital twin of the elements and serve as 
the basis for structural models used in vulnerability assessments neces -
sitates a survey campaign relying predominantly on drones.
This approach is necessary to accurately survey the tops of the fa -
çades, as the limited depth of field would otherwise result in significant 
distortions. For example, in the case of Santa Maria di Nazareth façade, 
the distance from the Grand Canal is only about 10 m. Relying solely on 
terrestrial photogrammetry would produce a highly inclined framework, 
leading to inaccuracies in the model, shaded areas, and uncontrolled 
interpolations between data points.
Similarly, for the San Francesco della Vigna façade, the issue of 
limited depth of field arises due to the presence of a building located just 
5 m in front of most of the façade. As with the first example, the dis -
tortions and shadowed areas caused by using terrestrial instruments 
only would be substantial and incompatible with the level of reliability 
required for the 3D model.
3.1. Historical background
The monumental façades of the analyzed churches are two of the 
most emblematic representations of Venetian identity. The first, Santa 
Maria di Nazareth (commonly known as the Scalzi Church), serves as a

prominent "landmark, ” recognizable since the late 19th century as a 
gateway to the city of Venice. The second, San Francesco della Vigna, is 
probably Andrea Palladio's first ecclesiastical project in Venice and the 
only one fully executed under his direction.
The Church of Santa Maria di Nazareth, designed by Baldassarre 
Longhena and completed with a Baroque façade by Giuseppe Sardi 
(1680), represents a unique iconographic landmark of the late Venetian 
Baroque. Located near Venice's Santa Lucia station, it is exposed to 
significant anthropic pressure and a harsh lagoon environment charac -
terized by saline mist, tidal fluctuations, and extreme thermal cycles. 
Uniquely for Venice, the façade is constructed entirely from Carrara 
marble ( Bettini and Frank, 2014 ), a rare lithotype in the Venetian 
context. Its complex geometry - rich in niches, recesses, and statuary - 
creates microclimates that promote differential interaction of the marble 
support with rainwater and salts. Over time, this saccharoid limestone 
has proven vulnerable, as evidenced by a 2013 incident involving the 
detachment of a capital's acanthus leaf. Subsequent diagnostics revealed 
severe degradation, including decohesion and pulverization reaching 
depths of up to 12 cm. Consequently, a conservative restoration project 
of this façade ( Rocca et al., 2022 ) was initiated, prioritizing experi -
mental, compatible, and reversible interventions. Beyond structural re -
covery, the project established a comprehensive technical-scientific 
database to monitor the efficacy of these actions and provide a frame -
work for future maintenance. Particular focus was required by the 
façade decorative-statuary apparatus. The Carrara marble statuary 
( Fig. 3 a) indeed remains a critical point due to its fragility, as evidenced 
by the historical loss of the 'Speranza' statue in 1920 ( SABAPV-AS ).
Designed by Andrea Palladio on commission from Patriarch Grimani 
and completed in 1564, the façade of San Francesco della Vigna is a 
seminal example of Venetian Renaissance architecture and an experi -
mental application of Palladian compositional principles. Clad entirely 
in Istrian stone, the façade's composition reflects the interior spatial 
hierarchy, with a central nave and lower side chapels, through a 
tripartite system of a central tympanum and lateral semi-tympanums.

3.2. Survey program
Regarding Santa Maria di Nazareth, a few years after the completion 
of the restoration work, it was decided to use this monumental façade as 
the first case study for developing the methodology described in Section 
2 . This methodology, based on extensive data acquisition, aimed to 
produce an historical digital twin representing the monument, facili -
tating a comprehensive understanding of its vulnerabilities and enabling 
ongoing safety assessments. This methodology was also applied to the 
façade of San Francesco della Vigna.
To address vulnerabilities in both the structural integrity and mate -
rial conservation of these façades and their artifacts, a comprehensive 
survey campaign was planned with the support of the VIDE laboratory 
(part of IR.IDE research infrastructure) at IUAV University of Venice, 
leading to the creation of the façade's digital twin through 3D modeling.
A 3D digital photogrammetry survey served as the basis for creating 
solid models used to assess static and seismic performance. As previously 
mentioned, the most suitable method for conducting an accurate survey 
under conditions like those of the two façades, required an intensive and 
strategic use of drone systems, complemented by some ground-based 
survey operations. For the aerial photogrammetric survey, a drone DJI 
Mavic 2 Pro with High Dynamic Range (HDR) video 4k 10 bit was 
employed, with characteristics explicated in Table 1 . The choices made 
for the photos taken by the drone are: exposure ISO 100, aperture f/5.6, 
exposure time 1/400s.
In order to check the correctness and accuracy of the photogram -
metric elaborations some relevant morphological points were selected 
on the façade (e.g., building corners) from a network of trilaterate key 
points, along with strategically distributed Ground Control Points 
(GCPs) consisting of clearly recognizable artificial targets. GCPs were 
homogeneously distributed across the façade to ensure comprehensive 
coverage, positioning them at the corners, at the center, and at various 
heights. Their coordinates were acquired using a total station to estab -
lish an absolute topographic reference. Subsequently, the GCPs were 
manually identified across multiple oriented images within the pro -
cessing software to optimize the model's internal and external 
orientation.
The façade of Santa Maria di Nazareth measures about 22.4m in 
width, 24.0m in height, while San Francesco della Vigna façade mea -
sures 32m in width and 28.0m in height.
Drawing upon experience from previous artifacts, to ensure a suffi -
cient point density and data to represent the actual state of the artifacts 
by means of a 3D model at a 1:20 drawing scale, the model must be 
capable of reaching 1 cm accuracy. As a consequence the ground sam -
pling distance (GSD) has to be equal to or below 1 cm/pixel. The 
theoretical value of the mean distance of acquisition to get the desired 
resolution is referred to an ideal condition, with a camera placed on a 
tripod without external interferences ( Banfi and Mandelli, 2021 ); 
considering instead the real conditions of execution of the survey in both 
cases the authors decided for a flight at a mean distance of 2 m from the 
asset. Consequently, an effective GSD of 0.7 mm/pixel was obtained. 
This accounts for potential motion blur and environmental instabilities 
(e.g., wind gusts and aerodynamic interference near the façade) that 
may affect pixel sharpness during flight. Consequently, the number of 
images collected was remarkable, almost 700 photos at the maximum 
resolution of the camera for every façade. They are gathered in JPG 
format and are comprehensive of the coverage also of decorative ele -
ments and of statues in niches and on top.

3.3. Support and role of terrestrial survey
The 3D survey of the façade systems was carried out also through 
other indirect techniques: laser scanning and static photogrammetry, 
both from a terrestrial point of view. In particular, the instruments 
employed are the FARO focus S 150 terrestrial laser scanner and the 
Nikon D850 digital camera with 50 mm f/1.4 lens, whose specifications 
are given in Table 2 .
This survey was not conducted to merge the data from the photo -
grammetric flight with the terrestrial photogrammetric data. Instead, 
the two techniques played distinct, complementary roles in the overall 
process. The Terrestrial Laser Scanner (TLS) was used to support the 
UAV survey by providing a high-accuracy contextual survey and a cor -
responding model of the surrounding environment, including the church 
and the area around the façade. This served as a stable topographic 
backbone to support the UAV survey. Regarding the close-range 
terrestrial photogrammetry, it served as a metric benchmark for the 
lower portions of the façades. By capturing these portions at a higher 
level of detail, it enabled a comparative validation of the UAV-derived 
model, ensuring consistency between the aerial and ground-based 
datasets. To guarantee spatial coherence across all platforms, the same 
GCPs of the aerial photogrammetric survey have been taken in the 
terrestrial survey, ensuring a unified coordinate system for the final 
comparative analysis.
For the photographic survey, the authors followed a shooting path 
similar to that of the drone, employing horizontal strips with compa -
rable distances and overlaps. Nevertheless, data acquisition was 
restricted to the lower half of the façade to mitigate excessive image 
distortion in the higher sections. Regarding the laser scans, five scans 
were conducted at distances of 10 to 20 m from each façade. For Santa 
Maria di Nazareth, the scans were taken from the quay in front of the 
church and from the Scalzi Bridge, the highest accessible point near the 
church. For San Francesco della Vigna, the scans were taken from the 
square in front of the church and from the terrace of a building located to 
the left of the façade.
4. 3D modeling and digital twin elaboration
The models were developed primarily using Agisoft Metashape Pro 
(version 2021, ( Metashape AgiSoft PhotoScan )). All UAV survey images 
were imported into the software. The automatic photo modeling process 
employs the SfM detection technique, which uses computer vision al -
gorithms to identify significant points in individual photographs, derives 
their parameters, cross-reference identifiable points across multiple 
images, and determines their spatial coordinates through nearly auto -
matic collimation ( Lucignano, 2021 ). During the image orientation 
phase, a sparse point cloud is generated. The subsequent phase involves 
extracting a dense point cloud, geo-referencing, scaling the model, and 
manually refining it to exclude noise or points unrelated to the analyzed 
geometry.
Next, a 3D photorealistic textured polygonal mesh is built over the 
dense cloud, creating three-dimensional objects based on parameters 
aligned with a three-dimensional coordinate system, effectively 
capturing both the texture, shape and geometry of reality.
The 3D modeling process then continues with the reconstruction of 
the façade system and its individual defining elements, completing the 
3D elaboration derived from the survey described in Section 3 . The solid 
model, which accurately represents the correct volumes, is generated 
from the mesh model exported from Agisoft Metashape Pro in OBJ 
format (along with its high-definition texture). This data is subsequently 
processed using the reverse engineering software Rhinoceros ( McNeel 
Rhinoceros ). Unlike a purely visual 3D shell, a volume-representative 
mesh ensures topological integrity, allowing for the precise calculation

4.1. Façades
The acquired survey data were processed and optimized to produce 
reality-based three-dimensional digital models of the façades (see 
Fig. 6 ). These figures demonstrate that by prioritizing precision and 
survey quality, a model with an average error of less than 1 cm for 
control points and distances was achieved. This level of accuracy is 
compatible with the objectives of the 3D model, which include control, 
measurement, detection of material and structural vulnerabilities, color 
alterations, cracks, damages, and the creation of a reliable model for 
subsequent numerical analyses.
The workflow continued with mesh model processing to generate the 
solid 3D model, which also accurately represents the correct volumes. 
Due to complexity, diversity of shapes, and scale of these façades, this 
process could not be fully automated and required manual reconstruc -
tion of the volumes in Rhinoceros. Classical architectural elements (such 
as columns and lintels) were modeled by extracting sections and ele -
vations directly from the 3D model or the point cloud.
As an example, Fig. 7 illustrates the workflow for the digital recon -
struction of the Historical Digital Twin of the Santa Maria di Nazareth 
façade, transitioning from the photorealistic 3D model to the solid one.
4.2. Statues
The complex geometries, such as those of the statuary apparatus (and 
other decorative elements like the shield), were processed separately 
from the entire façade to create high-resolution models of these elements 
independently of the overall architectural structure. This process can be 
conducted in two ways: either by isolating the point cloud around the 
decorative element within the global point cloud obtained from photo 
alignment or by using pertinent photos captured during the UAV survey 
focused on the statue.
The 3D modeling of the statues followed the same procedure used for 
the entire façade, as illustrated in Fig. 8 with the example of the statue of 
San Girolamo. Unlike classical architectural elements, statues and other 
decorative components are freeform structures, requiring a different 
approach for solid 3D model reconstruction. In these cases, the transi -
tion from the textured mesh model to the solid one is semi-automatic 
and includes generating NURBS models as well as manual corrections 
by the operator. These corrections address common mesh errors such as 
non-manifold edges, intersections, redundant faces, and other irregu -
larities. In particular, in the statues ’ case, in the final step of converting 
the surface mesh into a solid model, the methodology employs a reverse

5. Towards safety assessment
The obtained 3D model is used to identify vulnerabilities from a ki -
nematic perspective, including the presence of crack patterns, de -
tachments, chromatic alterations, deterioration of reinforcements and 
restraints introduced during restoration interventions (e.g., steel sup -
ports for statues or protective nets). It also allows the detection of po -
tential deficiencies in the performance of these interventions. For 
instance, the drone survey enables the evaluation of static risk factors, 
such as cracks, material detachments, and movement evolution (i.e., 
kinematics) in the upper parts of the façade and its projecting elements, 
including the coat of arms and statues. This information supports 
effective planning of potential direct interventions. Additionally, such 
factors may also serve as indicators of preservation risks in the façade's 
texture and integrity.
Thanks to the high precision of the 3D model, technicians with 
specialized engineering expertise can quickly identify critical points and 
interpret the structural behavior of the façade. Among the issues verified 
using the 3D model is the inclination of the upper portion of the façade 
of San Francesco della Vigna. An out-of-plumb condition was identified 
through verticality verification by analyzing thin slices of points (from 
the dense mesh or the model itself), reconstructing the tympanum's 
geometry and comparing it with the vertical axis.
Using the three-dimensional façade model, it is possible to under -
stand the role of individual components and use it as the basis for 
creating a numerical-mathematical model (detailed finite element 3D 
modeling, FEM) of the façade or its elements for static and dynamic 
structural safety analyses. The development of FEM models from SfM 
survey is a topic of considerable interest in literature, see, for instance, 
( Castellazzi et al., 2017 ; Gonizzi Barsanti and Guidi, 2017 ).
The model enables assessments of static and seismic vulnerabilities 
of the structure as a whole and of its constituent parts at different levels 
of detail. These analyses account for mechanical actions, such as wind, 
impacts, seismic forces, as well as for the effects of environmental var -
iations typical of the lagoon area. Specifically, the high-precision 3D 
digital model supports safety checks using either simplified ( Berto et al., 
2021 ) or advanced methods (e.g., FEM, in this study performed with 
MIDAS Gen software ( MIDAS Gen )). Depending on the required level of 
analysis detail, the historical digital twin can provide parameters for 
simplified methods or serve as a basis for computational FE models for 
in-depth static and dynamic evaluations.
5.1. Entire façade of Santa Maria di Nazareth
For example, focusing on the entire façade of Santa Maria di Naza -

5.2. Statuary apparatus of Santa Maria di Nazareth
Taking the statuary apparatus as an example, the generated 3D 
models and the collected data enable static and seismic vulnerability 
assessments of these elements. Despite being constructed of different 
materials — Carrara marble for the Santa Maria di Nazareth façade and 
bronze for San Francesco della Vigna — the statues share similarities in 
terms of analysis methodology. The different internal configurations 
(solid for marble and hollow for bronze) and material pathologies can be 
analyzed using the same approach. With the 3D model and its associated 
data, advanced methods (e.g., FEM) can evaluate stress concentrations 
and strain distributions, while simplified methods assess overturning 
tendencies under mechanical actions such as wind, shocks, or seismic 
forces. Fixed constraints were applied at the base of the statues, in order 
to reflect the actual physical connection between the statue and its 
pedestal, as evidenced by the Superintendency's archives and the data 
gathered during the survey program.
For example, Fig. 10 presents the results of the static analysis, pre -
liminary to the seismic vulnerability analyses that require the evaluation 
of the seismic action at the base of the artifacts (see Rocca et al., 2024 ; 
MIT, 2019 ; Baggio et al., 2018 ), of the San Bartolomeo statue's numer -
ical finite element model in terms of effective stresses. The analyses 
identify areas of maximum stress distribution, or zones potentially sus -
ceptible to damage in static conditions. Furthermore, they are a pre -
requisite for time-history simulations of potential strong motion events. 
The highest effective stresses on the San Bartolomeo statue are observed 
in the area connecting to the support trunk, which is attributed to the 
statue's pronounced eccentric center of gravity. However, the statue's 
safety under this load is confirmed, as the maximum stresses remain well 
within the Carrara marble's strength capacity.
6. Conclusions
Together with the classical methods of data collection — such as 
historical documentation and original 2D drawings to understand the 
construction techniques - this work demonstrates the utility of inte -
grating advanced 3D surveys based on UAV digital photogrammetry to 
create a Historical Digital Twin of the monument and its apparatus. 
Through this process, the vulnerability assessments of the two case 
studies, the façades of San Francesco della Vigna and Santa Maria di 
Nazareth in Venice, have been carried out effectively and 
comprehensively.
The integration of survey data with advanced technologies and 3D 
representation, coupled with structural engineering expertise, repre -
sents a highly effective, precise, and accurate approach to gaining 
knowledge, preserving and ensuring the fruition of cultural heritage. In 
this study, this methodology was specifically tailored to the preservation 
and enhancement of the Venetian "façade systems ” , contributing to aims 
of addressing all aspects of artistic buildings and assets life cycle - from 
analysis and maintenance to advanced digital assessments and conser -
vation strategies.
The acquired data were processed and optimized to generate a 
reality-based three-dimensional digital model of each façade, accurately 
capturing their geometric and morphological features. This model serves 
as a basis for identifying critical issues. Subsequently, a solid model, 
representative of both accurate geometry and correct volumes — referred 
to as the Historical Digital Twin — was developed from the initial mesh 
model. This Historical Digital Twin facilitates static and dynamic

analyses aimed at assessing the structural safety of the façade or its 
constituent elements.
The model's role as a “ place of verification ” underscores the impor -
tance of its high fidelity to real-world conditions, as this directly in -
fluences the reliability of subsequent evaluations. For monumental 
assets of this scale and complexity, such as the examined façade systems, 
achieving this level of accuracy would not have been possible through 
terrestrial surveys alone. Drone-based surveys were essential, especially 
for capturing the critical upper portions of the façades, where terrestrial 
methods would have resulted in distortions, shadowing, and gaps due to 
limited depth of field.
In summary, the critical and interpretative model obtained through 
this process not only serves as a database capable of describing the 
current state of conservation and identifying diverse issues but also 
ensures ongoing and effective monitoring when updated over time or 
integrated with new data. In particular, for the Scalzi façade, this tool 
can assist in developing a reasoned maintenance and monitoring plan, 
while for the façade of San Francesco della Vigna, it enables an assess -
ment of its current condition to prioritize necessary conservation and 
restoration interventions.
The Historical Digital Twin is proposed as a tool for “ preventive 
restoration ” (concept elaborated in Cesare Brandi's Theory of Restora -
tion ( Brandi, 1963 )), allowing for non-invasive monitoring and the 
simulation of future degradation scenarios to support administrative 
decision-making. Indeed, it enables analyses for safety assessments and 
analyses related to other risks, such as environmental conditions specific 
to the lagoon or the impact of weather over time. By situating this 
research within the broader scope of using information technologies to 
address challenges in construction technologies and management, this 
study underscores the transformative role of digital methods in bridging 
traditional conservation approaches with innovative engineering tools. 
This dual functionality — providing both documentation and analytical 
capabilities — makes it an invaluable tool for the preservation and safe -
guarding of cultural heritage.
