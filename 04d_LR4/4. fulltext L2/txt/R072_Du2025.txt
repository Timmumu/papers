ABSTRACT
Leveraging digital technology to improve public service capabilities in disaster prevention and mitigation is a key 
focus in geological hazard research. This research expands the utilization of numerical simulations for geological 
disaster forecasting, including studies on current-state inversion, future prediction techniques, and data inte -
gration. A geological hazard simulation framework endowed with long-term predictive capabilities was devel -
oped using the Material Point Method (MPM), with sensing data of the prototype system state and digital twin 
models constitute the core data components. A disaster-scenario twin model was established by extracting key 
elements from disaster scenarios, serving as the data storage medium for digital simulations. State sensing of the 
disaster prototype scenario was used to conduct system state inversion and optimize the selection of physico- 
mechanical parameters. The twin model was then updated using physico-mechanical models of geotechnical 
materials to forecast the future development of the disaster. Taking the Taihe landslide as a case study, the 
prototype system state was derived from monitoring data, along with the time-dependent characteristics of 
strength degradation of geotechnical materials. Inversion of disaster state and predictive simulations were 
performed using the twin model driven by data and physical principles. The landslide deformation direction, 
range, and local collapse locations between the twin model and the disaster prototype were compared to further 
achieve the constraints and optimization of the input parameters of the twin model. Building on this, long-term 
stability simulations for the landslide were performed, integrating the strength evolution of geotechnical ma -
terials to incorporate time-span characteristics in the landslide twin model for long-term behavior. The evolution 
of future disasters of the Taihe landslide over the next decade was forecasted. A disaster-scenario digital twin 
model, extending traditional numerical simulation in disaster fields, was developed. By integrating data and 
physics-driven approaches, it creates a simulation framework with temporal generalization capability. This 
advances the utilization of digital technologies in engineering geology and improves the accuracy of disaster 
prediction.
1. Introduction
Employing digital technology to improve public services for disaster 
prevention and mitigation, as well as to enhance efficiency, is a prom -
inent focus for research into geological hazards. The strengths of digital 
technology in geological hazards lie in two key areas: state sensing and 
predictive simulation of disasters. State sensing entails the deployment 
of diverse sensors to detect and gather information pertinent to 
landslides, such as rainfall, groundwater levels, moisture contents, and 
deformation data, to reconstruct the current deformation state of the 
landslide prototype system and evaluate its stability. Non-contact 
exploration technologies ( Frattini et al., 2018 ), such as synthetic aper -
ture radar, transient electromagnetic methods, and unmanned aerial 
vehicles (UAVs), provide data on terrain, surface textures, and geolog -
ical structures and enable the monitoring of regional-scale multi-tem -
poral surface deformation ( Bearzot et al., 2022 ). Even with their initial

cise mapping of landslide boundaries and morphological characteristics, 
and the identification of subtle changes in surface conditions over time. 
This capability is invaluable to understanding the spatial extent and 
characteristics of landslides, as well as for detecting early signs of ac -
tivity or assessing post-failure scenarios ( Casagli et al., 2010 ; Casagli 
et al., 2023 ).
Predictive simulation encompasses the assessment of landslide sus -
ceptibility and stability using techniques such as geostatistical methods 
and physico-mechanical approaches ( Gong et al., 2024 ; Reichenbach 
et al., 2018 ; Merghadi et al., 2020 ). Geostatistical methods begin with an 
examination of the spatial autocorrelation of landslide occurrences, 
summarizing disaster-inducing factors from numerous landslide samples 
to evaluate the susceptibility of a region to geological hazards. However, 
the lack of a deterministic analysis of the physico-mechanical mecha -
nisms underlying these phenomena often leads to predictive bias 
induced by an over-reliance on data. Physico-mechanical approaches 
usually involve solving disaster models through governing equations, 
enabling inversion of the current stability state and projection of future 
evolution trends. Physico-mechanical evaluation methods are primarily 
applied to the analysis of localized single-point landslide, with stability 
assessment results often aligning more closely with physico-mechanical 
principles. However, their application in disaster forecasting and early 
warning remains limited. The underlying reason for this is that the 
development of simulation algorithms for movement and accumulation 
of post-initiation disasters in the geological hazard field tends to prior -
itize phenomenological approximations. Emphasis on approximating 
the final deposition form often overlooks critical details of the disaster 
evolution process, diminishing the reliability of predictions for landslide 
initiation and dynamics. Moreover, simulations and predictive tech -
niques exhibit limited long-term predictive capabilities. The integration 
of most algorithms and models is tailored to specific disaster types or 
specific working conditions. The temporal concepts in calculations are 
often ambiguous, hindering the connection between numerical simula -
tion outcomes and disaster forecasting efforts.
Additionally, with the widespread adoption of high spatio-temporal 
resolution mapping and monitoring data collection technologies and the 
rapid advancement of emerging computational sciences, data storage 
formats and representations have become increasingly intricate. The 
fusion and processing of multi-source heterogeneous data pose a sub -
stantial challenge for the practical implementation of digital applica -
tions in the realm of geological disasters ( Li et al., 2022 ). Data serve as 
the cornerstone of digital technology. The twin model acts as a storage 
platform for predictive simulation data, while diverse external data 
sources drive the dynamic inversion and operation of the twin model. 
The integration and interaction between them are essential for realizing 
dynamic simulation, monitoring, and analysis of physical entities within 
a digital environment.
The rapid advancements in digital technologies such as the Internet 
of Things (IoT), big data, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence 
have led to the emergence of the transformative paradigm of “ digital 
twinning. ” Digital twins utilize IoT-based monitoring devices to collect 
data on field conditions, mechanical responses, and geological struc -
tures in engineering geomechanics. Using advanced data analytics and 
artificial intelligence, real-time inverse analysis and feature extraction 
are conducted, enabling the continuous spatio-temporal evolution of 
geomechanical parameters. Integrating cloud computing, multi-field 
continuous-discontinuous computations, and advanced technologies 
such as deep learning, digital twins' advance stability analysis, risk 
assessment, and insights into complex geomechanical mechanisms. 
Simultaneously, advancements in monitoring technologies — including 
interferometric synthetic aperture radar, microseismic monitoring, 
fiber-optic-based full deformation, laser scanning, and computed to -
mography — along with innovative macro and micro-scale numerical 
algorithms, including the finite-discrete element method, smoothed 
particle hydrodynamics (SPH), material point method (MPM), particle 
finite element method (PFEM), peridynamics (PD), phase field method 
(PF), and numerical manifold method (NMM), have opened additional 
avenues for advancing this paradigm ( Conte et al., 2019 ; Conte et al., 
2020 ; Yerro et al., 2019 ; Soga et al., 2016 ; Zhang et al., 2023 ; Dimililer 
et al., 2020 ; Giri et al., 2019 ; Du et al., 2023 ). The widespread adoption 
of machine learning, data-driven computation, and cloud computing 
further facilitates new pathways for landslide prediction and forecasting 
using digital twins. Embedding physical constitutive and governing 
equations into neural networks (e.g., PINNs) is a hot topic at the nexus of 
machine learning and computational mechanics. Using PDE residuals as 
part of the loss function makes neural network outputs follow physical 
laws. In data-scarce scenarios, physical constraints boost model gener -
alization and support multi-equation coupling modeling in complex 
systems ( Wang et al., 2024 ).
This research focuses on the application of extended numerical 
simulations grounded in the digital twin concept for forecasting 
geological disasters. Taking the Taihe landslide as a case study, it ex -
plores data-driven inversion of current disaster conditions, physics- 
driven prediction of disaster evolution, and data fusion. A simulation 
framework with time generalization capability is constructed adopting 
the MPM. Coupled with the evolution of properties of geotechnical 
materials, multi-perspective and multi-dimensional scenario analyses 
can reveal the mechanisms underpinning the initiation of landslide 
instability and predict its future evolution.

2.2. Analysis step inheritance algorithm
Time-dependent characteristics are a fundamental mechanical 
property of geotechnical materials, however, conventional landslide 
stability analysis mainly focuses on the elasto-plasticity of these mate -
rials, overlooking the temporal evolution of their stability state. The 
long-term stability of landslides, which is influenced by both internal 
and external factors, is closely related to variables such as fluctuations in 
the groundwater level and rainfall intensity. Evaluating long-term sta -
bility of landslides generally involves examining both external factors 
and the time-dependent characteristics of the mechanical properties of 
the material ( Federico et al., 2011 ; Alonso, 2021 ).
Analysis of landslide long-term stability typically requires calcula -
tions over periods measured in years. Due to issues such as time-step size 
and computational efficiency, explicit analysis methods necessitate a 
considerable amount of time to calculate the long-term stability. The 
primary factors controlling landslide long-term stability include the 
degradation of strength parameters and the topographical changes 
caused by cumulative deformation. This study selects key points in the 
long-term deformation process for analysis, breaking down the calcu -
lation of long-term stability into a series of analytical steps. Each step 
terminates when convergence is reached or motion becomes stable, with 
positions, stress states, displacements, velocities, volumes, and elasto- 
plastic states of material points (MPs) from the previous analysis step 
output and used as input for the current analysis step. During the initial 
geostress analysis step, MP displacement, velocity, and elasto-plastic 
states are reset to zero. The flowchart through the analytical step- 
inheritance algorithm is shown in Fig. 1 .

The digital twin framework for landslides centers on data of the 
prototype system state, using the twin model as a bridge to facilitate 
dynamic interactions. This includes prototype system state-driven pro -
cesses, twin model coupling, and feedback of state information via 
mutual information exchange. At the heart of the framework is the 
fusion of multi-source data with real-time updates, aimed at building a 
digital twin capable of dynamically representing the actual state of a 
given landslide. Specifically, the system state data of the prototype 
encompass terrain morphology, geological structure, surface and deep 
deformation, geotechnical parameters, rainfall, and groundwater. These 
datasets are obtained via UAVs, electromagnetic surveys, sensors, and 
global navigation satellite systems (GNSS). Leveraging integrated multi- 
source data, a 3-d geological model of the landslide twin is built, intu -
itively representing the current state of stability of the landslide. 
Building on this, the evolution of properties of the salient geotechnical 
material is integrated to enable dynamic updates to the deformation and 
stability states of the landslide.
Taking the construction of Taihe landslide digital twin scenario as an 
example, the process of geological feature extraction and state sensing is 
as follows: 1) High-precision surface topography data are acquired uti -
lizing drones equipped with high-resolution cameras or LiDAR, pro -
ducing a digital elevation model and orthophotos to supply topographic 
data for the twin model; electromagnetic methods are employed to 
deduce subsurface rock layer distribution, fault positions, and ground -
water distribution, offering data of geological structures for the twin 
model; 2) Surface and subsurface deformations of the landslide are 
monitored using GNSS and inclinometers, capturing deformation 
magnitude, direction, and rate data, which are used to describe the 
landslide deformation mechanism and stability, enabling the inversion 
of the current state of the landslide; 3) Laboratory physico-mechanical 
tests are conducted to determine critical parameters such as elastic 
modulus and shear strength of the landslide mass: these are integrated 
with the trend in material properties and strength parameters to facili -
tate the analysis of landslide deformation and long-term stability pre -
dictions. Fig. 5 depicts the complete workflow through geological 
feature extraction and state sensing in the Taihe landslide digital twin 
scenario, encompassing all steps from data collection and model 
development to state feedback.
Combining landslide state data and displacement-rate time series, 
the displacement increment is approximately 3 mm/d during the dry

is the bedrock-deposit interface, where significant relative deformation 
occurs near the interface. The surface displacement vector and the deep

2.4. Construction of the digital twin model
A top-down modeling approach is employed to create a structured 3- 
d MP model with equal spacings such that Δ z = Δ x = Δ y , using the MP 
spacing as the resolution to extract surface and stratigraphic interface 
point cloud models ( Fig. 6 ). The stratigraphic interface point cloud 
(including the glacial deposit/strongly weathered diabase interface, 
strongly weathered diabase/medium weathered gabbro interface, and 
sliding surface) is employed to slice the 3-d MP model. A binary tree 
model cutting algorithm is introduced to create a spatial position index 
between the MP model and the surface/stratigraphic point cloud data. 
The 3-d MP model is sliced based on this index, and material types are 
grouped and defined. The physical model is divided into five groups: the 
landslide mass, landslide surface, depositional body, strongly weathered

3. Digital simulation of the entire disaster evolution process
Accurately mapping the current state of geological disasters is 
essential for predicting and forecasting their future evolution utilizing 
the digital twin technology. The former involves an inversion process 
targeting the physical real-world state, while the latter requires 
combining the twin model that maps the real-world state with the laws 
of physico-mechanical evolution to simulate the future progression of 
the disaster. The process of disaster state inversion begins with a qual -
itative assessment of the stability control factors influencing the factor of 
safety of the landslide based on empirical knowledge. This is followed by 
inverting the landslide safety factor through use of deformation moni -
toring data and limit equilibrium analysis, optimizing the physico- 
mechanical parameters of soil. Ultimately, the optimal parameters for 
the mapped landslide prototype system as input were obtained for 
subsequent simulation. Subsequently, site surveys and three- 
dimensional monitoring are utilized to acquire deformation and dam -
age details of the landslide, such as surface deformation, deep defor -
mation, and locations of localized collapse. These, combined with the 
physico-mechanical parameters derived from qualitative inversion, are 
adopted to quantify the deformation and damage details of the landslide 
prototype (displacement values, areas of concentrated deformation, 
plastic zones). The physico-mechanical parameters of the landslide are 
further optimized to create a digital twin state corresponding to the 
current disaster prototype system, ensuring consistency in physico- 
mechanical parameters, stability states, and deformation and damage 
details. Building on this, a model update algorithm driven by nonlinear 
mechanics and evolution laws of geotechnical materials is adopted. This 
accounts for the time-dependent evolution of soil physico-mechanical 
parameters under the influence of dry-wet cycles on the southern pit 
slope of the Taihe Mine. A physical model approximating the landslide 
deformation mechanism is developed to represent and predict 
displacement of the landslide, enabling forward simulation and disaster 
forecasting of its future evolution. Herein, the prototype system refers to 
the actual Taihe landslide, while the twin model is a 1:1 particle model 
reconstructed from the landslide prototype system.
3.1. Inversion of prototype state data-driven model state
Deep displacement monitoring of the Taihe landslide shows that the 
landslide body is largely intact, with no secondary slip surfaces detected. 
The consistency with monitoring data of surface deformation further 
confirms that the landslide mass has not disintegrated and remains in a 
creeping state. The changes in deformation are stable, but the overall 
system has not yet converged. Under this premise, profiles are extracted 
from the digital twin model for the inversion of the geotechnical 
parameters of landslides, considering the three-dimensional effects. Two 
profiles, A-A ´ and B-B ´ ( Fig. 8 ), are selected. A comparison with 
geotechnical survey profiles further corroborates the accuracy of the 
constructed digital twin model in depicting the geological structure of 
the landslide prototype system. The parameters of the two profiles of the 
landslide body are inverted using the Morgenstern-Price method, 
thereby determining the comprehensive values for the parameters of slip 
zone soil: an apparent cohesion of 39 kPa and angle of internal shearing 
resistance of 7
◦
. On this basis, the material parameters for the stratig -
raphy of the Taihe landslide are obtained, incorporating laboratory 
physico-mechanical experimental results, and are listed in Table 1 .
The key issue in numerical simulations of geological disasters is to 
determine the initial geostress field of the model, which determines the 
reasonability of the subsequent numerical results. Initially, the whole 
model is assigned an elastic constitutive law and run for 10 s under 
gravity. Based on this, each layer is transformed from an elastic to an 
elastoplastic model, with actual deformation and strength parameters 
applied. These are adopted as the initial geostress field for the digital 
twin model. As shown in Fig. 9 , the distribution of initial geostress is 
generally aligned with typical patterns.
According to the slip zone parameters obtained from limit equilib -
rium inversion analysis, under the limit equilibrium state of the land -
slide, as the deformed mass continues to slide, the pit slope reveals the 
ancient slip surface, with the upper mass exhibiting noticeable external 
displacement, characterized by shearing features. The simulation results 
of stepped slope sliding deformation correspond to the actual landslide 
in terms of deformation state and range ( Fig. 9 b), with deformation 
concentrated within the deformed body surrounded by saddle-shaped 
boundary cracks.

3.2. Long-term stability simulation driven by the state evolution
3.2.1. Evolution of the prototype state
The landslide area is covered by thick loose Quaternary layers with 
low permeability and relatively rich shallow groundwater. Groundwater 
seepage activity as a major factor contributing to landslides and other 
geological hazards has a significant impact on the stability of the pit 
slope. The landslide site has an average annual precipitation of 1013 
mm, with the rainy season occurring from May to October, accounting 
for 94 % of the annual average precipitation. Precipitation from 
November to April of the following year accounts for only 6 % of the 
annual total. During the rainy season (May to October) each year, 
continuous rainfall leads to severe seepage on the pit slope, resulting in 
large areas of water accumulation in the low-lying areas at the base of 
the pit slope. It is believed that the landslide body, particularly the slip 
zone above the bedrock-deposit interface, is undergoing repeated wet- 
dry cycles, which is a key factor governing the future stability evolu -
tion of the Taihe landslide.

4. Conclusion
Taking the Taihe landslide as a case study, a digital simulation 
framework for geological disaster prediction was presented based on the 
MPM for large deformations. From a digital twin perspective, a digital 
twin model of geological disasters and their surrounding environments 
is established based on survey data. By integrating monitoring data to 
map the current state of the disaster prototype system and incorporating 
the time-dependent characteristics derived from the state evolution of 
geotechnical materials, the disaster state inversion and prediction 
simulation are performed using the twin model. This enables the twin 
model to characterize the actual state of the disaster, with a “ multi- 
dimensional ” time span, “ multiple ” physical information, and a 
“ diverse ” structural framework. It facilitates digital analysis and visu -
alization of various static and dynamic physical data within geological 
disaster scenarios. The following key conclusions could be drawn: 
(1) A digital simulation framework for geological disasters has been 
developed based on the MPM, where sensing data of the proto -
type system state and the twin model constitute the core data 
elements for digital simulation. This framework includes three 
steps: 1) Constructing a disaster-scenario twin model by 
extracting key elements from the disaster-prone scene, which 
serves as the data storage medium for digital simulation; 2) 
Performing state inversion of the disaster prototype system based 
on sensing data from the prototype disaster-prone scene, refining 
the selection of physico-mechanical parameters; 3) Updating the 
twin model driven by physico-mechanical models of geotechnical 
materials, and forecasting the future evolution of the disaster.
(2) Taking the Taihe landslide as a case, multi-source monitoring 
data are utilized to carry out the inversion of landslide states. By 
comparing the deformation direction, range, and local collapse 
position between the twin model and the landslide prototype, the 
input parameters of the twin model are constrained and opti -
mized. Based on this, the long-term stability of the landslide is 
simulated by incorporating the strength evolution of geotechnical 
materials, and the future disaster evolution of the Taihe landslide 
over the next ten years is predicted. The Taihe landslide twin

Nevertheless, the simulation-based extrapolation technology in this 
study has certain application limitations. At first, the discretization of 
phased time and the coarse-grained spatial discretization in the analysis 
of 10-year stability evolution are dictated by computational cost con -
straints. Secondly, the MPM-based technology is more applicable to 
colluvial and soil-landslides; the simulation of rock-landslides and col -
lapses requires the development of new algorithms and models. Thirdly, 
the current technology offers an inadequately refined portrayal of 
landslide initiation, necessitating the future development of MPM al -
gorithms incorporating rainfall and seismic triggers. Furthermore, given 
the uncertainty in landslide prototype data like the inference of 
geological lithology and the selection of material parameters, future 
work will delve into uncertainty analysis of prototype data and sensi -
tivity analysis of model input parameters.
