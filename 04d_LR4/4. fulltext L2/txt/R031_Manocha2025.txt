Abstract
Structural health monitoring (SHM) systems are intended to collect continu-
ous, current information on the condition of civic buildings, aircraft, and
industrial gear. These structures may decay over time as a result of slow pro-
cesses such as corrosion and fatigue, as well as unexpected catastrophes such
as natural or man-made disasters. It is important to note, however, that the
components of SHM systems may degrade after installation, resulting in incor-
rect information being supplied to decision-makers. To address this issue,
digital twin (DT) technology has been utilized to provide an accurate represen-
tation of the building's actual state. Additionally, a weighted-Bayesian belief
network (W-BBN) has been integrated into the framework to assess the build-
ing's condition. The framework also includes an example of maintenance and
inspection throughout the lifespan of the structure, incorporating a sophisti-
cated model of the SHM system to evaluate the level of risk. To show the
proposed framework's operations, a numerical analysis and technique demon-
stration are offered, utilizing the building's smart sensors as a reference. The
findings reveal that the unique SHM technology inspired by DT increases the
identification of building health in an efficient and automated way.

artificial intelligence, digital twin, quality assurance, structure health monitoring
1 | INTRODUCTION
Deterioration of a structure is a dangerous phenomenon
that can cause both chemical and physical harm. Environ-
mental conditions, slow mater ial degradation, and unex-
pected events can all play a role in the wear and tear of civil
structures as time progress es. Maintaining structures
requires a consistent strategy for routine inspections and
maintenance.
1 A monitoring system is a sustainable
approach that can minimize economic, health, and environ-
mental hazards and extend the service life of a building. 2,3
Structural health monitoring (SHM) systems have been
developed in recent years to provide information about the
health of a building by identifying “damage-sensitive
features.” These features measure variations in damage
indicators, which are categorized into three types: damage
quantification, damage localization, and damage detection.4
The degree of damage can be determined at multiple levels
of detail based on the information and technology used for
damage assessment. harm loca lization identifies a specific
region, whereas quantification provides more exact infor-
mation about the severity of the harm.

1.2 | State-of-the-art approaches
Various strategies are being developed to combat the
deteriorating processes. The importance of sensor valida-
tion tools (SVTs) in assessing data dependability for
chemical industry monitoring was initially realized by
Dunia et al.
8 Later, Friswell and Inman 9 investigated
the similar approach for SHM uses. Following that,
researchers developed SVTs using single-class classifica-
tion approaches and multivariate statistical methods.
5
One group of researchers investigates each sensor sepa-
rately to determine if the monitoring system is function-
ing correctly,
10 while the second group analyzes the
relationships between sensors in the network to identify
sensor defects by comparing data from all sensors in the
monitoring solution.
11–14 On the other side, there is an
increasing interest in applying machine learning to
uncover faults in diverse systems. 15 It has also been
acknowledged that SVTs are prone to defects due to their
limited operational capabilities and the difficulty in
detecting certain irregularities in the data they collect.
This poses risks that need to be addressed. However, con-
sidering that SVTs are typically expensive and require
dedicated processing units, incorporating them into a
SHM system needs to be evaluated in terms of its actual
value. Additionally, in SHM applications, there is a chal-
lenge of convincing administrators and owners about the
added value and societal benefits of implementing such
systems.
Numerous research has focused on the value of
information (VoI) derived from Bayesian decision the-
ory to determine the possible long-term financial effect
of a SHM system before its deployment.
16,17 This helps
reduce management expenses resulting from collecting
new knowledge. 18 Optimized methods of sensor net-
work implementation, 19 maintenance, 20 and data collec-
tion strategies 21 are examples of these studies. Those
interested can refer to a recent overview of the state of
the art regarding VoI. 22 Information assessment is cru-
cial for VoI evaluation as it is determined prior to the
“real” SHM outcome. 23 Perfect and imperfect information
are the two categories into which information is typically
divided.
16 When complete information is available, the con-
dition of a structure can be f ully understood. However,
when information is imperfect, uncertainty about the struc-
ture's condition remains. In the field of VoI, techniques
such as cumulative error
24 and probability functions25 are
commonly used to model imperfect information. Simula-
tion methods, both simple and complex, can be used to
model imperfect information.
18,26 Some researchers have
explored the relationship between data quality and VoI,
but according to one study, VoI related to the condition of
SHM systems has not yet been discussed.

1.3 | Research gap
There are several categories of sensor malfunctions that
can affect the accuracy of data due to various disrupting
factors. In vibration-based SHM, sensor failures are clas-
sified into seven categories: drift, bias, low accuracy, gain,
constant noise, baseline noise, and continuous record-
ing.
28 The first four types are considered “soft” faults
because they still provide some information about the
structure, while the remaining three are classified as
“hard” faults because they do not offer useful data. If
these errors are not assessed for reliability before proces-
sing the collected data, they can cause severe malfunc-
tions in monitoring systems. Current approaches for
SHM primarily rely on pre-processed environmental fac-
tors rather than raw data, which can result in poor per-
formance.
12 Therefore, it is crucial to develop an effective
SHM strategy that addresses these limitations and facili-
tates preventive maintenance.

2.2 | Virtual structure modeling layer
The proposed DT framework is compared to traditional
architectures in Figure 5. It allows engineers to detect
flaws in advance, improve the control system, and better
prepare for preventing unexpected damage. Additionally,
different infrastructure scenarios can be evaluated in a
virtual environment before making actual changes. The
framework consists of two phases: offline and online.
The offline phase involves high-precision simulation, a
knowledge library, a high-precision geometric model,
and a data library. The online phase includes the diagno-
sis process, model modification, reusability assessment,
and a data library. Each module is explained in more
detail below:
1. Geometric module : This module includes the
computer-aided design (CAD) model that was initially
created and an updated geometric model that reflects
the damaged features of the building for the high-
precision simulation. The geometric condition of a
building changes over time, and there may be differ-
ences between the final entity and the intended CAD
model due to processing errors. Additionally, imper-
fections or damages will occur and need to be dynami-
cally integrated into the geometric model.
2. Simulation module : The module includes simulation
of multiple systems, simulation of multiple physics,
modeling with reduced complexity, and modeling
with surrogates. Since monitoring the health of a
building is complex, its simulation model needs to
consider multiple systems. A building is influenced by
various environmental factors like air temperature,
solar radiation, humidity, wind, precipitation, air pol-
lution, and noise. To account for these factors, a
multi-physics simulation can improve the accuracy of
the model. It is also possible to use surrogate model-
ing techniques to speed up calculations without
sacrificing precision. This module also serves as the
model library for the knowledge library module.
3. Knowledge library module : This component can
deliver a pre-defined degradation status to the diag-
nostic module and can be supplemented by the
diagnosis module with the new degradation status.

proposed SHM framework can be outlined as follows:
1. The DT is distinguished by its functions (model

can be analyzed offline and directly related to the
application's situation for SHM.
3. The DT model differs from typical simulations in that
it may be modified in actual time, with a focus on
updating the model of uncertainty. A practical frame-
work has been developed for structure health manage-
ment, which can serve as a foundation for future
development.

3.4.1 | VoSH determination with
performance validation
The suggested approach for VoSH determination is evalu-
ated using a variety of performance criteria, including
precision, specificity, sensitivity, and F-measure. These
measures are critical because they enable a comprehen-
sive evaluation of model performance, especially in
instances when class imbalance may exist. Accuracy
measures the framework's overall soundness, whereas
sensitivity assesses its capacity to reliably recognize posi-
tive cases. Specificity complements sensitivity by asses-
sing the model's ability to recognize negative cases. The
F-measure combines accuracy and recall into a single sta-
tistic, offering a balance that is especially useful in situa-
tions where false positives and false negatives have
distinct consequences. Furthermore, the data used to
train the model comprises both normal and aberrant
events related to VoSH. This dataset is critical for verify-
ing that the model can effectively generalize to new data,
particularly in anomaly detection. To successfully evalu-
ate the effectiveness of the model, the dataset is split into
training and test sets, usually in an 80:20 ratio. This frac-
tion provides enough training while maintaining a large
amount of data for impartial testing. The training set is
used to fit the model, while the test set evaluates its pre-
dicted performance. In addition, three baseline classifiers
are used to assess the accuracy of VoSH determination:
random forest tree (RFT), decision tree (DT), and k-
nearest neighbor (k-NN) using the scikit-learn module.

4 | DISCUSSION AND
LIMITATION
A smart structure health monitoring solution is urgently
needed to quickly analyze the health of a building and
take preventative measures before severe consequences
occur. While efforts have been made to automate the
analysis of a structure's health, it is mainly done manu-
ally by experienced professionals. However, advanced
analytical tools, such as machine learning or artificial
intelligence, can effectively distinguish different patterns.
The challenge lies in the reliance on IoT sensors, which
can generate anomaly data due to environmental factors.
To overcome this limitation, a framework inspired by
DTs is proposed for structure health monitoring. This

5 | CONCLUSION
Monitoring frameworks for the health of structures often
provide modified data for decision-makers, but there is a
lack of published works on modeling information quality
for structure health analysis. This research aims to
address this gap by proposing a structure monitoring
framework inspired by DT technology and incorporating
the Weighted-Naïve Bayes theory to enable effective anal-
ysis of a structure's health. The framework improves on
existing models by accounting for irregular sensor condi-
tions, such as noise and missing information, through the
use of virtual events. In cases where there are no SHM
information available, virtual events inspired by DTs are
generated to address faulty sensor conditions. In order to
demonstrate the usefulness of the framework, a case
study was conducted and various performance measures
were calculated. The key findings of the proposed solu-
tion are as follows: (1) the SHM framework coupled with
the DT generally provides greater benefits compared to
traditional SHM systems; (2) the use of a DT eliminates
the impact of noise on the SHM results; (3) the suggested
solution's results are greatly influenced by prior knowl-
edge about the structure's condition and SHM systems, as
reflected in the previous chances, as well as value of util-
ity associated with various combinations of activities and
conditions of the architecture. In this sense, our work
contributes to the current body of information by creat-
ing an innovative approach that allows researchers to sys-
tematically monitor a building's health using DTs. Future
studies will look at further practical uses of DTs to sup-
port a wider range of services.
