Abstract: Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are increasingly used for bridge inspection to reduce hazardous 
access and improve scalability. Routes are often manual or coverage-driven, and the collected imagery is not 
always comparable or aligned with maintenance decision needs. This study proposes a knowledge-based UAV 
path planning approach that converts maintenance intent into executable and repeatable inspection routes. The 
method integrates element-level maintenance knowledge from field inspection and maintenance records encoded 
as priority weights and viewing requirements with BIM derived bridge geometry to generate candidate viewpoints 
and element specific inspection patterns for substructure and superstructure. Viewpoints are evaluated using a 
maintenance-weighted utility function. A case study on a conventional concrete box-girder toll-road bridge 
compares the approach with three baselines manual rule-of-thumb, a coverage only planner, and a robustness 
oriented feasibility proxy. Results show routes that remain traceable to maintenance priorities and support cycle-
to-cycle repeatability for continuous inspection planning. 
Keywords: Bridge Inspection; Bridge Maintenance; UAV Path Planning; Knowledge-Based Planning; BIM; 
Digital Twin; Viewpoint Selection; Repeatable Inspection Routes
I.Introduction
Bridges are critical components of transportation networks, and their long-term performance 
strongly depends on effective inspection and maintenance strategies. Aging infrastructure, increasing 
traffic demand, and environmental exposure have significantly intensified the need for reliable and 
timely bridge condition assessment (Agredo Chávez et al., 2024; Mishra et al., 2022). Conventional 
bridge inspections are predominantly based on manual visual surveys, which are time-consuming, 
costly, and often limited by accessibility and safety constraints, particularly for hard-to-reach 
components such as bridge decks, girders, bearings, and piers (Gucunski et al., 2015; Ye, 2020, 2022).
In recent years, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have emerged as a promising alternative for bridge 
inspection, offering improved accessibility, reduced inspection time, and enhanced safety for inspectors 
(Aliyari, 2022; Ayele, 2020). Equipped with high-resolution cameras and stabilized platforms, UAVs 
enable close-range visual inspection without the need for scaffolding, traffic closures, or specialized 
access equipment (Ameli, 2022; Biscarini, 2020). Consequently, UAV-based inspection has been 
increasingly adopted in both research and practice for bridge condition assessment and monitoring.
Alongside the growing use of UAVs, significant research efforts have focused on automated damage 
detection using computer vision and artificial intelligence techniques. Deep learning-based methods 
have demonstrated strong capabilities in detecting cracks, spalling, corrosion, and other surface defects 
from UAV-acquired images (Hu et al., 2024; Kao, 2023; Luciw et al., 2024). While these approaches

have substantially improved post-processing and interpretation of inspection data, they largely assume 
that inspection data are already available and of sufficient quality. The flight planning process that

Existing UAV flight planning strategies for infrastructure inspection are commonly based on generic 
coverage path planning or viewpoint optimization principles originally developed for mapping, 
photogrammetry, or robotic exploration tasks (Aela, 2024; Fang, 2024; Hildmann & Kovacs, 2019). 
These methods typically aim to maximize surface coverage or minimize flight time and energy 
consumption. Although effective for geometric data acquisition, such approaches do not explicitly 
account for bridge maintenance objectives, such as the prioritization of critical structural components 
or the need for consistent inspection viewpoints across multiple inspection cycles (Falorca, 2021; Ri, 
2024).
More recent studies have attempted to tailor UAV path planning to bridge inspection scenarios by 
considering environmental constraints, complex geometries, or specific structural configurations. For 
example, research has addressed UAV navigation under bridge decks with limited GNSS availability 
(Shults et al., 2023), wind-aware path planning for long-span bridges (Xu, 2021), and surface-specific 
inspection of bridge substructures (Omar & Nehdi, n.d.). Despite these advances, most existing 
approaches remain predominantly geometry-driven, focusing on obstacle avoidance and surface 
coverage rather than inspection relevance from a maintenance perspective.
Parallel to developments in UAV inspection, the field of bridge maintenance has seen increasing 
interest in knowledge-based systems and decision support tools. Knowledge graphs, ontologies, and 
data-driven frameworks have been proposed to organize maintenance knowledge, defect characteristics, 
and decision rules in a structured manner (Gao et al., 2024; Hoang, 2021). More recently, advanced 
reasoning frameworks integrating large language models with bridge maintenance knowledge graphs 
have demonstrated the potential to support expert-level maintenance decision-making (Wang et al., 
2026). These studies highlight the importance of domain knowledge in guiding maintenance actions but 
generally focus on diagnosis and recommendation rather than the operational execution of inspections.
As a result, a critical gap remains between maintenance knowledge and UAV-based inspection 
planning. Current UAV inspection workflows rarely incorporate maintenance knowledge, such as 
component criticality, historical damage patterns, or inspection requirements, into the path planning 
process. Consequently, inspection routes are often inconsistent across inspection cycles, limiting their 
usefulness for long-term monitoring and maintenance planning (Yeum & Dyke, 2015).
To address this gap, this study proposes a knowledge-based approach to UAV path planning for 
bridge inspection and maintenance. The proposed approach integrates maintenance knowledge with 
bridge geometry to define inspection objectives and generate UAV flight paths that are inspection-
oriented, maintenance-relevant, and repeatable. By shifting the focus from generic coverage to 
knowledge-informed inspection planning, the proposed framework aims to support continuous bridge 
inspection and provide a practical foundation for long-term maintenance-oriented UAV deployment.

2.1 UAV-Based Bridge Inspection in a Maintenance Context

as a safer alternative to conventional access methods, but also as a pathway to make inspections faster 
and more scalable. Recent application reviews report that drones can capture close-range imagery for 
components that are otherwise difficult to access—such as the underside of decks, substructures, and 
elevated superstructure elements—while reducing the need for traffic closures or risky manual access

At the same time, the literature makes it clear that inspection value is determined by more than the 
mere availability of drone imagery. Several studies emphasize that UAV inspection outcomes can vary 
widely because of differences in flight execution, camera-to-surface distance, viewing angles, and 
lighting conditions, particularly around shaded regions or complex geometries (Hou et al., 2022; 
Panigati et al., 2025). These findings highlight a practical challenge that is often understated in 
technology-driven demonstrations: unless data acquisition is performed in a consistent manner, it 
becomes difficult to compare condition information across inspection cycles and to use UAV outputs 
reliably for maintenance planning.
A parallel stream of research has focused on image-based defect interpretation, where deep 
learning and related computer vision techniques are used to identify cracks and surface damage from 
captured imagery. This line of work has produced promising results and continues to mature rapidly 
(Aliyari et al., 2021). However, these advances typically assume that the input imagery already satisfies 
inspection needs in terms of resolution, visibility, and coverage. In practice, the quality and relevance 
of the data depend heavily on how the drone is guided during inspection, including whether the UAV 
repeatedly captures comparable viewpoints and meets inspection-specific viewing requirements. As a 
result, recent reviews increasingly point to flight planning and operational standardization as limiting 
factors for turning UAV inspection from a one-off assessment into a repeatable maintenance-support 
workflow (Hou et al., 2022; Panigati et al., 2025).
In maintenance settings, repeatability and prioritization become especially important. Unlike 
exploratory surveys, bridge inspections are expected to support consistent condition tracking over time 
and to focus effort where it matters most for maintenance decisions. The emerging consensus is that 
UAV inspection will be most valuable when it can consistently capture comparable viewpoints and 
sufficiently detailed observations for maintenance-critical components, rather than simply maximizing 
generic surface coverage (Hou et al., 2022; Toriumi et al., 2022). This maintenance perspective

2.2 UAV Path Planning for Bridge Inspection
Research on UAV path planning for bridge inspection has grown alongside the wider adoption 
of drones in infrastructure monitoring. Much of this work starts from a practical observation: even when 
a drone platform and camera are suitable for inspection, the usefulness of the collected data depends 
strongly on where the UAV flies, how it approaches each component, and how consistently viewpoints 
can be reproduced. As a result, many recent studies formulate path planning as a combination of 
viewpoint selection and route optimization, typically aiming to reduce redundant observations while 
maintaining adequate coverage and feasible maneuvering around complex bridge geometries (Li et al., 
2025).
Several contributions have emphasized coverage- and efficiency-oriented planning, where the 
primary objective is to maximize inspected surface area or minimize flight time and path length. This 
direction is often motivated by limited battery endurance and the need to complete inspections within 
operational constraints. In bridge contexts, the focus has been placed on distributing viewpoints to 
reduce overlap and avoid unnecessarily sharp turns, which can improve both flight stability and practical 
field execution (Li et al., 2025). Such approaches provide important foundations for operational 
feasibility, especially when inspection tasks are framed as surface coverage problems.
Another line of work has sought to address bridge-specific operational challenges that cannot be 
captured by generic coverage formulations. Environmental disturbance, particularly wind effects, has 
been incorporated into planning and evaluation to improve route realism for long-span bridges and 
exposed conditions (Xu & Dai, 2024). In addition, the GNSS shadowing that frequently occurs beneath 
bridge decks and near substructures has motivated planning strategies that account for navigation 
limitations and operational modes, recognizing that feasible inspection routes may require different 
behaviors under degraded positioning conditions (Wang, 2024). These studies improve robustness by 
acknowledging that bridges are not open, ideal environments for UAV navigation.
2.3 Knowledge-Based Bridge Maintenance, Gaps, and Research Positioning
Bridge maintenance research increasingly uses knowledge graphs and ontologies to structure 
components, defects, inspection records, and maintenance actions for more consistent retrieval and 
decision support (Zhang et al., 2023). Recent studies extend this by enriching maintenance knowledge 
from textual records and formalizing actionable preservation rules, while LLM-assisted reasoning has 
been introduced to emulate expert workflows over maintenance knowledge graphs (Gao et al., 2024; 
He et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). However, these approaches still largely produce diagnosis or 
recommendations rather than executable inspection actions. This leaves a clear gap in translating 
maintenance knowledge into inspection objectives and repeatable UAV routes for continuous 
inspection and maintenance planning, as summarized in Table 1.

UAV inspection and planning studies have improved feasibility and robustness in bridge 
environments, addressing factors such as wind, GNSS shadowing, and viewpoint redundancy, and some 
work has linked UAV trajectories to digital-twin or bridge management workflows (Bono et al., 2022). 
However, these approaches rarely formulate planning as the translation of maintenance knowledge into 
explicit inspection objectives, so routes are still largely derived from geometric and operational 
considerations while maintenance knowledge remains separate. Taken together, the literature points to 
a gap in connecting knowledge-based maintenance systems with UAV path planning through an explicit 
pipeline that maps component criticality, inspection requirements, and condition history into executable 
and repeatable routes. This study addresses that gap through a knowledge-based planning approach that 
uses maintenance intent to define inspection objectives and combines it with bridge geometry and UAV 
constraints to generate feasible, element-appropriate routes that remain consistent across inspection 
cycles.
III. PROPOSED FRAMEWORK
3.1 Framework Overview 
This study proposes a knowledge-based framework for UAV path planning that is intended to 
support bridge inspection as a repeatable input to maintenance planning. The framework is designed 
around a simple premise: inspection routes should not be derived solely from geometric coverage or 
operational convenience, but should reflect maintenance intent by prioritizing maintenance-critical 
components and meeting inspection-specific viewing requirements. Figure 2 illustrates the overall 
workflow and the information flow between its main elements.

The framework takes three primary inputs. First, it requires a representation of bridge geometry 
that enables element-level inspection planning, such as decks, girders, piers, and other substructure 
regions. This geometry can be obtained from different sources depending on data availability, including 
a simplified structural model, a surface model derived from point clouds, or geometry extracted from a 
BIM environment; the framework does not depend on a single data format as long as the geometry can 
be represented in an inspection-relevant way. Second, the framework incorporates maintenance 
knowledge as prior information that captures inspection priorities and requirements. In practice, this 
includes component criticality, historical condition or damage patterns, and element-specific 
requirements related to camera-to-surface distance, viewing angle, and expected observation quality. 
Third, UAV operational constraints are considered to ensure field feasibility, including stand-off 
distance for safe operation, sensor field-of-view limits, endurance, and any site-specific operational 
restrictions.
Based on these inputs, the framework produces two outputs that are directly relevant to 
maintenance-oriented inspection. The first is a set of inspection viewpoints that are explicitly tied to 
bridge elements and their inspection requirements, rather than being selected purely for generic 
coverage. The second is an executable UAV route that connects these viewpoints while respecting 
operational constraints. A key design requirement is repeatability: the framework is intended to generate 
inspection paths that remain consistent across inspection cycles unless maintenance knowledge 
indicates a justified change in inspection priorities. This is essential for longitudinal comparability, 
allowing inspection outcomes to be interpreted not as isolated observations but as part of a continuous 
monitoring process that informs maintenance planning over time.
3.2 Knowledge-based planning workflow
Building on the overview in Section 3.1, this subsection explains how maintenance knowledge is 
used to shape UAV inspection planning in a way that remains practical for field deployment and useful 
for maintenance decision-making. The workflow begins by translating maintenance intent into explicit 
inspection objectives, rather than treating the bridge as a generic surface to be covered. This emphasis 
reflects recent observations that UAV inspection outcomes can be inconsistent across cycles when route

The first step is to interpret maintenance knowledge into element-level priorities and inspection 
requirements. Each bridge element is associated with a priority level that reflects maintenance 
relevance, for example based on component criticality, historical condition patterns, or known 
vulnerability to specific deterioration mechanisms. In addition, each element is assigned basic viewing 
requirements that define what constitutes an acceptable inspection observation, such as a desired 
camera-to-surface distance, a preferred range of viewing angles, and minimum expectations for 
visibility and coverage. Together, these attributes determine not only which elements should be 
inspected first, but also how strictly the inspection should be performed for each element.
The second step uses bridge geometry to generate inspection patterns that are appropriate for 
different structural elements. Instead of relying on a single coverage strategy, the workflow adopts 
element-specific inspection logic. For instance, deck undersides are naturally suited to longitudinal or 
strip-like passes that maintain stable stand-off distance under shaded conditions, while girders can be 
inspected through guided sweeps aligned with their dominant direction. Piers and substructures may 
require orbital or circumferential viewpoints to ensure that occlusions are minimized and multiple faces 
are observed. The role of geometry in this step is therefore not limited to collision avoidance; it provides 
the spatial context needed to translate inspection requirements into feasible candidate viewpoints.
The third step selects candidate viewpoints and constructs an executable route under UAV 
operational constraints. Candidate viewpoints are evaluated based on their ability to satisfy element-
specific viewing requirements while contributing to the inspection objectives defined by maintenance 
knowledge. The route is then formed by connecting the selected viewpoints in a feasible order that 
respects practical constraints such as stand-off distance, field-of-view limits, endurance, and site-
specific restrictions. At this stage, efficiency remains important, but it is treated as a constraint-aware 
planning consideration rather than the sole objective.

4.2 Viewpoint Selection and Route Generation
Candidate viewpoints are generated using bridge geometry and element-specific inspection 
patterns. Deck undersides are sampled through strip-like passes, girders through directional sweeps, and 
piers through multi-face viewpoints to reduce occlusion. Viewpoints are filtered for feasibility under 
UAV constraints, then associated with utility 𝑈(𝑣)and travel cost 𝐶(𝑣𝑖,𝑣𝑗). Route generation selects and 
orders viewpoints to maximize inspection utility under a flight budget, which is naturally framed as a 
prize-collecting or orienteering-type problem rather than a shortest-path task. A practical 
implementation uses a two-stage procedure: viewpoints are selected using a utility-to-cost criterion, 
then ordered using a lightweight routing heuristic to reduce travel cost while preserving inspection 
value. Repeatability is supported through deterministic viewpoint sampling and stable element anchors, 
with knowledge updates triggering local refinements rather than full route regeneration (Hou et al., 
2022; Toriumi et al., 2022).
4.3 Evaluation Metrics and Baseline Comparisons
Evaluation considers maintenance usefulness alongside operational feasibility. Maintenance 
usefulness is measured through critical-element coverage (the proportion of high-priority elements 
meeting minimum viewing requirements) and viewpoint compliance (distance/angle satisfaction). 
Operational feasibility is assessed through path efficiency (flight distance or estimated time), while 
repeatability is quantified through route similarity across cycles and consistency in inspected elements 
and compliant viewpoints. Baselines include a manual rule-of-thumb plan, a geometry-driven coverage 
route without maintenance weighting, and a feasibility-oriented robust plan (e.g., wind- or GNSS-
aware) that does not encode maintenance priorities. These comparisons isolate the contribution of 
knowledge-driven objectives, geometry-informed inspection patterns, and repeatability design (Hou et 
al., 2022; Panigati et al., 2025).

V. Case Study and Experimental Setup
5.1 Study Area and Bridge Model
The case study considers a conventional concrete box-girder highway bridge on a toll road. The 
bridge is modeled at the element level using a BIM-based geometry source, which enables consistent 
identification of inspection targets and supports repeatable planning across inspection cycles. In this 
study, the bridge geometry is organized into maintainable components aligned with the project work 
breakdown structure (WBS), separating substructure and superstructure elements. This element-
level representation is used to (i) define inspection elements ℰ, (ii) assign maintenance priorities and 
viewing requirements per element, and (iii) generate geometry-informed candidate viewpoints and 
executable UAV inspection routes.

5.2 Maintenance Knowledge and Inspection Requirements
The maintenance knowledge used in this case study was derived from field inspection 
documentation and maintenance records obtained for the concrete box-girder toll-road overpass 
described in Section 5.1. These records were consolidated at the element level and aligned with the 
bridge WBS so that condition information and maintenance intent can be linked directly to executable 
UAV inspection planning. The resulting knowledge representation is summarized in Table 2, which 
reports the inspection elements ℰ, their priority weights, and the viewing requirements adopted for route 
generation.
Two complementary components are defined for each element 𝑒 ∈ ℰ. The first is a maintenance-
informed priority weight 𝑃𝑒∈ [0,1], which reflects how strongly the inspection should focus on element 
𝑒from a maintenance perspective. In practical bridge maintenance settings, 𝑃𝑒is driven by factors such 
as component criticality, historical condition patterns, and recent inspection findings. To preserve 
consistency across inspection cycles while allowing priorities to respond to new evidence, the study 
adopts a smoothed update formulation,
𝑃(𝑡+1)𝑒 = clip[0,1](𝛼𝑃(𝑡)𝑒 + (1 ― 𝛼)𝑃(𝑡)𝑒 ),
where 𝑃(𝑡)𝑒 represents the evidence-informed priority estimate derived from the most recent 
inspection outcomes and 𝛼controls the stability of the prioritization over time. This design supports 
repeatable inspection planning while accommodating maintenance-driven changes when new condition 
information becomes available.
The second component is the element-specific set of viewing requirements 𝑅𝑒, which defines the 
minimum conditions for an observation to be considered inspection-valid for that element. In this study, 
𝑅𝑒is specified through stand-off distance limits [𝑑𝑚𝑖𝑛𝑒 ,𝑑𝑚𝑎𝑥𝑒 ]and admissible viewing angle limits 
[𝜃𝑚𝑖𝑛𝑒 ,𝜃𝑚𝑎𝑥𝑒 ], together with basic line-of-sight feasibility. These requirements are intended to reflect 
inspection practicality and data sufficiency. For example, under-deck and box-girder observations 
typically demand tighter distance control and more stable viewing angles to ensure comparable surface 
detail, whereas pier observations can tolerate broader viewpoints due to multi-face accessibility.

5.3 UAV Platform and Operational Constraints
To ensure that the generated inspection routes remain executable in real bridge environments, this 
study defines UAV capabilities and operational constraints that bound both viewpoint feasibility and 
route construction. The UAV is treated as a multirotor inspection platform equipped with a stabilized 
RGB camera, reflecting typical bridge inspection practice where hovering and controlled stand-off 
distances are required. Rather than binding the method to a single manufacturer model, constraints are 
expressed in a platform-agnostic form, so that the framework can be reproduced for different UAVs 
provided comparable sensing and endurance envelopes are available.

Recent studies increasingly move beyond “UAVs can inspect bridges” toward “how UAV 
inspection can be operationalized as a repeatable program.” Reviews emphasize that adoption barriers 
are often practical—standardization, comparability, and integration with decision processes—rather 
than purely sensing capability (Bolourian, 2020; Bono, 2022). Methodologically, several strands 
address parts of this puzzle. Coverage path planning and viewpoint optimization reduce redundancy 
and improve flight smoothness (Li et al., 2022), wind-aware planning improves realism in exposed 
environments (Xu & Dai, 2024), and GNSS-shadow-aware substructure planning improves feasibility 
under decks (Kim et al., 2023). Digital-twin-linked approaches define reference trajectories and connect 
UAV inspection to bridge management workflows, which strengthens integration at the system level 
(Bono et al., 2022). Practical flight planning toolkits also show that “optimized routing” can be 
embedded in repeatable workflows when geometry is available (Fatemidokht et al., 2021). At the same 
time, adaptive inspection strategies increasingly exploit prior information to trigger detailed inspections 
in areas likely to contain defects (Chen et al., 2023), but these approaches still often treat maintenance 
knowledge as an auxiliary cue rather than a formal objective that controls viewpoint validity and cross-
cycle repeatability.
Within this landscape, the distinguishing contribution demonstrated here is the explicit mapping 
of maintenance intent into inspection objectives and then into executable routing decisions under a 
shared budget. The proposed method is not positioned as replacing robustness- or coverage-focused 
advances; instead, it complements them by making “maintenance relevance” the primary planning 
signal while keeping feasibility filters compatible with wind/GNSS or other operational constraints. 
This integration is important because bridge maintenance decisions depend on consistent, comparable 
observations of the same critical elements over time, not simply on the availability of UAV imagery. 
The heatmap visualization in the BIM environment (Figure 6) further supports this positioning by 
showing that the element priorities driving the route are traceably mapped onto geometry, enabling 
transparent communication between maintenance engineers and UAV operators.

VII. Conclusion
This paper presented a knowledge-based approach to UAV path planning for bridge inspection and 
maintenance, motivated by the practical need to make UAV inspections not only feasible, but also 
repeatable and directly useful for maintenance decision-making. The key idea is to translate 
maintenance intent into explicit inspection objectives by assigning element-level priorities and viewing 
requirements, and then to generate executable routes by selecting and ordering candidate viewpoints 
under a mission budget. By embedding maintenance weighting into the planning objective, the 
framework shifts route design from generic surface coverage toward inspection plans that target 
maintenance-critical components with inspection-valid viewpoints.
The case study on a conventional concrete box-girder toll-road bridge demonstrated that the 
proposed planner can operate within standard UAV operational constraints while producing routes that 
are clearly distinguishable from manual and geometry-driven baselines. Under the same budget 
assumptions, all compared methods achieved full coverage of high-priority elements (CEC = 1.0), 
indicating that the bridge-scale inspection scope was achievable across strategies. Differences emerged 
more clearly in route structure and compliance-oriented indicators, where the proposed method provides 
a transparent mechanism to maintain consistent inspection intent across cycles rather than relying on 
operator heuristics or average-coverage optimization. The integration of BIM-based visualization 
further strengthened traceability by confirming that element priorities driving the route are correctly 
mapped onto the bridge geometry, enabling clearer communication between maintenance teams and 
UAV operators.
From a research perspective, the contribution of this work lies in making the maintenance-to-
execution link explicit: maintenance knowledge is operationalized as a route planning objective, rather 
than remaining a post-processing or decision-support artifact. This positioning complements recent 
advances in geometry-driven coverage optimization and robustness-aware navigation by providing a 
planning layer that can incorporate those feasibility improvements while preserving maintenance 
relevance. In practical deployments, this approach supports continuous inspection by enabling 
comparable viewpoints over time and allowing routes to adapt selectively when maintenance priorities 
change, rather than requiring complete re-planning from scratch.
Several limitations provide clear directions for future work. First, the current feasibility modeling 
uses simplified proxies for environmental and navigation effects; integration with higher-fidelity wind 
fields, GNSS-denied localization models, and obstacle-aware planning would strengthen operational 
realism. Second, viewpoint validity is defined through interpretable distance and angle requirements, 
but future studies should link these criteria more directly to measurable data quality outcomes such as 
crack detectability limits, photogrammetric reconstruction accuracy, or uncertainty in defect 
quantification. Third, although element priorities were derived from maintenance records and mapped 
to BIM, broader validation across multiple bridge types and inspection regimes is needed to establish 
generalizability and to refine how priorities and requirements should be standardized across agencies.
Overall, the proposed knowledge-based planning framework provides a practical step toward 
maintenance-oriented UAV inspection programs, where flight routes are not only flyable, but also 
repeatable, traceable to maintenance intent, and suitable for long-term condition tracking and 
maintenance planning.
