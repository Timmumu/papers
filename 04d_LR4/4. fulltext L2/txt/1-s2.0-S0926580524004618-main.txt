A B S T R A C T
Roofs are one of the building elements most exposed to environment-induced deterioration, and their
deterioration can rapidly result in potential safety hazards to occupants or even buildings’ failure. In pursuit of
cost-effective, efficient and pro-active building roof monitoring, this paper presents a computing pipeline for:
1. Automated roof orthophoto generation that uses as input the 3D reconstruction from structure-from-motion
photogrammetry and the building’s Digital Twin (DT) model, and outputs occlusion-free panel orthophotos with
metric resolution. 2. Automated orthophoto semantic segmentation using deep learning to extract visible roof
sub-components from the orthophotos. Various segmentation models are tested and contrasted. The approaches
are validated with three real case studies of traditional buildings with slated pitched roofs. The proposed
approach employs a generic pipeline which could be applied to any element of the building fabric.
1. Introduction
As a key component of building infrastructure, the roof is highly ex-
posed to environmental elements. Elevated temperatures, moisture, and
material soiling can lead to staining, weathering, and damage to various
materials [ 1]. Over time, these factors contribute to increased roof
fragility, component failure, and load redistribution, posing additional
risks to the building and its occupants [ 2]. For instance, significant
exposure to weather often leads to deterioration in the slated areas
of building roofs [ 3]. Moreover, extreme climate events such as high
summer temperatures, heavy winter precipitation, unpredictable winds,
and intense storms are exacerbating the deterioration of buildings.
In the case of traditional buildings, proactive conservation measures
to prevent failures have become crucial [ 4]. Consequently, routine
monitoring of building roofs is essential to detect and address defects
promptly.
Visual inspection is a widely established method for inspecting
and diagnosing building roofs, both flat and pitched [ 5,6]. However,
conventional methods heavily depend on manual effort for both obser-
vation and analysis: surveyors must access roofs physically, requiring
them to climb and walk around, which demands specific skills and ex-
perience. Typically, scaffolding is necessary for safe access, significantly
increasing the time, cost, and safety risks associated with the inspection
process. Overall, the traditional manual roof monitoring approach is
costly, inefficient, and fraught with safety hazards. In contrast, the use

While the advancements in efficient and safe visual data acquisition
are widely adopted in practice, the analysis of the acquired images of-

unstructured format within text documents [ 6]. For historic buildings,
it is common to include manually annotated orthophotos of façades
(which cover pitched roof panels) to indicate deteriorations and defects
(refer to Fig. 1). Orthophotos offer the advantage of providing uniform
scale across the entire image, facilitating the measurability of all de-
tails. This uniformity makes it easier to compare deterioration across
different sections of the orthophoto, thereby aiding in quantifying
maintenance and repair needs [ 9]. However, generating orthophotos
for certain architectural elements, especially roofs, can be problematic
due to potential occlusions by chimneys or other structures.
In the broader Facility Management (FM) industry, Digital Twins
of building assets — typically derived from Building Information Mod-
elling (BIM) models — are proposed to record life cycle information of
assets and support asset managers make decisions related to operation
and maintenance, potentially aided by Artificial Intelligence (AI) [ 11].
However, this concept remains largely aspirational, with actual imple-
mentations still in their early stages and primarily considered for new
buildings, which benefit from modern design and construction practices

This paper aims to contribute to the development of DT-based
solutions for the safe and efficient monitoring of roofs. The paper more
specifically focuses on traditional slated roofs that are prominent in
historic buildings but still common in modern construction. First, we
propose a method for generating high-resolution vertical orthophotos
for pitched building roofs using an existing building DT. This method
aims to produce images that are free from occlusions caused by other
building elements and exhibit reduced blur. Secondly, we develop
a deep learning-based approach for semantically segmenting the or-
thophotos into their constituent sub-elements, such as slates, leadwork,
and others. This segmentation is crucial as it allows subsequent deteri-
oration and defect detection tools to operate optimally, being trained
and applied specifically to the materials they are designed to assess.
The remaining of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2
reviews the literature and state of the art on: roof inspection, the
applications of orthophoto and photogrammetry, and image semantic
segmentation techniques. Section 3 gives an overview of the proposed
pipeline, with the methods for its two successive stages of orthophoto
generation and orthophoto semantic segmentation detailed in Sec-
tions 4 and 6, respectively. Sections 5 and 7 report the experimental
results obtained for those two stages, respectively. Finally, Section 10
concludes the work, reviewing its strengths and limitations and offering
some outlooks for future work.
This paper builds on the two conference papers, [14,15], that intro-
duced the two stages of the method presented here, i.e. orthophoto gen-
eration and orthophoto semantic segmentation. This paper revisits the
presentation of the methods (in particular the orthophoto generation
one) and extends their validations with additional case studies.
2. Related works
2.1. Digital twinning technologies for building management and monitoring
DT technology can be used for many purposes in the built environ-
ment: A BIM-based DT is used as a database, which links real-time
collected data (e.g., occupancy count) and static data (e.g., mainte-
nance history and technical documentation) to the 3D representation
of facility [ 16]. These links help ease information retrieval for var-
ious building monitoring and maintenance applications. For exam-
ple, Seghezzi et al. [17] propose a Building Management System (BMS)
based on an occupancy-oriented DT to detect actual room occupancy,
for optimising space management, organisation, and cleaning activities.
Another area of active research in the DT domain is the creation
of DTs for existing buildings. For example, Bassier and Vergauwen
[18] present a novel method to automatically reconstruct BIM wall
objects and their topology, Lin et al. [ 19] use the 3D reconstructed
data of elevated structures to realise defect detection within an end-to-
end robotic bridge inspection system, and Armeni et al. [ 20] propose
a hierarchical, detection-based approach for semantically parsing the
point cloud data of buildings.
In the context of historic buildings, Rocha et al. [ 21] employ a
manual modelling workflow based on photogrammetry and laser scan-
ning data to develop a BIM model of a historic house in Lisbon for
conservation and restoration.
Within the context of Digital Twinning for building fabric monitor-
ing, there is great interest in automating data acquisition and process-
ing in order to efficiently, robustly and safely detect and monitor de-
fects, and support computer-aided maintenance decision-making [ 22].
Regarding defect detection, Valero et al. [ 23] use machine learning
to detect defects in 3D point clouds of masonry walls using machine
learning, while Idjaton et al. [ 24] detect spalling zones in orthophotos
of limestone walls using a deep neural network. And Loverdos and
Sarhosis [25] apply deep learning to a large brickwork masonry walls
image dataset to realise automated brick segmentation and crack de-
tection. Regarding roof image analysis, work seems to have mainly
be reported for the monitoring of flat roofs. Yudin et al. [ 26] use
aerial images for flat roof defect segmentation (hollows, swelling, folds,
patches and breaks), based on several deep neural networks.
To our knowledge, no work has been reported to date that focuses
on pitched slated roofs. In comparison to those other building fabric
elements, historic pitched slated roofs have a more complex composi-
tion with multiple sub-components (slate, leadwork, tower, etc.), which
brings challenges to defect detection. To realise DT-based slated roof
panel condition monitoring, it is thus necessary to distinguish the sub-
components inside the roof panel orthophoto, so that specifically-built
defect detection solutions can be applied to each sub-component with
uniform materials.
2.2. Photogrammetry in AEC-FM sector
As a popular capturing reality method, digital structure-from-
motion photogrammetry has been an active topic for generating 3D
models of buildings due to its high level of automation and the fact
that it can generate 3D data at low cost [ 27]. Thanks to the increased
availability of UAVs, UAV-based structure-from-motion photogrammetry
has been applied in several contexts, such as archaeology, disaster
monitoring, construction monitoring, building surveying, or land map-
ping [28]. Due to the feature of non-destruction and remote sensing,
this technology is particularly suitable for roof data capturing.

Structure-from-motion photogrammetry can support various use
cases in the AEC-FM sector. An important one is the creation of
semantically-rich 3D models, or Digital Twins (DTs), of existing build-
ings or infrastructure assets, commonly referred to as ‘Scan-to-BIM’
[29–31]. In particular, Golparvar-Fard et al. [ 32] have focused on
automatically generating as-built models from unstructured collec-
tion of construction images by digital photography (Scan-to-BIM by
photogrammetry). Civil infrastructure elements and construction opera-
tions can be monitored by aligning the photogrammetric reconstructed
model to the as-planned BIM model (Scan-vs-BIM) [ 33]. During main-
tenance, ‘Scan-vs-BIM’ [ 34] can be applied, by comparing the as-
designed model and 3D data, to identify the objects and any change
(e.g. structural displacements).
2.3. Orthophotos in building monitoring
As a photogrammetric map with a uniform dimensional scale, anor-
thophoto corrects the distortion and relief, and its generation is achieved
by an orthographic projection on vertical planes [ 35,36]. Orthopho-
tos are convenient and are widely used in practice for monitoring
buildings for actual surface measurement and analysis, in particular
façade elevations. In recent research, high-resolution orthophotos of
façades have been used as input data for automated detection of façade
defects [23,24,37].
However, different from building façades, roofs (especially pitched
roofs) have a more complex composition and are often partially oc-
cluded by other building elements, such as chimneys, dormers, aeri-
als, satellite dishes, etc [ 38]. As a result, building façade elevation
orthophotos may not enable a full view of roofs, which can cause
difficulty in monitoring. Fig. 2 shows an example of façade elevation
orthophoto commonly generated for a building. One can see that, while
the stone façade(s) appear(s) fully, the middle roof panel is occluded
by the chimneys, pipes, etc. These problems challenge the generation
of clear and complete orthophotos (without occlusion and with limited
blur), and ultimately the development of automatic defect detection
models, as the models need to be agnostic to different image acquisition
situations (e.g. angle and distance).
Moreover, roof panels typically consist of sub-components or parts
made from a variety of materials, such as slate tiles and leadwork.
For the effective application of defect detection methods, such as those
previously presented in the context of masonry, it becomes essential
to first segment the orthophotos into these sub-components. This seg-
mentation allows for the isolation of target areas, particularly slate
tiles which cover the largest areas and are most susceptible to frequent
deterioration—thereby facilitating more precise and effective analysis.
2.4. Semantic segmentation and its use in construction informatics
2.4.1. Semantic segmentation
Semantic segmentation was developed decades ago and has been
developed into a well-established tool in Computer Vision with its
powerful pixel-level segmentation ability. This technique is applied to
different types of data, including 2D image and video, and 3D point
cloud data. In construction informatics research dealing with 2D and 3D
data (e.g. Scan-to-BIM), semantic segmentation is increasingly regarded
as an essential step after data collection, to provide further information
useful to support subsequent tasks such as object detection [ 39]. This
technique has been applied to different types of data, including: (1)
2D image of indoor scene [ 40] and aerial images of different architec-
ture [41]; (2) 3D point cloud of building interiors [ 39], plumbing and
structural components [42], 4D point cloud (3D point cloud videos with
temporal information) captured by auto-driving cars and robot [ 43].
Specifically in its application to 2D images, semantic segmentation
enables assigning a class label to each pixel of an image, and such pixel-
level semantic information can help make judgements or be leveraged
by other tasks [44,45]. Traditional machine learning methods, such as
object contour detection, recognition, simple filtering and other image
processing work, can realise segmentation, especially in some contexts
where the colour, texture and other features are uniform within the
same object, e.g. in some medical science studies [ 46]. These machine
learning-based methods rely on the careful design of feature extractors
by humans, but they have the advantage of lower requirements in terms
of computational power and data quantity.
In comparison, deep learning models aim to learn those features
from the data itself and commonly outperform machine learning mod-
els if they are trained with sufficiently large and representative datasets.
Different deep architectures have been introduced, Convolutional Neu-
ral Networks (CNNs) being prominent. As a result of active research
in this area, the performance of semantic segmentation models has
greatly improved, not only in accuracy but also in speed [ 47]. dif-
ferent CNN-based deep learning models have been proposed for 2D
image data segmentation. DeepLabV3, a widely used system developed
by Google [ 48], can handle the problem of segmenting objects at
multiple scales with cascaded module and Atrous Spatial Pyramid
Pooling(ASPP). Compared to DeepLabV3, PointRend demonstrates better
performance (higher mIoU value), by extracting the point features
made up of fine-grained features and coarse prediction [ 49]. Specif-
ically, PointRend handles better the problems of smooth regions and
blurry contours from the bilinear sampling process. Thanks to the
recognisable features, objects can be easily detected and classified, but
the precision of contour detection is low.
In recent years, Vision Transformer (ViT) has achieved performance
superior to the commonly used CNNs, by splitting each image into
patches. Due to their outstanding performance in image classification,
these models have also been explored for semantic segmentation: Seg-
menter can capture global interaction between elements of a scene using
transformer, rather than the traditional convolutions, which would eas-
ily cause information loss [ 50]. SegFormer reaches better performance
and efficiency by re-designing the Transformer encoder and uses a
simple multilayer perceptron (MLP) decoder [51]. However, all authors
also point out that ViT relies heavily on large-scale datasets to achieve
good performances [52].
Within these main classes of deep learning models, different variants
can be created by modifying different components, such as the loss
function. Focal Loss is a commonly used loss function for dealing with
class imbalance and putting more focus on the hard and misclassified
examples, by multiplying each class loss with a weighting factor [ 53].
Dice Loss can deal with the imbalance problem between foreground
and background, by giving more importance to the foreground than
the background, thus making the model region-related [54]. These two
loss functions alongside the defaultCross-Entropy Loss can be paired and
used together in improving model training [ 55].
Besides, label correction is a common practice in semantic seg-
mentation, particularly in the task of 2D image segmentation. Label
correction involves refining and updating the annotations of training
data to improve the quality and accuracy of segmentation models. This
process addresses issues such as annotation errors, inconsistencies, and

ambiguities, which are prevalent in large datasets. Wang and Yushke-

fusion and corrective learning, which combines segmentations from
multiple atlases and corrects label errors during training. Hesse and
Namburete [ 57] focused on improving U-Net segmentation through
active contour-based label correction, which corrects imperfect ground-
truth labels by aligning them with object boundaries, thus enhancing
boundary predictions in medical ultrasound imaging. Ibrahim et al.
[58] introduced a semi-supervised framework with self-correcting net-
works, which uses a small set of fully supervised images and a larger
weak set with only bounding box labels. Their self-correction module
iteratively improves the generated labels during training, significantly
reducing the annotation effort while maintaining high performance.
Similarly, Mendel et al. [59] proposed an Error-Correcting Supervision
approach, where a secondary correction network assists the primary
segmentation model by generating pseudo-labels for unlabelled data
during training, resulting in consistent improvements over supervised
baselines. Integrating these advanced label correction techniques into
the training pipeline not only enhances performance metrics but also
contributes to the generalisation ability of the models, making them
more effective in real-world applications. The semantic segmentation
method employed herein also applies a contour-based label correc-
tion, using the prior knowledge contained in the BIM model that the
segmented images are aligned with.
2.4.2. Semantic segmentation in construction informatics
Semantic segmentation has been actively explored in construction
informatics using different models, now principally deep learning mod-
els. Examples include: equipment and worker segmentation in images
for visual understanding on construction sites [ 60], building interior
segmentation of point clouds [ 20], material and damage (e.g., cracks,
spalling, and corrosion) segmentation in images [ 61,62] and point
cloud [63,64]. In the context of historic building monitoring, semantic
segmentation has also been used in masonry wall semantic segmenta-
tion [65], crack segmentation in masonry wall images [ 66]. However,
there is seemingly no specific research dealing with semantic segmen-
tation of data (images or point clouds) from other building fabric
elements like roofs.
2.5. Contribution above state of the art
This study develops an automated pipeline for monitoring slated
pitched building roofs. It produces an occlusion-free orthophoto for
each roof panel, which is then semantically segmented into individ-
ual sub-components including ‘slate’, ‘lead’, and ‘other’. This method,
following a ‘Scan-vs-DT’ approach, facilitates the use of deep learning
models for detecting deteriorations and defects in each sub-component
area. Ultimately, this allows for the comprehensive documentation of
all deteriorations and defects, along with their progression, in the roof
DT (logbook). While tailored for pitched slated roofs, this methodology
could potentially be adapted to other building fabric elements, such as
walls.

successive, fully automated stages:

The process takes two inputs:
• A structure-from-motion photogrammetric reconstruction of a
roof that is referenced in the building’s local coordinate system;
and
• The Digital Twin 3D model of the building being monitored. The
model must identify each individual roof panel as an element,
with its own geometry and other semantic identification.
The output of stage A is an orthophoto for each roof panel, with
minimised blur and without projection artefacts. Stage B then segments
each one of these orthophotos semantically into its main material
sub-components: slates, leadwork, and other. These segmented areas
are then processed in Stage C using dedicated algorithms to output
deterioration and defects related to that type of material. The detected
deteriorations and defects are then systematically recorded (including
with their exact position) in the building’s Digital Twin. The scope of
this paper is Stage A and Stage B. Stage C is not covered.
Stage A’s method is detailed in Section 4 with associated experimen-
tal validation reported in Section 5. Stage B’s method is then detailed in
Section 6 with associated experimental validation reported in Section 7.
4. Roof panel orthophoto generation
The roof orthophoto generation method is composed of 3 main
successive steps: 1. Creating panel orthophoto templates, 2. Selecting
the most suitable images, 3. Instantiating orthophoto. Step 1 is con-
ducted once offline, while Steps 2 and 3 are conducted each time
an image acquisition campaign is conducted by UAV survey for that
roof, for example, once a year in a proactive maintenance regime. Step
2 selects from the complete set of UAV-taken images, those images
most suitable to generate an orthophoto for each roof panel. Step 3
then processes those selected images to ‘texture’ each panel orthophoto
template, thereby instantiating an orthophoto from the given data
acquisition campaign. These three steps are detailed in Sections 4.1 to
4.3, respectively.
4.1. Creating panel orthophoto templates
The first (offline) step for generating orthophotos is to build an
orthophoto template for each roof panel. As illustrated in Fig. 4, each
panel orthophoto template is defined by a canvas with associated mask:
• The canvas is the vertical face of the Horizontally-Oriented Bound-
ing Box (HOBB) that is parallel to the main horizontal direction
of the roof. The HOBB is the Bounding Box of the object that
is object-oriented in the horizontal plane and axis-oriented ver-
tically. The pixel resolution of the orthophoto image canvas is set
so that each pixel is square with dimensions 𝛿 × 𝛿, e.g. 𝛿 = 5 mm .
• the mask of the orthophoto template is defined by projecting all
the panel’s mesh triangles onto the canvas, which defines the
extent of the roof panel within the canvas. In the mask, the RGB
value of each pixel outside the projected triangles is set to [0, 0,
0] (black), and those inside the projected triangles is set to [1, 1,
1] (white).
This process is illustrated in Fig. 4 for one roof panel.

4.3.2. Final orthophoto
While individual orthophotos can sometimes be used as-is for anal-
ysis, they may not encompass the entire roof or may be marred by
occlusions. To remedy this, all individual images for a given panel are
aligned and merged to produce a single comprehensive orthophoto.
This process is achieved as follows:
1. Aligning orthophotos by Scale-Invariant Feature transform
(SIFT) feature matching. To eliminate the small shift between
orthophotos caused by the small errors from the photogram-
metric process and avoid its influence on the merging process,
SIFT features are detected and matched across images [ 67].
These matchings are then used to calculate homographies to
shift all individual orthophotos to one baseline one (selected as
the orthophoto from the most suitable UAV image as defined
in Section 4.2). Fig. 6 illustrates this alignment process and its
benefit on the final merged image.
2. Merging all orthophotos for one roof panel into one. To ex-
clude pixels from occluders, the aligned individual orthophotos
can be merged by calculating the median value of the RGB for
each orthophoto pixel. If, as we have observed, occlusions ap-
pear in fewer than 50% of the images, merging the orthophotos
helps eliminate occlusions.
The above process aims to generate a final orthophoto of good
clarity and occlusion-free.

6. Semantic segmentation
The input to this stage is an orthophoto of a slated roof panel
generated by the method presented in Section 4 . The generated or-
thophoto shows the roof panel that is composed of different essential
sub-components: slates and leadwork, as well as other less frequent
elements (e.g. stone, grid, equipment, glass, ladder, etc.).
Since the intention of this work is to find defects in slated areas
as well as leadwork areas, and these materials have distinct types
of defect types, it is best to first segment the orthophoto to isolate
these particular subparts. For this, we explore different methods for
(pixel-level) semantic segmentation.
6.1. Dataset
The data output of Duff House by Li et al. [ 14] includes data for
36 panels. We split these into 27 panels for training and 9 panels for
testing. For each panel, we retain maximum 10 unmerged orthopho-
tos obtained from different photos of the same panel (with different
angles and covering the panel in various ways). This dataset is used
for developing and comparing different initial semantic segmentation
models.
In addition, the St Mary’s Church data include data for 18 pan-
els. With this data, we create another dataset composed of one un-
merged orthophoto per panel. This dataset is used solely to test the
generalisability of the models created using the Duff House dataset.
Finally, a combined dataset using data from the two buildings is
created. It is divided into a training dataset that is ∼80% of the overall
dataset (27 panel orthophoto data of Duff House, along with 13 panel
orthophoto data of St Mary’s Church) and a testing dataset that is the
remaining ∼20% (9 from Duff House, along with 5 from St Mary’s
Church). Here, only one unmerged orthophoto is kept for each panel.
We select the orthophoto with the largest coverage of the panel.

6.2. Deep learning model
As discussed earlier in Section 2, state-of-art models that can be use-
ful to the specific problem in this study include: DeepLabV3, PointRend,
Segmenter, Segformer. Basic hyper-parameter settings are tuned for best
performance by comparing these deep learning models. The best set-
tings are reported in Table 1 . All models were pre-trained using their
default weights.
For each model, K-fold cross validation is applied during training:
the entire training dataset is first split into 𝑘 folds (𝑘 = 5 in this study).
The model is trained 𝑘 times on each 𝑘 − 1 folds, and then validated on
the remaining fold. The model performances are evaluated using the
average value of these 𝑘 time validation results, and the best model is
selected based on those. Then, the best model is retrained using the
entire training dataset, and the final result is obtained by testing this
model on the testing dataset.
During training, the input images are not rescaled. This is because
most of the roof orthophotos are rectangular with varying width-to-
length ratios, and the resizing processing operations that are typically
applied in semantic segmentation pipelines would result in information
loss. But, to meet the requirements of input image size and com-
puting capacity limits, all images are cropped (tiled) and read as
512 × 1024 × 3 matrices.
As will be shown in the Experimental Validation using the Duff
House dataset (Section 7.3), PointRend with default backbone (ResNet)
and loss function (Cross-Entropy Loss) achieves best performance all
four initial models. As a result, further experiments are conducted
by using different loss functions including Dice Loss (sensitive to re-
gion detection) and Focal Loss (sensitive to imbalance problem). With
grid search as a tool for hyperparameter adjustment, specific weights
between different losses are selected for best performance.
The models trained with the Duff House dataset were then tested
with the St Mary’s Church dataset to assess its generalisation ability.
The results lead us to finally use the combined orthophoto dataset (Duff
House + St Mary’s Church) for training and testing a final model with
best segmentation performance and generalisation ability.
6.3. Background label correction
In most deep learning semantic segmentation methods, precise de-
lineation of segmentation boundary is a challenging problem, with
many confusions arising at those boundaries. Fig. 14 illustrates this
issue with a typical output of the semantic segmentation models we
explored. The figure highlights the discrepancies between thePrediction
and Ground Truth (GT) for the different classes.
However, in this study, the set of background pixels is actually
known a priori, because the panel boundary is defined by the Digital
Twin 3D model projection (see [ 14] for details). In other words, the
Ground Truth for the background class is known a priori. Therefore,
an extra step is introduced to correct the False Positive (FP) and False
Negative (FN) results for the background class:
1. False Negative ( 𝑃 𝑟𝑒𝑑𝑖𝑐𝑡𝑖𝑜𝑛 ≠ 0 and 𝐺𝑇 = 0 ): In this case, the
predicted label is simply changed back to ‘0’ (i.e. background
class).

background pixels (i.e. class ‘0’), then the grid is expanded by one
pixel (i.e. 5 × 5 grid) and the process is repeated until a at least
a non-zero label is found. As will be shown, this simple process
works well in our context.
As will be shown in the Experimental Results (Section 7), while the
baseline semantic segmentation results are already good, this process
delivers some additional improvements.
6.4. Label merging strategy
As we have seen in Section 4, each individual orthophoto may
not necessarily cover entirely a given roof panel of interest. Besides,
the textured parts of multiple individual orthophotos will most likely
overlap. Ultimately, we do not wish to have many orthophotos (with
their individual semantic segementation) for each roof panel, but a
single one covering the overall panel with a single semantic segmen-
tation. This can be achieved using either of the following two merging
strategies:
• Label Merging Strategy 1 : Merge the unmerged orthophotos as
described in Section 4, and then apply the semantic segmentation
to the merged orthophoto.
• Label Merging Strategy 2 : Merge the semantic segmentation re-
sults of all unmerged orthophotos. For each pixel, the label that
appears most frequently (top 1 class) among the unmerged or-
thophotos is selected as the final label. In cases when two (or
more) classes have the same frequencies for all unmerged or-
thophotos, the final label will be selected in the following order
of priority: other, leadwork, slate. For example, if slate and other
appear the same time for one pixel, then other would be chosen
as the final label.
These two merging strategies are compared on the model trained with
the combined dataset.
7. Experimental results
In this section, evaluation metrics for semantic segmentation are
first introduced. Then all the experimental results are reported. Differ-
ent models are trained using the Duff House training dataset and tested
using the Duff House and St Mary’s Church testing datasets. The best
model is selected by comparing these results, and it is finally re-trained
and tested using the combined dataset. All the training and testing work
is completed in the Google Colab Pro environment, with NVIDIA A100
GPU 40 GB.
7.1. Evaluation metrics for semantic segmentation
The segmentation results for each class are evaluated using two
parameters:
• Intersection over Union (IoU) : computed to assess the overlap
between Prediction and Ground Truth segments.
• Accuracy (Acc) : calculated by dividing the sum of the True Pos-
itive pixels by the sum of the True Positive pixels and False
Positive pixels.
To compare the general testing performance between different models,
the following overall evaluation metrics are evaluated:
• aAcc: the Accuracy of all pixels, evaluating the classification
accuracy.
• mIoU: the mean IoU of all classes; mIoU is an important indicator
to measure the accuracy of overall semantic segmentation.
• mAcc: the mean Acc of all classes, evaluating the overall perfor-
mance in pixel classification.
In the following, we report results for different models in the form
of tables and confusion matrices. In the tables, we report for each
model: the overall aAcc, mIoU and mAcc, and then IoU and Acc for each
of the four classes. We also report relative (in percentages) confusion
matrices.
7.2. Background label correction
Regardless of the model employed, the Background model correction
step described in Section 6.3 can be applied to correct FP and FN for the
background class. We thus demonstrate the benefits of this correction
using one model (which we will see later performs well): PointRend.
Table 2 and Fig. 16 show the results obtained when training and
testing this model on Duff House dataset, before and after applying the
background label correction results.
While the baseline performance is already quite good (all metrics
>85 and most of them are >95), the additional corrective step im-
proves performance for all classes, in particular the leadwork and other
classes, which had the lowest performance without this correction. The
higher increases in mIoU ( +1.58 to 94.22) and mAcc ( +1.15 to 97.25)
also indicate a reduced difference in performance among the different
classes. Although anticipated, this improvement is welcome, because
the background regions are often next to leadwork regions. And so, any
correction of background class would most likely benefit the leadwork

proposed background label correction method.
