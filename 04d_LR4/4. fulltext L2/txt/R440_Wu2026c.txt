ABSTRACT: Translucent polymer-concrete claddings are emerging as multifunctional
building skins that merge structural performance, daylighting, and human−architecture
interaction. However, the lack of integrated design frameworks coupling durability, real-time
aesthetics, and long-term service-life prognosis limits their adoption. Here, we present a glass-
fiber-reinforced polymer (GFRP) mesh-enhanced translucent polymer-concrete (GFRP-
TRPC) panel that simultaneously serves as load-bearing façade, AI-driven light canvas, and
self-sensing structural element. Epoxy-vinyl ester polymer concrete with 30 vol % recycled glass
aggregate and embedded PMMA optical fibers (ϕ 1 mm) was fabricated by a one-step vacuum-
assisted resin infusion process, yielding 30-mm-thin elements with 65 MPa compressive
strength and 22% optical transmittance. Bidirectional E-glass GFRP grids (44% fiber volume)
restrained microcracking and enabled distributed fiber Bragg-grating (FBG) sensing at 5 cm
pitch. A vision transformer−generative adversarial network (ViT-GAN) converted real-time
skeleton streams from Azure Kinect into 30 fps light patterns on a 12 m curved light-emitting
diode (LED) back wall, producing an interactive “living fresco” that increased visitor dwell time by 340%. Edge-deployed TinyML
CNNs processed FBG signals to detect 0.1 mm cracks with 96.7% accuracy and autonomously dimmed the artwork as a safety cue.
Synchrotron X-ray CT revealed interfacial void fractions below 1.2%, validated by digital image correlation strain fields. A calibrated
reduced-order digital twin predicted only 7.8% stiffness loss and <5% optical attenuation after 30 years of Shanghai climate loads,
with maintenance alerts triggered six months in advance. This study demonstrates a scalable pathway for polymer-composite
building skins to merge artistic expression, structural integrity, and intelligent longevity management.
1. INTRODUCTION
The building envelope is rapidly evolving from a passive
separator to an active, multifunctional skin that simultaneously
bears structural loads, modulates daylight, harvests informa-
tion, and communicates with occupants.
1−3
This trans-
formation is driven by increasing demands for energy
efficiency, occupant well-being, and adaptive building perform-
ance in response to climate change and urbanization pressures.
Among emerging technologies, translucent concrete (TRC)
has attracted significant attention for its ability to transmit
natural light while maintaining the compressive strength of
conventional concrete.
4,5
Traditional TRC systems incorporate
optical fibers or light-transmitting aggregates within Portland
cement matrices to create architectural elements that blur the
boundary between structure and fenestration.
6
However, state-of-the-art TRC systems face several
fundamental limitations that restrict their widespread adoption.
First, the reliance on brittle cementitious matrices and steel
reinforcement leads to high self-weight (typically 2.4−2.8 g/
cm
3
), limiting their application in lightweight façade systems.
7
Second, the alkaline environment of cement causes degrada-
tion of embedded optical fibers over time, reducing light
transmission efficiency.
8
Third, conventional TRC lacks
integration with digital building systems, missing opportunities
for real-time interaction and adaptive performance.
9
Finally,
the absence of validated long-term durability models makes it
difficult for architects and engineers to predict service life and
plan maintenance schedules.
10
Polymer-matrix composites offer a promising alternative
route to address these limitations. Epoxy- or vinyl-ester-based
polymer concretes reinforced with glass-fiber-reinforced
polymer (GFRP) grids can achieve high specific stiffness,
excellent corrosion resistance, and superior design flexibility
compared to cementitious systems.
11,12
The neutral pH
environment of polymer matrices is compatible with optical
fibers, enabling stable light transmission over extended
periods.
13
Moreover, the electrical properties of polymer
composites can be tailored to support embedded sensors and
actuators, creating opportunities for intelligent building skins.
14
Recent advances in fiber-optic sensing, particularly fiber
Bragg gratings (FBGs), have enabled distributed structural
health monitoring (SHM) in composite structures.
15,16
When
integrated with machine learning algorithms, FBG sensor

Despite these technological advances, three critical gaps
remain in the development of next-generation translucent

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1. Material System Design
2.1.1. Matrix Formulation. The polymer-concrete matrix was
formulated using a bisphenol A epoxy-vinyl ester resin system
(Derakane 411-350, Ashland Inc., Columbus, OH) selected for its
superior mechanical properties, chemical resistance, and optical
clarity. The resin exhibits a glass transition temperature of 108 °C
and maintains dimensional stability under thermal cycling conditions
typical of building façade applications. A stoichiometric ratio of
100:35 (by weight) resin/methyl ethyl ketone peroxide (MEKP)
initiator was employed to ensure complete polymerization while
maintaining adequate pot life for large-scale fabrication.
The aggregate system consisted of 100% recycled soda-lime glass
particles (Vitro Minerals, Mexico City, Mexico) with a graded size
distribution ranging from 0.5 mm to 4.0 mm. The glass aggregate was
selected for its optical compatibility with the polymer matrix,
achieving a refractive index match (n = 1.52 ± 0.01) that minimizes
light scattering at the interfaces. The cured vinyl ester polymer matrix
has a refractive index of n = 1.53 ± 0.01 at a 550 nm wavelength,
ensuring minimal Fresnel reflection losses at matrix-aggregate
interfaces. Prior to use, the aggregate was cleaned in 10% hydrochloric
acid solution to remove surface contaminants and dried at 110 °C for
24 h to eliminate moisture that could interfere with polymerization.
2.1.2. Optical Fiber Integration. Optical-grade poly(methyl
methacrylate) (PMMA) fibers (Mitsubishi Rayon Eska GH-4001,
Tokyo, Japan) with a core diameter of 1.0 mm, core refractive index
of n = 1.49 ± 0.005, and cladding index of n = 1.40 ± 0.005 were
selected as light-transmitting elements. These fibers exhibit low
attenuation (0.2 dB/m at 550 nm wavelength) and excellent
compatibility with the polymer matrix. The fibers were arranged in
a regular 20 mm × 20 mm grid pattern to achieve uniform light
distribution while maintaining an adequate structural cross-section for
load transfer.
2.1.3. GFRP Reinforcement System. Bidirectional E-glass
GFRP grids (Saint-Gobain Vetrotex, Chambe ́ry, France) with 600
tex rovings and 10 mm × 10 mm spacing provided primary structural
reinforcement. The grids were positioned at the neutral axis of the
panel cross-section to maximize the bending resistance. A fiber
volume fraction of 44% was achieved through careful layup
sequencing and consolidation pressure control during fabrication.

adequate workability while maximizing mechanical properties.

2.3. Vacuum-Assisted Resin Infusion (VARI) Process
2.3.1. Mold Preparation and Fiber Positioning. A precision
aluminum mold (3000 mm × 1000 mm × 30 mm) was fabricated
with integrated heating elements and temperature control to maintain
uniform processing conditions. The mold surface was treated with a
semipermanent release agent (Chemlease 15 Sealer, Chem-Trend,
Howell, MI) to ensure consistent part release and surface quality.
GFRP reinforcement grids were pretensioned to 300 N/m using a
custom tensioning frame to minimize fiber waviness and ensure
uniform load distribution. PMMA optical fibers were positioned using
robotically controlled tensioning at 30 N per fiber to prevent buckling,
while maintaining straight light paths. Three-dimensional printed
poly(lactic acid) (PLA) combs fabricated on an Ultimaker S5 system
provided precise fiber spacing and alignment during the infusion
process. Fiber alignment tolerance was maintained at ± 0.5° for
GFRP grids and ± 1.0° for optical fibers using the robotic positioning
system with real-time optical verification to ensure optimal light
transmission and mechanical performance.
2.3.2. Resin Infusion and Curing. The VARI process was
conducted at 60 °C under a 95 kPa vacuum to ensure complete wet-
out and minimize void content. Resin flow was monitored using
transparent flow mesh, and flow front progression was recorded at 30
s intervals. The infusion time was optimized at 4 h to achieve
complete saturation while preventing fiber displacement.
The resin viscosity profile followed a time-dependent curve from
initial 1.2 Pa·s at 25 °C, decreasing to 0.8 Pa·s during the 60 °C
infusion phase, and increasing to 2.5 Pa·s at the gel point. This
controlled viscosity profile was essential for achieving complete fiber
wet-out while minimizing void formation.
Following infusion, the panel was subjected to a two-stage curing
cycle: initial cure at 60 °C for 4 h under vacuum, followed by postcure
at 80 °C for 2 h at atmospheric pressure. Temperature was monitored
using embedded thermocouples and controlled within ±2 °C
throughout the cycle, with the exotherm peaks limited to +5 °C
through active cooling when necessary to prevent thermal degradation
and residual stress development.

2.6.2. Edge AI Processing Architecture. Real-time signal
processing was implemented on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin edge
computing platform (60 TOPS AI performance). A TinyML
convolutional neural network based on the MobileNet-V3 architec-
ture was developed for crack detection and classification. The network
consisted of 260,000 parameters quantized to INT8 precision using
TensorRT optimization to achieve 9 ms inference latency.
The TinyML CNN was selected after benchmarking against
traditional signal processing methods, showing 96.7% accuracy versus
82.3% for wavelet-based approaches while maintaining edge
deployability with minimal computational overhead. This architecture
enables real-time processing of sensor data directly at the edge,
eliminating the need for cloud connectivity and reducing latency for
safety-critical applications.
The crack detection algorithm processes FBG strain data using a
sliding window approach with a 100-sample history. Features
extracted include statistical moments, frequency-domain character-
istics, and wavelet coefficients. The classification output provides
crack probability with 96.7% accuracy and 94.5% precision based on
validation against artificially induced defects.
To address false positives and system conflicts, we implemented a
multilevel decision framework: (1) Temporal filtering requiring
consistent anomaly detection across 3 consecutive sampling windows
(300 ms) before triggering alerts; (2) Spatial correlation verification
requiring at least 2 adjacent FBG sensors to detect anomalies; and (3)
A priority override protocol that immediately transitions from
aesthetic display to safety alert mode when crack probability exceeds
0.85 but implements a “fade transition” for probabilities between 0.7
and 0.85 to avoid jarring visual changes. This approach reduced false
positive rates from 3.3% to 0.8% in our validation testing.

2.7. AI-Driven Light Narration System
2.7.1. Computer Vision and Human Pose Estimation. An
Azure Kinect DK sensor captured RGB-D video streams at 30 fps with
1920 × 1080 resolution. Real-time human pose estimation was
performed using the MediaPipe Pose framework to extract 17-
keypoint coordinates representing major body joints. The pose data
were filtered using a Kalman filter to reduce noise and ensure smooth
motion tracking.
2.7.2. Vision Transformer−GAN Architecture. A custom
Vision Transformer−Generative Adversarial Network (ViT-GAN)
was developed to convert human pose sequences to dynamic light
patterns. The generator network maps 17-keypoint skeletons to 4096-
channel grayscale patterns controlling individual PMMA fiber
illumination. The discriminator network ensures realistic and
aesthetically pleasing light transitions.
The ViT-GAN architecture was selected after evaluating several
alternatives, including CNN-based GANs and LSTM-RNNs.
Comparative testing showed that ViT-GAN achieved 27% higher
perceptual quality scores and 35% lower latency compared to CNN-
GAN alternatives, particularly in maintaining temporal coherence
between sequential human poses while generating aesthetically
pleasing light patterns.
