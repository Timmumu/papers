ABSTRACT
Vibration accidents of high-rise buildings are frequently reported around the globe and the downtime of high-rise 
buildings for accident investigation can lead to substantial social and economic losses for stakeholders. This 
research developed an edge-cloud collaborative framework for vibration anomaly detection and vibration source 
localization. Firstly, a high-precision vibration sensor with wireless time synchronization and edge computing 
capability is introduced. Secondly, a vibration anomaly detection algorithm is proposed based on edge 
computing of sensor nodes. Thirdly, a vibration source localization algorithm is proposed based on cloud 
computing of a structural dynamics digital twin model. Finally, the effectiveness of the proposed methods is 
validated through a case study of a 17-story high-rise building, and the results indicate that vibration anomaly 
events can be reliably detected and localized. The outcomes of this study are expected to provide a useful 
reference for vibration anomaly management of high-rise buildings.
1. Introduction
According to the Council on Tall Buildings and Urban Habitat, there 
are more than 6400 high-rise buildings over 150 m built worldwide by 
March 1, 2023 [1] . High-rise buildings serve crucial urban functions, 
including hospitals and office spaces. Any sudden occurrence of vibra -
tion anomalies in these structures can impair their functionality, 
resulting in substantial direct and indirect economic losses [2] .
Several high-rise buildings have experienced vibration anomalies 
that differ from those caused by typhoons or earthquakes. These vibra -
tions are more sudden and usually cause widespread panic. It is usually 
impossible to find the cause of the vibration in the first place, and 
subsequent investigations result in prolonged closures of the buildings, 
leading to significant economic losses. For example, on May 18, 2021, 
the Saige Building ( Fig. 1 a) in Shenzhen encountered a vibration 
anomaly caused by wind-induced vortex resonance of the top-floor mast 
[3] . After nearly four months of closure, the building resumed operation, 
but the owner suffered a net profit loss of approximately 55 million RMB 
that year [4] . Similarly, on February 7, 2013, occupants on the 20th 
floor of the Information Building in Shanghai experienced intermittent 
abnormal shaking, prompting an emergency evacuation. Nonetheless, 
the cause of the building's shaking remains unknown [5] . Additionally, 
on September 5, 2013, a 50-story office building in Nanjing underwent 
several intense up-and-down shaking events within an hour due to 
construction work involving a road roller at a nearby construction site 
[6] . Moreover, on July 5, 2011, TechnoMart ( Fig. 1 b), a 39-story 
shopping mall building in Seoul, experienced abnormal vertical 
shaking lasting 10 min due to crowd movement inside the building, 
leading to a two-day evacuation of residents [7,8] . Lastly, on January 
20, 2022, a 33-story high-rise residential building in Seoul encountered 
structural resonance caused by group dancing within the building after 
two months of investigation and lockdown [9] .
High-rise buildings typically exhibit relatively low lateral stiffness 
and damping ratios, making them susceptible to external excitations 
such as wind loads, crowd activities, and mechanical vibrations. Struc -
tural vibrations induced by earthquakes or typhoons exhibit distinct 
characteristics that can be quickly identified to prevent unnecessary 
panic. Vibrations caused by equipment operation or human activities 
within the 1 – 10 Hz frequency range not only coincide with many high- 
order vibration modes of tall buildings but also fall within the sensitive

the cause and location of such vibration sources often leads to public 
panic events. The aforementioned vibration anomaly events of high-rise 
buildings in Shenzhen, Seoul, and Nanjing demonstrate that vibration 
sources can be located at the top, middle, and base of the buildings. Yet, 
in each case, the inability to immediately identify the vibration source 
led to building evacuations and months-long investigations before 
resuming normal operations, resulting in substantial economic losses. If 
a vibration anomaly can be detected and localized while still weak 
(below the human perception threshold), timely elimination of the 
source can prevent such events from escalating into public incidents.
In the field of structural health monitoring (SHM) for high-rise 
buildings, extensive research has been conducted on operational 
modal analysis (OMA) [11 – 14] and damage detection and localization 
[15 – 17] . However, limited research has been reported on the detection 
and localization of vibration anomalies specifically for high-rise build -
ings. Existing research on vibration source localization primarily focuses 
on seismic localization, occupant localization, and pipeline leakage 
localization. For instance, travel-time inversion was proposed to deter -
mine earthquake hypocenter coordinates [18] . The double-difference 
earthquake location method was proposed to minimize the residuals 
between observed and theoretical travel-time differences of seismic 
pairs at each station [19] . Schloemann et al. [20] introduced a time 
difference of arrival (TDoA)-based method to localize impact sources 
within buildings. Mirshekari et al. [21] proposed an occupant localiza -
tion approach using TDoA and footstep-induced floor vibrations. Lah 
et al. [22] introduced a pipeline leakage localization method by esti -
mating TDoA through vibration signal correlation. Wang and Zhu [23]
developed a two-stage impact source localization method based on the 
time of arrival (ToA) of impact waves recorded from multiple vibration 
monitoring points. The localization accuracy was validated through 
numerical simulations and field experiments conducted at a construc -
tion site with ongoing impact piling work.
These algorithms were designed for transient pulse excitations, 
whereas high-rise building vibration anomalies are typically non-pulse- 
like and persistent, limiting the applicability of TDoA/ToA methods. 
Consequently, vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise 
buildings remain underexplored.
In most SHM applications, the SHM system is designed based on the 
cloud computing pattern, where all sensor data are transmitted to a 
cloud server for centralized processing [24,25] . While this pattern is 
feasible in wired SHM systems, it has been found to be challenging and 
inappropriate for wireless sensor networks (WSNs) when it comes to 
transmitting large time-history data [26] . On one hand, implementing a 
WSN based on the cloud computing pattern would result in high energy 
consumption and reduced battery life of sensor nodes due to the 
continuous wireless connection to the cloud server and the significant 
data transmission requirements for time-history vibration data. On the 
other hand, the cloud computing pattern is not suitable for real-time 
detection of vibration anomalies due to the inherent delay in data 
transmission and cloud analysis.
Edge computing is a computing paradigm that performs computation 
at the network edge, which has gained significant research interest in 
the field of SHM. For instance, Wei et al. [27] proposed a multi-resource 
computing offload algorithm for optimizing energy consumption in 
mobile edge computing, resulting in more than 12 % of energy saving. 
Zonzini et al. [28] introduced an innovative extreme edge data 
compression strategy tailored for low-power and low-cost SHM appli -
cations. Experimental results from wind turbine monitoring demon -
strated that this strategy reduced the energy consumption of a sensor 
node by over 16 %. Additionally, Abner et al. [29] presented three 
strategies for conserving battery power in wireless monitoring sensors 
for infrastructures using edge computing, significantly enhancing the 
sensor's battery lifespan and reducing the frequency of on-site hardware 
maintenance. Based on the above research, edge computing shows 
promise in addressing challenges related to response time, battery life, 
and bandwidth costs [30] . However, due to limited computing and 
memory resources, as well as network bandwidth constraints, edge 
computing faces challenges in achieving satisfactory performance when 
dealing with complex problems.
Owing to the complementary advantages and disadvantages of cloud 
computing and edge computing, an increasingly common approach is to 
integrate them in real-world applications [31] . This approach is known 
as edge-cloud collaboration (ECC), which combines the low latency 
characteristics of edge computing with the powerful computing capa -
bilities of cloud computing [32] . Early explorations of ECC have been 
conducted in both academia and industry. For example, Yu et al. [33]
designed an ECC framework for industrial anomaly detection, where 
cloud algorithms are responsible for extracting and optimizing anomaly 
diagnosis rules, while edge algorithms detect anomalies based on these 
rules. This method significantly reduces network load and computa -
tional pressure on the cloud. Gang et al. [34] proposed an ECC frame -
work for electric load monitoring, which performs processing at the edge 
and optimization in the cloud. Ding et al. [35] introduced an ECC 
framework for healthcare monitoring that enables lesion detection at the 
edge and high-performance modeling in the cloud. As observed, current 
ECC frameworks primarily operate in the mode of edge detection with 
cloud-based dataset training or modeling. However, no reports have 
been made on edge-cloud collaborative intelligence for anomaly 
detection and localization in high-rise buildings.
The aforementioned edge and clouding computing can help to ac -
quire, process and analysis data from physical world. When combined 
with more complicated simulation and visualization models as the dig -
ital replica of the real world entities, the concept of digital twin can be 
involved. A digital twin represents a cyber-physical system that enables 
visualization, modeling, simulation, analysis, prediction, and optimi -
zation [36] . It has found applications in various civil engineering fields, 
including buildings [37 – 40] , infrastructure [41,42] , and construction 
sites [43,44] . The digital twin is considered a promising technology with 
the potential to transform the construction industry and provide solu -
tions to various challenges [45] . Furthermore, Jiang et al. [46] argued 
that technologies such as digital twin, building information modeling 
(BIM), and cyber-physical systems (CPS) are not mutually exclusive; 
instead, they can mutually reinforce civil engineering digitalization. 
Therefore, the digital twin represents a broader concept that can be 
integrated with edge-cloud collaborative computing, BIM models, and 
simulation models to establish a comprehensive digital system for 
enhanced management and disaster prevention of civil structures.
Therefore, an edge-cloud collaborative digital twin framework is 
presented for the purpose of vibration anomaly detection and localiza -
tion in high-rise buildings. The composition of the framework is detailed 
in Section 2 , followed by the introduction of the hardware composition 
and vibration anomaly detection algorithm in Section 3 . In Section 4 , a 
vibration source localization method is proposed. Additionally, Section

2. Overview of the methodology
The proposed edge-cloud collaborative (ECC) digital twin framework 
for vibration anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings 
comprises three main components: edge sensing for vibration anomaly 
detection, cloud computing for vibration source localization, and a BIM- 
empowered monitoring management platform, as illustrated in Fig. 2 .
The first module is edge sensing for vibration anomaly detection, 
which involves: (1) a novel wireless time-synchronized smart sensor 
node, and (2) a vibration anomaly detection algorithm specifically 
designed for high-rise buildings.
The second module utilizes cloud computing for vibration source 
localization and BIM-based visualization, and consists of three compo -
nents: (1) a digital twin model for structural dynamic simulation, con -
structed using modal parameters derived from operational modal 
analysis; (2) a vibration anomaly detection algorithm that performs vi -
bration source localization by utilizing both the measured and simulated 
spectrum amplitude features; and (3) a BIM-empowered online moni -
toring and management platform capable of displaying real-time vi -
bration data during normal operation states and visualizing abnormal 
vibration sources through the BIM model during abnormal states.
3. Edge sensing for vibration anomaly detection
The detection of vibration anomaly in high-rise buildings requires 
high-precision time-synchronized monitoring of building vibrations. In 
this section, details of the smart sensor node and vibration anomaly 
detection algorithm are introduced.
3.1. Wireless time-synchronized smart sensor
Extensive research has been conducted on wireless time- 
synchronized smart sensors for structural health monitoring, with 
notable contributions including the state-of-the-art MEMS-based Xnode 
sensor developed by Professor Spencer's team [47] . This sensor in -
corporates three dedicated circuit boards handling data acquisition, 
wireless transmission, and power management respectively. The struc -
tural health monitoring system consists of multiple sensing nodes and a 
gateway node, with wireless time synchronization implemented using 
the IEEE 802.15.4 protocol [48] . This system has been successfully 
deployed in bridge monitoring applications.
Building upon the Xnode design concept, we propose a new wireless 
time-synchronized smart sensor (WTSS) featuring three key improve -
ments: First, our design achieves greater functional integration by

3.2. Vibration anomaly detection algorithm
This section describes the proposed vibration anomaly detection al -
gorithm implemented at the sensor node, as depicted in Fig. 4 a. The 
algorithm comprises four primary steps:
(1) The WTSS nodes are adopted to acquire the vibration on different 
stories of a high-rise building as the historical data for a certain period of 
time. Note that the historical vibration data is acquired during normal 
operation of the building, the acquired historical vibration data are 
deemed as the baseline to evaluate the normality of current vibration.
(2) Short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is performed on the histor -
ical vibration data using the window size of a few minutes. The 
maximum amplitude values at different frequency points are extracted 
as the historical spectrum envelope, represented by the dashed line in 
Fig. 4 b.
(3) Applying STFT to the current vibration data using the same 
window size to obtain the current spectrum, illustrated by the red line in 
Fig. 4 b.
(4) Comparing the current spectrum with the historical spectrum 
envelope. If the current spectrum exceeds α times the historical spec -
trum envelope at any frequency point, it is determined that a vibration 
anomaly event has occurred, and the corresponding frequency is 
recorded as the vibration anomaly frequency ω .
To ensure a timely response for vibration anomaly detection, the 
algorithm can be implemented using Python and executed on the 
Raspberry Pi module of the WTSS. When a vibration anomaly event is 
detected, the WTSS nodes promptly upload the spectrum feature data, 
which includes the vibration anomaly frequency ω and its amplitude, 
along with the high-sampling-rate vibration time-history data to the 
cloud server.

4.2. Vibration source localization algorithm
The vibration anomaly investigated in this study primarily focuses on 
building vibrations caused by harmonic excitation. A series of harmonic 
excitation tests conducted in this study reveal that the amplitude of 
structural frequency response at different floors varies significantly 
when horizontal harmonic excitation is applied at a certain floor. Fig. 6
illustrates the acceleration spectra of the 5th, 8th, 11th, 14th, and 17th 
floors of a 17-story high-rise building when horizontal 8 Hz harmonic 
excitation was applied to the 5th, 8th, and 11th floors, respectively. The 
results indicate significant variations in the amplitudes of the accelera -
tion spectra at 8 Hz for the five floors.
As evident in Fig. 6 , the floor closest to the excitation source does not 
necessarily exhibit the largest amplitude. This is because in high-rise 
buildings, the dynamic response typically results from the super -
position of multiple vibration modes. Excitation at different floors 
activates distinct modal combinations ( Fig. 7 ). When the excitation 
source is proximate to the stationary point of a vibration mode, the floor 
near the stationary point exhibits relatively small amplitude.
The DTM discussed in Section 4.1 is utilized for conducting time- 
history analysis. An 8 Hz harmonic excitation is applied to the DTM, 
and the acceleration spectra of all stories are obtained. The amplitudes 
at 8 Hz in the acceleration spectra from both the test and the simulation 
are extracted as spectrum amplitude feature (SAF) vectors. Fig. 8 illus -
trates the normalized SAF vectors for different excitation conditions. 
The comparison exhibits correlative trends between the SAF vectors 
obtained from the test and the simulation under identical harmonic 
excitation conditions. Additionally, the SAF vectors exhibit distinct 
patterns for excitations on different floors, allowing for the prediction of 
the excitation location based on the shape of the obtained SAF vector.
To further demonstrate this phenomenon, we have added a simula -
tion case study where 8 Hz harmonic excitations were sequentially 
applied on the 2nd to 17th floors. As shown in Fig. 9 , the SAFs of the 
building exhibits completely different patterns when excited at different 
floors. By comparing the measured SAF with the simulated SAF values, 
the approximate location of the vibration source can be determined. 
Moreover, the results in Fig. 9 a (pink curve) further demonstrate the test 
observations in Fig. 6 . When the excitation was applied on the 5th floor, 
the large vibrational amplitudes occurred on the 8th and 14th floors, 
while the 5th and 11th floors showed relatively small responses. 
Consequently, an algorithm for vibration source localization is proposed 
based these observations.

5. BIM-empowered online monitoring and management 
platform
This study has developed a web-based platform for data visualization 
and vibration anomaly management in high-rise buildings. The platform 
specifically integrates the BIM model of the building to visualize the 
sensor locations and the floors where vibration sources are detected. 
Fig. 11 illustrates the main components of the platform, which includes 
the data flow module, the back-end data processing module, and the 
front-end visualization module.
(1) Data flow module:
The data flow module of the platform operates as follows. The fre -
quency spectra and vibration data from all WTSS nodes are transmitted 
to the cloud server via the MQTT protocol. The server stores the newly 
uploaded data in the MySQL database as hot data. After a certain time 
period, the server transfers the hot data from the MySQL database to 
object storage as cold data to improve operational efficiency. The cold 
data includes raw data, which preserves the original vibration data with 
a high sampling rate, and downsampled snapshot data for fast preview.
(2) Back-end data processing module:
The back-end data processing module of the platform connects with 
the MySQL database and object storage using the Flask-SQLAlchemy 
library [62] . The module consists of a series of algorithms, including 
operational modal analysis and DTM updating using the obtained modal

7. Discussion and limitations
This study presents an integrated wireless smart sensor system with 
edge-cloud computing algorithms for vibration monitoring in high-rise 
buildings, featuring three key innovations. First, we developed an 
edge-cloud collaborative framework that distributes vibration detection 
algorithms to edge nodes, which reduces cloud computing workload and 
data transmission requirements while enabling near real-time vibration 
source localization and visualization. Second, we designed a compact, 
highly integrated wireless sensor that combines high-precision vibration 
measurement (0.2 μ g/(Hz)
1/2 
noise density) with onboard edge 
computing capabilities (Linux-based Python environment), achieves less 
than 20 μ s time synchronization through a novel LoRa-based chain 
network, and enables direct 4 G cloud connectivity without requiring 
gateway nodes. Third, we established a digital twin-based vibration 
source localization methodology employing an updated flexural-shear 
MDOF model for building simulation, performing dynamic analysis 
under floor-specific excitations, and implementing SAF vector matching 
between simulated and measured responses to identify vibration 
sources.
This study demonstrates the feasibility of an edge-cloud collabora -
tive framework and proposes a novel vibration source localization 
method. However, three key limitations warrant discussion to guide 
future research directions:
(1) The current SAF vector comparison method achieves only zonal- 
level localization because SAF vectors exhibit similar patterns when 
excitations are applied to adjacent floors. This limitation underscores 
the need for more precise floor-specific identification techniques.
(2) The framework currently supports only single-frequency steady- 
state excitations. Extending it to multi-source and non-stationary vi -
bration scenarios will require further theoretical and experimental 
development.

8. Conclusions
An edge-cloud collaborative digital twin framework for vibration 
anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings is proposed in 
this study. The framework includes a wireless time-synchronized smart 
sensor (WTSS), a series of vibration anomaly detection and localization 
methods, and a BIM-empowered online platform. The inferred conclu -
sions are as follows:
(1) A digital twin framework for vibration monitoring of high-rise 
buildings is proposed in this study. The framework features vibration 
monitoring and anomaly detection at the edge sensors. Meanwhile, the 
digital twin model updating, vibration source localization, and platform 
management are performed at the cloud server. The assessment results 
can be visualized through a BIM-based management platform.
(2) A wireless time-synchronized smart sensor (WTSS) is proposed in 
this study. The sensor integrates a high-sensitive accelerometer, an edge 
computing module, a wireless data transmission module and a wireless 
time synchronization module. The WTSS sensor is very compact in size 
with the dimension of 10 cm*8 cm*4.5 cm. The sensitive and vibration 
measurement performance of the WTSS sensor is validated by 
comparing with the PCB393B31 piezoelectric sensor.
(3) An edge-cloud collaborative assessment method for vibration 
anomaly detection and localization in high-rise buildings is proposed. 
The method was validated through a series of harmonic excitation tests 
for a 17-story high-rise teaching building. A very weak 8 Hz harmonic 
excitation with the excitation force amplitude of 82.9 N was applied 
using a shaking table deployed on 5th, 8th, 11th, 14th, and 17th floors 
respectively. Although the excitation is very weak, the proposed WTSS 
sensors can clearly identify the features of the excitation and the vi -
bration anomaly event can be detected through the edge computing of

WTSS sensors using the proposed anomaly detection algorithm. More -
over, the location of the vibration source can be estimated through the 
cloud computing of the proposed vibration localization algorithm using 
the structural dynamics digital twin model.
In summary, the outcomes of this study are expected to provide a 
useful tool for the rapid detection and localization of vibration anomaly 
in high-rise buildings and assist in safety operation and maintenance of 
building structures.
