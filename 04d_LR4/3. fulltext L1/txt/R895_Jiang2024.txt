ABSTRACT 
Unsafe hoisting operations have been consistently associated with numerous safety incidents involving tower 
cranes. Currently, the predominant measures to mitigate these operations center around comprehensive training 
and education, emphasizing standardized protocols prior to hoisting activities. Despite concerted efforts in this 
direction, a conspicuous research gap persists in early-warning mechanisms during the construction phase. This 
paper aims to address this gap by proposing an innovative early-warning methodology, inspired by the principles 
of digital twin and knowledge graph. We firstly introduce a digital twin framework designed to mirror the real- 
time operational status of the tower crane. This framework enables the immediate detection of deviations or 
infractions as they occur. Subsequently, we develop a knowledge graph capable of promptly identifying unsafe 
hoisting operations by leveraging real-time data obtained from the digital twin. To validate the efficacy of our 
proposed methodology, we construct a scaled-down replica of a tower crane and establish a tailored digital twin 
system. The findings of a series of experimental trials prominently underscore the system ’ s capability to generate 
timely alerts in response to unsafe hoisting operations while maintaining an impressively low rate of false alarms. 
1. Introduction 
The construction industry has long acknowledged the considerable 
risks inherent in tower crane operations. Numerous academic studies 
and industrial reports have demonstrated the potential compromise of 
crane stability due to unsafe hoisting operations including untested 
lifting, aggressive operation, and excessive swinging ( Wiethorn, 2018 ). 
While tower crane operators generally receive trainings regarding 
operational specifications, violations are inevitable during the actual 
construction process. Therefore, proactive intervention is necessary to 
guarantee operational safety. 
Previous research has introduced proactive hoisting monitoring 
methods that can leverage Internet of Things (IoT) techniques to address 
issues like excessive loads, blind spots, and collisions ( Zhu et al., 2023 ). 
Some studies have amalgamated data from various sensors to prevent 
intrusion events, particularly unauthorized entry into the hoisting 
operation area ( Gu et al., 2012 ). Nevertheless, these studies often 
overlook the operational roles of tower crane operators, and do not 
extensively monitor various violations that may occur during the entire 
hoisting process. 
Recent studies have increasingly adopted digital twin (DT) to capi -
talize on the immersive experience for hoisting safety. The DT can 
simulate realistic hoisting operations before actual construction, facili -
tating technical disclosure ( Sepasgozar, 2020 ), remote training ( Li et al., 
2012 ), and other activities. Additionally, it provides comprehensive 
information to improve the navigation of hoisting routes ( Ji and Leite, 
2018 ). Studies have also incorporated finite element mechanical models 
into the DT of the tower crane to assess stability during hoisting oper -
ations ( Jiang et al., 2022 ). An essential rationale for these applications is 
that the DT represents the correspondence between virtual and physical 
spaces in construction activities ( Su et al., 2023 ) ( Opoku et al., 2021 ) 
( Kang and Mo, 2024 ). The utilization of DT can be categorized into six 
levels, including simulation, mapping, control, prediction, optimization, 
and symbiosis ( Tao et al., 2022 ). Existing applications of DT in tower 
crane operations primarily concentrate on simulation and mapping 
scenarios, resulting in underutilization of DT for controlling event 
occurrences. 
To leverage the DT for event control, it is crucial to employ numer -
ical models, rule-based reasoning, and artificial intelligence techniques 
that incorporate domain knowledge for event identification, fault

To address these concerns, this study aims to develop an early-

review is conducted to analyze common unsafe hoisting operations. 
Subsequently, a DT framework for hoisting operations is introduced,

associated DT system, enabling multifaceted experiments on unsafe

2. Related works 
2.1. Unsafe tower crane operations 
Worker operations play a pivotal role in determining the safety risks 
associated with tower crane operations. In-depth safety analysis by Im 
and Park (2020) focused on crane safety regulations in South Korea, 
meticulously examining 288 safety provisions. Their findings high -
lighted the need for enhanced monitoring of worker operations. Chen 
et al. (2022) suggested that the optimal management strategy for tower 
crane operators involves stringent supervision, comprehensive mainte -
nance practices, and strict adherence to safety regulations. Moreover, 
the Crane Safety Index identified the operator proficiency as a crucial 
factor shaping the safety landscape of crane operations (Mohandes et al., 
2022). Lingard et al. (2021) formulated a model to comprehend the root 
causes of crane safety events, qualitatively analyzing lifting-related ac -
cidents and identifying a total of 77 contributing factors. Among these 
factors, several are intertwined with worker behaviors, such as de -
viations from instructions, lack of familiarity with machinery, and 
non-compliance with procedures. Similarly, case studies conducted in 
Hong Kong (Tam and Fung, 2011) and South Korea (Shin, 2015) 
underscored the prominent role of worker operations in on-site safety 
management. 
On a global scale, various countries and regions have established 
standards that incorporate safety protocols for hoisting operations. 
Categorizing unsafe hoisting operations within these regulatory frame -
works can be achieved through three principles. Firstly, by restricting 
the extraction of operational specifications to instances involving the 
practical use of tower cranes, excluding organizational management, 
installation, removal, and maintenance processes. Secondly, by focusing 
on operation specifications relevant only to tower crane operators, 
rather than other workers at the construction site. Thirdly, by scruti -
nizing operation specifications directly linked to the activity itself, 
without encompassing workers’ physiological, psychological, or 
training aspects. Table 1 presents examples of unsafe hoisting operations 
from standards in the United States (The American Society of Mechan -
ical Engineers, 2020), the United Kingdom (The British Standards 
Institution, 2019), China (Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Devel -
opment of the People’s Republic of China, 2010), and ISO (International 
Organization for Standardization, 2020). 
2.2. Management of hoisting safety

the management of hoisting safety (Sadeghi et al., 2021). Substantial 
scholarly contributions have focused on the development of intelligent 
methods aimed at enhancing hoisting activities. These methods 
encompass supporting operators during hoisting procedures and 
providing timely alerts for safety concerns. 
In the realm of assisting hoisting operations, Shapira et al. (2014) 
introduced a laser scanning-based method to deal with blind spots. This 
approach effectively quantified both visible and concealed regions 
within the field of vision for tower crane operators. Lee et al. (2012) 
pioneered a tower crane navigation system that provided crane opera -
tors with comprehensive insights into their surroundings and the lifted 
object. Fang et al. (2018) proposed real-time reconstruction of the 
environment using computer vision technology, Inertial Measurement 
Unit (IMU), and scene modeling, enabling dynamic hoisting path plan -
ning to address safety risks posed by blind spots. Chi et al. (2012) studied 
on the user interface of remote tower crane operation systems, seeking 
to leverage comprehensive scene information for making informed 
operational decisions. Zhou et al. (2019) devised a swift-deploying, 
versatile IoT monitoring system, with a specific emphasis on large 
structure. Zhang et al. (2023) proposed immersive collaborative training 
for diverse hoisting tasks. Sun et al. (2023) introduced methods for 
collecting human/team behavior data and conducting quantitative risk

3. Early-warning methodology for unsafe hoisting operations 
3.1. DT of tower crane hoisting 
The hoisting process represents a quintessential manifestation of a 
“ Pick and Place ” task, necessitating a synergistic collaboration among 
the driver, signalman, and rigger. Within the mechanical motion plane, 
hoisting entails three primary directions of motion: lifting, luffing, and 
rotation, often compounded by the rotational effect of the lifting rope. 
Each hoisting task is executed either autonomously or in conjunction 
with others. Concurrently, it is imperative to consider the influences 
stemming from wind conditions and the presence of surrounding 
obstacles. 
Hence, in the endeavor to establish a DT model for tower crane 
hoisting, it is paramount to comprehensively integrate motion data of 
the inherent mechanisms ’ degrees of freedom and contextual 
environment data. The authors ’ prior research introduced the DT 
concept specifically tailored for mechanical motions of tower cranes 
( Jiang et al., 2022 ), underscoring the indispensability of integrating 
human-machine environment data through IoT. This integration facili -
tates functions such as mechanical simulation analysis and safety 
warnings through the interaction of virtual and physical information. 
Guided by these principles and the unique attributes of hoisting opera -
tions, the proposed DT for tower crane hoisting operations encompasses 
the following components. 
(1) Formulating a virtual model that meticulously replicates the 
dimensional proportions and dynamic motion patterns of the 
tangible tower crane in the physical realm; 
(2) Equipping the virtual tower crane with a well-defined method -
ology to identify unsafe hoisting operations. 
This methodology is subsequently employed to ensure the opera -
tional safety of actual tower crane, as shown in Fig. 1 . 
The fundamental principle underpinning DT for tower crane hoisting 
lies in the mapping rules of the physical and virtual objects, as delin -
eated in Fig. 2 . This study, aligning with the operational sequence of 
tower crane activities, systematically categorizes the pertinent physical 
objects (including their material attribute) involved in hoisting opera -
tions, such as the jib, trolley, hook, rope, suspended load, and ambient 
conditions. These objects inherently give rise to distinct operational 
parameters, subsequently relayed to the virtual model through the 
mapping rule. Notably, the virtual object model encapsulates the rep -
resentation of physical object, while the temporal evolution of attribute 
parameters is conveyed through time series data.

3.3. Integrating DT and knowledge graph for early-warning 
The integration of DT with knowledge graph represents a crucial step 
towards achieving data-driven early-warning of unsafe hoisting opera -
tions, as depicted in Fig. 7 . This association mechanism is multifaceted: 
Firstly, the mapping data is linked with hoisting parameters within the 
knowledge graph, serving as input. Secondly, according to hoisting pa -
rameters, the knowledge graph outputs safety event entities. In turn, the 
early-warning information is visualized within the DT and disseminated 
to pertinent workers, thereby facilitating comprehensive control 
feedback. 
4. Validation through laboratory experiment 
4.1. Scaled tower crane and its digital twin 
This study adopts an experimental approach to validated the pro -
posed early-warning methodology, utilizing a scaled replica of a tower 
crane. In our previous research effort, a meticulously crafted scaled 
model was developed, maintaining an approximate geometric ratio of 
“ 1:80". This scaled-down model underwent comprehensive assessment, 
confirming its ability to replicate certain characteristics of a full-scale 
tower crane in terms of motion patterns, boundary constraints, and 
material property distribution ( Jiang et al., 2022 ), as illustrated in 
Fig. 8 . 
Functionally, the motion system of the scaled tower crane model 
comprises three primary mechanisms: the lifting mechanism, rotation 
mechanism, and luffing mechanism, each with specified boundary

parameters outlined in Table 4 . The lifting mechanism utilizes an elec -
tric hoist with two lifting speeds: 60 mm/s and 30 mm/s. Meanwhile, the 
rotation and luffing mechanisms employ the Mitsubishi servo motor 
KN43J-S100, enabling a maximum rotational speed of 3000 r/min and

Conversely, the manual mode replicates real-world crane operation

The DT of the tower crane comprises two principal components: 
formulating the virtual model for the tower crane structure and its 
corresponding motion regulations, and integrating the proposed graph 
model that identifies unsafe hoisting operations. In this study, a precise 
virtual model of this scaled tower crane was crafted using SolidWorks 
software. This intricate model contains vital components including the 
hoist, mast, jib, counter-jib, ropes, as well as intricate motion mecha -
nisms like the hook and luffing apparatus. Subsequently, the entire 
model was imported into the Unity Engine (UE) platform, facilitating the 
establishment of mapping rules governing the tower crane ’ s range of 
motion. These rules governed degrees of freedom related to lifting, 
luffing, rotation, and hook swinging, while simultaneously addressing 
local constraints concerning the crane ’ s foundation, structural elements, 
and intricate mechanical interconnections, as depicted in Fig. 9 . 
This study adopted a third-party service approach to oversee and 
monitor unsafe hoisting operations. A data collection method relying on 
attachment sensors was implemented during experiments, involving the 
establishment of data collection infrastructure and the formulation of 
transmission protocols. Multiple STM32 microcontrollers interface 
power modules, communication modules, and sensor modules, incor -
porating three 9-axis IMU sensors and two laser distance sensors. Posi -
tioned strategically on the lifting, luffing, and rotation mechanisms, IMU 
sensors measure rotation angles, luffing speeds, lifting speeds, and 
swinging angles. Magnetic adhesion connections between sensors and 
various mechanisms ensure flexibility for diverse hoisting tasks. 
Communication between sensors and the virtual tower crane is 
facilitated by a wireless architecture rooted in the Message Queuing 
Telemetry Transport (MQTT) protocol. STM32 microcontrollers initiate 
connections with nearby wireless access points, subsequently linking to 
the MQTT server for data transmission. On the user end, an MQTT 
communication module retrieves sensor data from the server, capturing 
transmitted readings. This data is correlated with the degrees of freedom 
exhibited by various components within the virtual model, ensuring 
seamless synchronization with the actual movements of the tower crane, 
as illustrated in Fig. 10 .

4.2.2. Knowledge graph implementation 
To implement the knowledge graph in the DT for early warning, 
entities pertinent to unsafe hoisting operations are extracted and 
formalized into an ontology. Subsequently, these entities and their in -

5.1. Analysis of mapping accuracy 
The precision of data mapping for hoisting, luffing, and rotation was

mapped data (highlighted in orange), and a 95% confidence interval 
error band (indicated by the blue band). On the right side, error variance 
and quadratic fitting curves are displayed. 
Regarding lifting data (see Fig. 14 ), theoretical data for three sets of

mapped data, affirming data collection accuracy. Consistency in lifting 
errors across experiments suggests stable variations for each hoisting

across all three experiment sets, with fluctuations attributed to power 
system influences at hoisting initiation and termination, and the RMSEs 
of lifting are 5.53 mm, 10.18 mm, 13.18 mm. Nevertheless, on the 
whole, mapped lifting data can effectively identify unsafe hoisting 
operations.

5.2. Analysis of early-warning accuracy 
This study extensively analyzed both ACC (Accuracy) and FA (False 
Alarm) indices for early-warning of unsafe hoisting operations. ACC 
denotes the precision in generating alerts during unsafe hoisting, 
calculated as the ratio of accurate identifications to total experiments; 
FA represents the rate of false alarms during routine hoisting operations 
under consistent conditions, calculated as the ratio of incorrect identi -
fications to total experiments. The findings presented in Fig. 17 are 
based on the experimental setup detailed in Table 7 . Each set of data was 
obtained from the results of ten repeated experiments. 
Experiments revealed consistent ACC of 0.7 across all operation 
types, with SE1, SE2, SE6, and SE7 showing higher ACC and low FA. 
However, SE3 exhibited low ACC and high FA due to fluctuating weight 
thresholds during lifting acceleration and deceleration, along with var -
iations in test hoist height distances from actual operation scenarios. 
Moreover, SE5 (reverse operation) demonstrated elevated false alarm

5.3. Consistency analysis for DT of tower crane 
The evaluation of DT consistency holds significant importance as it 
serves as a primary mechanism to assess the practical applicability of 
DT. In this discourse, we delve into the assessment of the virtual-physical 
consistency of the developed DT of the tower crane, drawing upon 
established methodologies ( Zhang et al., 2022 ) ( Uhlenkamp et al., 
2022 ). This evaluation primarily encompasses four key indices: geo -
metric dimension, physical attribute, operational status, and regulatory 
protocols. Geometric dimension consistency scrutinizes the variance 
between the structural geometric dimensions and their numerical rep -
resentations. Physical attributes predominantly pertain to mechanical 
properties, gauged through dynamic response data of the tower crane 
structure. Operational status reflects the hoisting activities, assessed by 
the precision of lifting, luffing, and rotation variation. Regulatory 
compliance is evaluated by the warning accuracy pertaining to opera -
tional safety incidents. For these indices, comprehensive numerical data, 
inclusive of geometric dimensions and physical properties, have been 
compiled from our prior research ( Jiang et al., 2022 ), and operational 
status and regulatory adherence are captured as depicted in Figs. 14 – 17 .

6. Conclusion 
The study introduces an early-warning methodology that combines 
DT and knowledge graph to promptly identify unsafe hoisting opera -
tions of tower cranes. A pivotal aspect of this methodology involves 
synchronously mapping operational data for the tower crane by creating 
a DT. Subsequently, this data undergoes analysis to detect unsafe 
hoisting operations using a bespoke knowledge graph. The effectiveness 
of this methodology is validated through meticulously designed exper -
iments, wherein the results indicate that the confidence intervals of the 
mapped data pertaining to lifting, luffing, and rotation variations 
encompass the theoretical data. The results also reveal that the warning 
rate for identifying unsafe hoisting operations surpass 70%, while 
simultaneously maintaining the false alarm rate below 20%. Moreover, 
the DT consistency is evaluated, and high-quality mapping is realized in 
geometric size, physical properties, operation status and management 
rules. These findings underscore the efficacy of the proposed method -
ology in pinpointing and preventing unsafe hoisting operations. 
Nonetheless, it is important to acknowledge that this study primarily 
focused on a scaled-down tower crane within a laboratory setting, 
neglecting the influence of the dynamic site environment on the preci -
sion of the warnings. In more intricate on-site operations, there likely 
exist operator-specific habits, which introduce further disturbances that 
necessitate additional investigation. Additionally, the introduced DT 
may encounter cumulative errors in terms of data mapping. 
Future research directions can be pursued along two avenues. Firstly, 
there is a need to analyze the sources of consistency disparities in the DT 
of the tower crane and to develop a more comprehensive mapping 
framework. Secondly, it is essential to expand the range of operational 
scenarios for tower cranes by accruing empirical data from real-world 
hoisting activities.
