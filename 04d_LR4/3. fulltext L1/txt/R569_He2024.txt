A B S T R A C T
In complex mountainous terrains, tunnel construction faces unique challenges from high-temperature water
inrush hazards, a systemic risk arising from the interplay of stress, seepage, and temperature fields. Traditional
simulation methods, focusing on isolated disaster scenarios, fall short in addressing the multifaceted nature of
these risks due to geological ambiguity and data incompleteness. Digital twin technology presents an effective
solution to these challenges; however, its core challenge lies in how to utilize digital twin technology for
data-model-co-driven simulation analysis of coupled multi-physical fields in situations of incomplete data.
This paper introduces a digital twin paradigm for the simulation analysis of water inrush, which significantly
enhances efficiency and accuracy through the integration of advanced machine learning and finite element
analysis techniques. Specifically, this is achieved by combining a high-precision geological modeling method
based on Gaussian Processes (GP) with a parameter calibration method through Gaussian Process-Differential
Evolution (GP-DE) back-analysis. Firstly, a voxel structure is utilized to integrate the multi-field attribute
features of the tunnel environment. Secondly, through the integration of multi-source advanced geological
prediction data, we construct a dynamic digital twin model of the tunnel environment leveraging machine
learning techniques. To overcome the issue of low modeling accuracy, the GP is employed, enhancing the
exploitation of latent information within multi-source geophysical data. Lastly, we utilize the GP-DE back-
analysis method to calibrate the parameters of the tunnel environment, thereby enhancing the precision and
reliability of water inrush simulations. The method has been validated through application to a section of an
ultra-high-temperature water inrush tunnel in China, featuring a burial depth of 230 meters. The accuracy of
the method is corroborated by the monitoring data from the tunnel, supporting dynamic optimization design
and safety prevention measures during construction.
1. Introduction
In the advancement of railway construction in mountainous regions,
tunnels constitute more than 80% of the total infrastructure, with many
being deeply buried and facing disaster risks such as high geostress,
high geothermal conditions, and sudden water inrush (Li et al., 2021a).
Typically, engineers depend on geophysical and sensor data to predict
the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face and to formulate
the corresponding construction plans. Owing to the concealed and
spatiotemporally heterogeneous nature of the tunnel environment, this
setting transforms into a complex and open spatial environment (Wang
et al. , 2021 ). In actual construction, engineers often depend on the
interpretation of advanced geological prediction data to forecast the
geological conditions, attributable to the limitations of detection meth-
ods (Cui et al. , 2015; Huang et al. , 2022; Xin-rong et al. , 2015). The

Additionally, during construction, risks associated with
high-temperature water inrush in these geothermally active environ-
ments are more complex than traditional hazards such as high rock
temperature or sole water inrush (Wang et al., 2024a). This type
of thermal damage constitutes a systemic and comprehensive risk,
arising from the interplay of stress, seepage, and temperature fields,
which poses significant challenges in risk prevention and control during
construction (Liu et al., 2023c). Existing simulation methods that
focus on single disaster types are inadequate in accounting for the
coupled effects between multiple disaster risks, thereby hindering ac-
curate risk assessment. In disaster simulations, certain key parameters
cannot be measured accurately, leading to a significant discrepancy
between the calculated results and the actual situation. Consequently,
addressing the second core challenge in this study involves constructing
a thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) coupling water inrush simulation
model featuring accurate key parameters.
To address these challenges, the solution strategy must be intimately
aligned with practical engineering considerations, necessitating a focus
not only on precision but also on efficiency—the hallmark of digital
twin (DT). Through the integration of multi-source real-time data,
mechanistic models, and expert knowledge, DT provides an accurate
representation of the physical world and facilitates risk assessment,
prediction, and decision-making (El Saddik et al., 2021). Recently,
bolstered by the integration of the Internet of Things, big data analysis,
and advanced simulation algorithms, DT has seen a progressively wider
application in tunnel engineering (Hu et al., 2021). Indeed, DT offers
several advantages in tunnel engineering, including:
(1) DT enables the integration of multi-source observational data
and facilitates the real-time updating of geological models (Wu et al.,
2022). This rapid updating mechanism enhances the timeliness and
accuracy of the model, enabling it to more accurately reflect the actual
underground situation.
(2) DT employs advanced machine learning methods to delineate
the characteristics and trends of incomplete data in the tunnel and to
unveil its underlying principles (Latif et al., 2023; Erharter et al., 2022).
(3) Through the integration of models from various disciplines (Tao
et al., 2022), DT is capable of accurately simulating the disaster process
due to the coupling effect of multiple factors in tunnels.
Although DT has found applications in domains like tunnel rock
design (Xiaojun et al., 2023), operations and maintenance (Yu et al.,
2021), and lighting environment optimization (Shen et al., 2022),
its deployment in tunnel geological modeling and disaster simulation
remains limited. This limitation emerges due to the specialization of
these methods for specific scenarios, resulting in a lack of universality.
Therefore, in addressing the aforementioned core challenges, this paper
proposes a geological modeling and water inrush simulation analysis
method tailored for DT applications. Specifically, this study advocates
for the adoption of voxel models as the foundational framework for
DT. Voxel models are considered ideal owing to their ability to store
multiple attributes of the geological environment and to enable efficient
dynamic updates (Wu et al., 2022). The contributions of this study are
as follows:
(1) A voxel model was utilized to integrate multi-source geophysical
data, effectively characterizing the multi-dimensional attributes of the
tunnel environment. The Gaussian Process (GP) method was employed
to establish the nonlinear relationship between these attributes and
adverse geological conditions. This approach addresses the limitation
of traditional single data sources, which fail to fully capture the com-
plexity of the surrounding rock, thereby enhancing the accuracy and
reliability of the DT model of the tunnel geological environment.
(2) We propose a parameter calibration method based on the Gaus-
sian Process-Differential Evolution (GP-DE) back-analysis to improve
the reliability of the water inrush simulation by accurately calibrating
the tunnel environmental parameters. This method addresses the high
parameter uncertainty and insufficient simulation precision in tradi-
tional models when dealing with complex underground environments.
(3) We applied the proposed method to a section of a tunnel with
a burial depth of 230 meters in southwest China, which is prone to
ultra-high-temperature water inrush. The experimental results prove
the effectiveness and superiority of our method.
The structure of this paper is as follows: Section 2 briefly reviews
previous studies and summarizes the shortcomings of DT modeling of
geological environments and high-temperature water inrush disaster
simulation. Section 3 introduces a DT paradigm for geologic modeling
and inrush water simulation analysis. Section 4 introduces the mathe-
matical principles of geological environment DT modeling based on GP
and high-temperature water inrush simulation parameter calibration
based on GP-DE back-analysis. We verified the effectiveness of this
method using data from a tunnel section with a burial depth of 230
meters in southwest China and discussed the results and performance
in Section 5. Finally, in Section 6, we summarize the paper.
2. Related works
2.1. DT modeling of geological environments
The crux of tunnel geological DT modeling resides in constructing
high-precision 3D geological models. Existing methods are broadly
categorized into three types: geological statistical methods, implicit
modeling methods, and geophysical exploration interpretation meth-
ods:
(1) Geological statistical methods: For modeling rock mass frac-
tures, after collecting and statistically analyzing fracture orientation
data, stochastic Discrete Fracture Network (DFN) geological models
can be generated using methods such as Monte Carlo simulations.
These models aim to statistically replicate the actual distribution of
rock fractures (Gottron and Henk, 2021; Li et al., 2021b; Fan et al.,
2023b). Additionally, borehole data surrounding the tunnel can be used
in conjunction with Pluri-Gaussian Simulation (PGSIM) and Sequential
Gaussian Simulation (SGSIM) to generate 3D spatial variability and
uncertainty models of geological-geotechnical parameters (Gangrade
et al., 2022a,b). Although geological statistical methods demonstrate
significant potential, their effectiveness remains highly dependent on
the integrity and representativeness of the underlying datasets (Ogbu
et al., 2024).
(2) Implicit modeling methods: These methods create three-
dimensional geological models by solving implicit functions to re-
construct surfaces that represent the isosurfaces of geological bodies’
geometric shapes (Zhong et al., 2021). Typically, functions such as
Radial Basis Functions (RBF) and Hermite functions are employed to
formulate interface functions, using geological points, boreholes, and
other data as constraints to determine optimal geological interfaces.
Boolean operations are then used to segment different geological bod-
ies. This approach offers several advantages, including a high degree of
automation, smooth model surfaces, and the elimination of topological
errors (Sun et al., 2024). However, implicit modeling faces challenges
when applied to tunnel geological modeling, particularly in moun-
tainous tunnel construction. The harsh environmental conditions and
technical limitations often result in the collection of limited borehole or
cross-sectional data, leading to incomplete and sparse datasets (Xiong
et al., 2018). This issue is especially pronounced with small datasets,
where the spatial resolution of the generated geological model may not
meet the high-precision requirements essential for DT applications.
(3) Geophysical exploration interpretation methods: These meth-
ods construct geological models by observing and interpreting various
changes in geophysical fields (Rezaie et al., 2017; Olierook et al., 2021;
Panzera et al., 2022). In the tunneling field, techniques such as TSP
and Tunnel Reflection Tomography are commonly used to determine
the spatial distribution of faults, fractured zones, and underground
cavities (Fan et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2020). Additionally, methods
like TEM and IP are frequently employed to detect water content and
delineate the spatial distribution of water-rich zones (Nie et al., 2024;

In response to these challenges, and considering the increasing

2.2. Simulation of high-temperature water inrush
Presently, theories such as peridynamics (Gao et al., 2021), finite
element method (Wang et al., 2023), discrete element method (Zhou
et al., 2020), and smoothed particle hydrodynamics (Fan et al., 2023a)
are utilized to simulate and analyze the spatiotemporal dynamics of wa-
ter inrush disasters. To address the coupling effects of high geotempera-
ture, osmotic pressure, and geostress, many researchers have developed
various THM coupling mechanism models, including saturated porous
medium THM coupling models (Zhao et al., 2020), THM coupling
models that consider rock damage (Shi et al., 2023), and thermal-
nonlinear hydraulic-mechanical models (Liu et al., 2018). However, the
focus of most research on rock stability analysis or on the influential
factors frequently does not directly guide tunnel construction (Peng
et al., 2022), primarily attributed to the poor construction environment
and technical limitations that result in incomplete data collection,
hindering accurate determination of the boundary conditions and key
parameters. Nevertheless, with the aid of the back-analysis technique,
it remains possible to calibrate the numerical simulation parameters
utilizing some monitoring data in the tunnel. Back-analysis involves
inferring unknown environmental parameters through iterative opti-
mization utilizing displacement, stress, and water inrush data recorded
during the construction process (Berti et al., 2017; Feng et al., 2000;
Kaiser et al., 1990). Optimization methods can generally be divided into
deterministic and stochastic methods. Deterministic methods, including
the Levenberg–Marquardt and Nelder–Mead simplex methods, often
fall into local optima and are deemed unsuitable for problems with
multiple inversion parameters (Li et al., 2023). Stochastic optimization
methods, such as particle swarm optimization (PSO) (Chen, 2021),
differential evolution (DE) (Chang et al., 2023), simulated anneal-
ing (SA) (Gutierrez, 2020), and genetic algorithms (GA) (Sun et al.,
2021), have demonstrated flexibility in addressing complex nonlinear
optimization challenges. Moreover, existing back-analysis studies in
underground engineering typically rely on a single displacement mon-
itoring value to calibrate mechanical parameters (Zhang et al., 2020;
Luo et al., 2018). However, this approach is not suitable for mountain-
ous tunnel environments, which are influenced by the coupling effects
of high temperature, high water pressure, and high geostress. This
paper proposes a GP-GE back-analysis parameter calibration method
based on a THM coupled model.
3. DT paradigm for modeling and simulation analysis
This section introduces a DT paradigm for geological modeling and
water inrush simulation analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The concept
of DT has been extensively explored and defined by numerous scholars.
Building on the definition provided by Xiaojun et al. (2023), this
paper adapts the DT framework to suit the specific requirements and
applications of tunnel construction. The framework is divided into four
key components: Digital Twin Scene (DTS), Digital Twin Entity (DTE),
Digital Twin Disturbance (DTD), and Digital Twin Processor (DTP).
These components are not merely theoretical constructs but are also
practically applied in tunnel construction, demonstrating their real-
world utility. By using tunnel construction as an illustrative example,
this paper seeks to bridge the gap between conceptual DT frameworks
and their implementation in engineering practice, thereby advanc-
ing the understanding and application of DT in complex geological
environments.
DTS refers specifically to the application of DT technologies in the
context of tunnel water inrush scenarios. It is a focused, task-specific
application within a defined time and space, aimed at addressing
particular engineering challenges. While a single DT environment can
encompass multiple DT scenes, our DTS is dedicated to the real-time
acquisition and analysis of data related to high-temperature water
inrush hazards in tunnels.
The DTE component for tunnel modeling includes the tunnel struc-
ture, the geological environment (comprising rock mass fracture zones
and water-rich areas), and the distribution of multiple physical fields,
such as temperature and seepage fields. The construction of the tunnel
structure entity is facilitated by MicroStation software, which processes
the tunnel design’s Building Information Modeling (BIM). The fractured
zones and water-rich areas within the geological environment are
modeled using machine learning techniques based on a voxel model.
The distribution of multiple physical fields is depicted through voxel
models obtained from numerical simulations.
Excavation represents the primary role of DTD in tunnels. Following
excavation, changes in the geological environment trigger PE equip-
ment to commence data collection. This data is then fed into the DTP
for processing, geological modeling, and simulation analysis, guiding
the safe construction of the tunnel.
DTP primarily handles data processing and model invocation. It in-
volves selecting and applying appropriate models to integrate, process,
and analyze input data, thereby generating computational results. In
this study, after integrating data based on the voxel model, various
machine learning algorithms were evaluated, and the best-performing
model (as detailed in Section 4.2) was employed for geological mod-
eling. Subsequently, the parameter calibration model (as described
in Section 4.3) was applied to enhance the reliability of numerical
simulations.
4. Methodology
4.1. Overview
This section delineates the reliable simulation method for high-
temperature water inrush utilizing the DT model of the tunnel geo-
logical environment with comprehensive detail. Fig. 2 illustrates the
workflow, which comprises two key steps:
(1) DT modeling of the tunnel geological environment: Following
the integration of data within the voxel model, several machine learn-
ing algorithms were evaluated, and the best-performing model was
selected for geological modeling.
(2)Reliable Simulation Analysis Utilizing the DT Model: A back-
analysis method employing GP-DE is proposed, which takes into ac-
count the THM coupling to calibrate the tunnel environmental param-
eters. Subsequently, the multi-physical field distribution around the
tunnel is calculated by simulation based on the optimal parameters,
which further complements and improves the DT model.
4.2. DT modeling
i. Voxelization and integration of multi-source data.
Due to simpler topology, higher computational efficiency, and faster
update speed (Wu et al., 2022; Jørgensen et al., 2013; Liu et al.,
2023a), this study opts for square-shaped voxels over triangular-shaped
voxels (such as triangular prism voxels). These characteristics make
them particularly well-suited for high-precision geological modeling in
small areas and for representing digital twin models (Aleksandrov et al.,

(such as TSP) is already in a 3D regular grid format. Converting this
data into triangular prism voxels would not only introduce additional
complexity but could also introduce noise through interpolation or
resampling, potentially compromising the accuracy and stability of the
predictive model (Li and Heap, 2014).
For TSP data, TSP 303 Plus equipment along with its accompanying
software is employed for data processing, producing spatial coordinates
and characteristic values of geophysical parameters including longitu-
dinal wave velocity, shear wave velocity, and Poisson’s ratio. These
parameters are recorded in a textual format, with the voxel model
being constructed by directly assigning the values to the corresponding
coordinates. For TEM data acquisition and processing, the YCS-2000 A
mining transient electromagnetic instrument and its accompanying
software were utilized. The exported data provided resistivity distri-
butions across multiple planes within the spatial domain; however,
these distributions were unevenly spaced, with some regions exhibiting
sparse data points. The RBF interpolation method was selected for its
robustness and stability under such conditions. This method is widely
recognized in geological modeling, with numerous studies attesting to
its reliability in producing accurate and dependable results in similar
contexts (Hillier et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2018; Rusu and Rusu, 2006).
By applying RBF interpolation, we achieved a smoothly continuous
resistivity distribution, which was subsequently used to construct the
voxel model.
The subsequent step involves the unified integration of the voxel
models, which includes the following procedures: (1) Unifying the
coordinate system: Extracting grid position data from the voxel models
and mapping it onto a common spatial coordinate system for seamless
integration. (2) Defining the modeling area: Focusing on the area
around the tunnel face, the voxel models are integrated within a
specified spatial range. (3) Unifying the grid resolution: Resampling
the voxel models within the defined spatial range using the linear
interpolation method to ensure a consistent grid resolution.
ii. Model evaluation and selection.
For quantifying rock integrity and water inrush intensity in tunnels,
this study references the China national standard for the engineering
classification of rock mass ( Wu et al. , 2014), where rock integrity is

We employed six machine learning algorithms: XGBoost, AdaBoost,
CatBoost, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and GP.
These algorithms were chosen for their specific strengths in addressing
the challenges associated with tunnel geological modeling. XGBoost
and AdaBoost excel in boosting techniques, enhancing model accuracy
by combining weak learners ( Chen and Guestrin , 2016 ; Zhu et al. ,
2009). CatBoost was selected for its proficiency in handling categor-
ical data, which is prevalent in geological datasets ( Prokhorenkova
et al. , 2018 ). SVM was chosen for its robustness in managing high-
dimensional data ( Jian et al. , 2012 ), while RF was included for its
ability to mitigate overfitting and effectively handle a large num-
ber of input variables ( Dong et al. , 2013 ). GP was utilized for its
superior performance in small sample sizes and nonlinear modeling
scenarios (Zheng et al., 2022).
Each algorithm was trained on the integrated dataset, with hy-
perparameters optimized for optimal performance. We evaluated the
models using Mean Average Precision (mAP) as the assessment metric.
The model with the highest mAP score was selected for the final
geological modeling. This best-model selection method ensured the
adoption of the most accurate and reliable model, thereby guaranteeing
the precision and broad applicability of the DT framework.
iii. Calculation of unfavorable geology.
Considering the smaller data coverage of TEM compared to TSP,
and the presence of spatial areas exclusively containing TSP data,
the constructed models are categorized into two groups: one utiliz-
ing only TSP data, and the other incorporating both TSP and TEM
data. By doing calculations on each voxel, the method calculates the
three-dimensional distribution of rock mass integrity and water inrush
intensity levels within the specified area. This procedure ultimately re-
sults in the creation of a DT model representing the tunnel’s geological
environment.
4.3. Reliable simulation analysis based on DT model
i. THM coupling model and parameter calibration.
For the reliable simulation of high-temperature water inrush, se-
lecting a suitably complex mechanism model is crucial because of
the sophisticated interplay between the seepage field, stress field, and
temperature field. This interplay involves seepage affecting both tem-
perature and stress distribution, stress variations influencing rock per-
meability, and temperature changes impacting the hydrodynamic prop-
erties and mechanical behavior of the rock ( Liu et al. , 2023b). There-
fore, a model that can accurately represent these physical interactions
is essential for simulating the high-temperature water inrush process
effectively. In this research, COMSOL Multiphysics is employed to
develop a coupled mechanical-hydraulic-thermal model incorporating
Darcy’s Law for fluid flow, porous media heat transfer, and solid me-
chanics for structural analysis. To simulate real-field conditions while
simplifying the mathematical model, the following assumptions are
made: (1) The rock is considered an isotropic porous medium. (2) The
fluid, exclusively water, follows Darcy’s law for flow and Fourier’s law
for heat transfer, assuming local thermal equilibrium.(3) A uniform
load is applied at the top of the model.
In addition to selecting a comprehensive mechanism model, the ac-
curacy of simulation parameters is crucial for ensuring the reliability of
results. This study utilizes a back-analysis technique, a well-established
method for parameter calibration, which enables the refinement of
model parameters based on observed data. Specifically, a joint back-
analysis method using the GP-DE algorithm is proposed. This technique
calibrates critical simulation parameters, such as elastic modulus, per-
meability, and geothermal gradient, by conducting a detailed analysis
of key indicators, including tunnel vault settlement, water inflow, and
rock temperature, as illustrated in Fig. 3 .

5. Experiments and analysis
5.1. Study area and dataset
A section of a mountain tunnel, known for its complexity and
challenging conditions, serves as the experimental site for this study.
Fig. 4 displays the geological longitudinal section of the area, show-
ing a tunnel that spans 3 km with a depth exceeding 1200 m. The
lithological composition mainly consists of schist, with occurrences of
granite and various structural rocks. The site is characterized by well-
developed joints and rock masses that are predominantly intact yet
partly fractured, situated within the Milin fault zone. This fault zone is
notable for its intense hydrothermal activity; atmospheric precipitation
and melting snow absorb underground heat, leading to the formation
of high-temperature thermal water that permeates through rock frac-
tures. As a result, the tunnel environment experiences high geothermal
temperatures, significant seepage pressures, and strong geostresses, fre-
quently causing high-temperature water inrush incidents. The complex
tunnel environment, combined with the limitations of detection meth-
ods and the multi-source, heterogeneous, and incomplete nature of the
available detection data, presents substantial challenges in accurately
developing a DT model of the tunnel’s geological environment using
conventional approaches. Therefore, this particular tunnel was selected
for the experimental demonstration of the methodologies proposed in
this study.

5.2. DT modeling and accuracy evaluation
Due to the variability of tunnel geological conditions, a significant
imbalance exists in the collected sample categories related to water
inrush intensity and rock integrity. Maintaining a data sample ratio of
approximately 1:1 is essential to more accurately reflect classification
results ( Ruben et al. , 2022 ). Consequently, this study employed the
adaptive synthetic algorithm to address the imbalance in sample data.
Given the smaller data range of TEM compared to TSP, and the
existence of spatial areas with only TSP data, predictive models were
constructed using either TSP data alone or a combination of TSP and
TEM data. To ensure the precision of the DT model, this study ex-
tensively evaluated algorithms including XGBoost, AdaBoost, CatBoost,
SVM, RF, and GP. The GP model employed maximum likelihood esti-
mation for hyperparameter optimization, while Bayesian optimization
algorithms were applied to the other models. The experimental results,
presented in Fig. 6 , utilized mAP as the criterion for comparative
analysis of the six algorithms. The results indicate that for evaluating
rock mass integrity, XGBoost, AdaBoost, CatBoost, SVM, and GP all
demonstrated high performance, with mAP values exceeding 0.92,
significantly outperforming RF, which achieved mAP values of 0.81
and 0.80. Notably, the simultaneous use of TEM and TSP data did not
significantly enhance the performance of any model. In the assessment
of water inrush intensity, models using only TSP data yielded lower
mAP values, ranging from 0.54 to 0.78. However, the introduction of
TEM data significantly improved the performance of all models, with
XGBoost, AdaBoost, and CatBoost achieving mAP values greater than
0.93, notably outperforming SVM at 0.72 and RF at 0.74. In summary,
while TSP data alone is effective for evaluating rock mass integrity,
it is insufficient for accurately assessing water inrush intensity. The
inclusion of TEM data, however, significantly enhances the accuracy of
water inrush intensity evaluations. XGBoost, AdaBoost, CatBoost, and
GP all demonstrated strong performance on the dataset used in this
study, making them viable options for subsequent geological modeling.
The GP model was selected for DT modeling due to its excellent per-
formance in our experiments, particularly its effectiveness in handling
small, noisy datasets. In complex mountainous tunnel environments,
where data collection is challenging and often results in incomplete,

5.3.2. Back-analysis
The range of values for environmental parameters within the tunnel
is detailed in Table 1 . The model applies elastoplastic theory and
the Mohr–Coulomb failure criteria to define mechanical parameters.
Elastic modulus and cohesion, identified as irreversible variables in
Liu’s research ( Li et al. , 2023), are thus earmarked for back-analysis.
Porosity, permeability, and hydraulic gradient, vital for influencing
groundwater flow and the stability of subterranean structures, are
deemed critical hydraulic parameters and selected for back-analysis. In
the context of thermal analysis, the geothermal gradient and thermal
conductivity, pivotal for simulating geothermal flow, are chosen as pa-
rameters for back-analysis. The determination of these parameter value
ranges is based on laboratory analyses and extensive references from
the Engineering Geology Handbook ( The Compilation Committee of
Engineering Geology Handbook, 2007) and ‘‘Code for Geological Inves-
tigation of Water Resources and Hydropower Engineering’’ ( GB50487,
2008), ensuring a thorough and comprehensive approach to accurately
modeling the tunnel environmental conditions.

ment of vault, water inflow, and tunnel face surface temperature. The

5.3.3. Simulation based on optimal parameters
Optimal parameters were applied for simulation, as depicted in
Fig. 14. The analysis of the temperature field revealed a gradual
increase in temperature from the surface to deeper underground lev-
els. However, temperatures near the tunnel walls remained lower,
illustrating the thermal conductivity characteristics of the subsurface
environment and the cooling effects induced by tunneling activities.
Regarding the seepage field, the findings indicate that water pressure
peaks within water-rich areas, subsequently dispersing towards the
tunnel’s palm surface and adjacent areas of lower pressure. This behav-
ior aligns with established theories of water movement within porous
media. Integrating these multifaceted simulation outcomes into a voxel-
based DT framework significantly broadens the model’s applicability
and depth. Moreover, this integration provides extensive data support,
crucial for the design and execution phases of tunnel construction
projects.
5.3.4. Reliability evaluation
To verify the accuracy of the proposed method, predictions re-
garding the vault settlement, surrounding rock temperature, and water
inflow at positions 6 m and 15 m ahead of the excavation face were
made. These predictions were then compared with on-site monitoring
data, as detailed in Table 3. The forecasts for vault settlement and
internal rock temperature were highly accurate, exhibiting a MAPE of
5.4% and 2.9%, respectively. However, the accuracy of water inflow
predictions at the tunnel face was comparatively lower, at 16.1%. Field
observations indicate that the tunnel water inflow at the face is signif-
icantly influenced by the location and quantity of deepened boreholes.
Consequently, variability in predicted water inflow is anticipated, given
the non-uniform distribution of surrounding rock fractures.
The results suggest that the excavation analysis, based on the DT
model, closely aligns with the subsequent construction excavation pro-
cess. The DT model of the tunnel’s geological environment, constructed
in this study, accurately depicts the actual distribution of water-rich
and fractured zones in the tunnel, showcasing high accuracy and reli-
ability.
5.4. Discussion
High-temperature water inrush poses significant risks to complex
mountainous tunnel construction. Accurate geological modeling and
reliable simulation analysis are crucial for disaster prediction and the
optimization of construction design. This paper distinguishes itself from
previous studies on water inrush disaster simulation in several key
aspects:
(1) This study improves the efficiency of tunnel engineering. By
leveraging a digital twin framework, we integrated multi-source hetero-
geneous data, multidisciplinary mechanistic models, and high-
performance algorithms to achieve automated and precise geological
modeling as well as reliable simulation of water inrush hazards. In
practical applications, the entire process of data acquisition, process-
ing, analysis, and visualization is completed in approximately 2 h.
In contrast, according to feedback from on-site engineers, traditional
rock mass environment prediction typically takes around 2 days, in-
volving manual sketching of the tunnel face, processing of advance
geological prediction data, followed by interpretation by specialized
engineers, and finally sending back a comprehensive assessment re-
port to guide construction (Shi et al., 2017; Liu and Gan, 2023; Liu
et al., 2009; Huang et al., 2022). This approach not only significantly
improves efficiency but also ensures accuracy. Compared to the uncer-
tainties that may arise from manual interpretation, the results obtained
through high-performance machine learning algorithms and coupled
simulations in this study are far more reliable.
(2) An innovative approach is introduced by grading two key factors
that influence water inrush: rock mass integrity and water inflow inten-
sity. These classifications are then used to develop a high-resolution
digital twin geological model, significantly enhancing the reliability
of simulations. A key aspect of this research is the integration of
high-resolution advanced geological forecasting data, which has en-
abled the construction of a geological model with a resolution of
1 meter—sufficient to support high-precision water inrush simulations.
In contrast, previous studies typically relied on geological models de-
rived from interpolated sparse borehole data, whose resolution is far
inferior to that of models obtained directly from geophysical data at
the tunnel face (Liu et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b;
Gao et al., 2019). The inaccuracies in these lower-resolution geological
models and their boundary conditions have often compromised the
reliability of simulation.
(3) This paper presents a novel GP-DE back-analysis parameter
calibration method developed within a THM coupled model. By uti-
lizing temperature, water inflow, and displacement monitoring data
from within the tunnel, this method directly or indirectly calibrates the
key simulation parameters, thereby further enhancing the reliability

its applicability and precision.
(2) The employed mathematical model has been simplified to suit
the tunnel environment, which may not sufficiently depict high-temp-
erature inrush water scenarios in more complex geological settings.
6. Conclusions
This study introduces a novel method for simulating high-temp-
erature water inrush incidents based on a DT model of the tunnel’s
geological environment, aimed at tackling water inrush and high tem-
peratures in complex tunnel construction. The approach integrates
voxel models with advanced geological prediction data, utilizing high-
performance machine learning algorithms to construct a DT model of
rock mass integrity and water inrush intensity. By combining machine
learning algorithms with the finite element method and employing
GP-DE for multi-source tunnel data joint back-analysis, the study accu-
rately estimates optimal tunnel environmental parameters, facilitating
reliable simulation analysis of high-temperature water inrush. Overall,
this methodology provides a novel approach to addressing complex
challenges in tunnel engineering and establishes a solid foundation
for future research. Future developments will build on this study,
further enhancing digital twin models and simulation methods to more
effectively mitigate various disaster risks in tunnel engineering.
