Summary
Traditional post-earthquake inspection of civil infrastructure is conducted manually,
taking a considerable amount of time and often putting inspectors in harm's way. This
problem is exacerbated in modern cities, where millions of people can be left home-
less until their residences are deemed safe to reinhabit. Image collection enabled by
commercial unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) combined with computer vision-based
techniques has provided an alternative with high potential for rapid post-earthquake
inspection. However, the extracted images of the damage alone are inadequate to
evaluate the system-level safety condition of a structure. The quality of the visual
information also heavily relies on the effectiveness of the UAV inspection scheme
which is susceptible to environmental uncertainties. To this end, a graphics-based
digital twin (GBDT) framework is developed for UAV-aided post-earthquake inspec-
tion of high-rise buildings and validated using a high-rise building in Guangzhou,
China. The GBDT is comprised of a finite element (FE) model and a photorealistic
computer graphics (CG) model, with the latter being informed by the former, jointly
providing as a comprehensive virtual representation of the structure so that every
step of the post-earthquake inspection procedure can be planned and evaluated vir-
tually. First, to avoid the cumbersome nature of constructing the graphical represen-
tation of the numerous components in high-rise buildings, the CG model in the GBDT
is created by automatically importing structural components from the FE model and
adding nonstructural components according to the dimensions of the as-built struc-
ture. This fast modeling process as well as the accuracy of the virtual presentation
are validated by point cloud comparisons between the CG model and the as-built
structure. Subsequently, the GBDT is used to showcase the evaluation of UAV flight
schemes for post-earthquake inspection of high-rise buildings. To shorten flight time
and place more emphasis on potential damage, FE analysis is conducted to determine
the earthquake-induced damage locations. Consistent damage hotspots are then

1 | INTRODUCTION
Earthquakes are among the most catastrophic natural disasters faced by modern societies. Damage to civil infrastructure is inevitable due to the rapid
urbanization in regions of high seismicity. Rapid inspection and evaluation of damaged structures therefore become crucial and urgent to ensure the
safety of life and property, as well as to restore normalcy to society. Traditional post-earthquake inspections are usually conducted by structuralengi-
neering experts based on visual checks and empirical judgment. For example, during the 1978 Thessaloniki earthquake in Greece, it took almost 1000
engineers about 2 months to inspect more than 150,000 apartments in 30,000 buildings within Thessaloniki city and 30,000 apartments in 13,000
buildings in the surrounding rural area. This earthquake, along with the subsequent 1981 Aklyonides–Korinthos earthquake, led to the establishment
of the Earthquake Planning and Protection Organization of Greece in 1983, which marked the beginning of systematic post-earthquake inspection
guidelines.
1 Similarly, the U.S. Applied Technology Council published a field manual for post-earthquake safety evaluation of buildings in 1989. 2 In
1997, China passed the law on Earthquake Prevention and Disaster Reduction 3 which provided similar guidelines. Despite the standardization of
post-earthquake inspection procedures by these manuals and laws, inspections are still conducted manually, and therefore are incredibly time-
consuming and perilous for inspectors, especially when dealing with high-rise buildings. Because of the significant heights and dimensions of such
buildings, damage to the upper floors is nearly impossible to identify from the ground and thus requires extensive inspection time inside the damaged
building. Moreover, high-rise buildings play a major role in sheltering large populations in cities; until these buildings are inspected, the resid ents are
essentially homeless. Thus, means for rapid post-earthquake inspection of the civil infrastructure is direly needed.
With the recent development of the drone industry,
4–7 image collection enabled by commercial unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) provides a
significant opportunity for rapid post-earthquake inspections. Researchers have proposed that UAV-collected images can be utilized to build com-
puter graphics (CG) models with point cloud reconstruction techniques,
8,9 which can then be used for visual inspection. The capacity and effec-
tiveness of the inspection can be further facilitated by automated damage detection based on the UAV-collected images. Early research about
damage detection techniques employed heuristic feature extraction methods, applying a threshold or a classifier to the output of a manually
designed filter for a particular type of damage. 10–13 To address the challenges faced by such heuristic feature extraction techniques, such as com-
plex design, narrow applicability, and low accuracy, researchers have turned toward machine learning-based methods. 14,15 Machine learning-
based algorithms can handle several types of damage simultaneously. Damage can also be projected onto a mesh model to better guide engineers
in assessing structural conditions. For example, Hoskere et al. 16 generated a 3D mesh model from UAV-collected images of a damaged building
following the 2017 Central Mexico Earthquake. By projecting the inferred damage information onto the 3D mesh model, a condition-aware model
was developed for damage documentation and higher-level inspection decisions. However, such drone surveys still need to be flown by human
operators in the field.
The Resilient City initiative developed by the San Francisco Bay Area Planning and Urban Research Association (SPUR) is aimed at developing
customized inspection programs and pre-assess structures to enable rapid post-earthquake response. 17 While the Resilient City initiative is
designed to allow building owners to better respond to seismic disasters, human inspectors are still relied upon.
Inspired by the Resilient City Initiative, Wang et al.18 proposed a graphics-based digital twin (GBDT) framework for automated computer vision-
based post-earthquake inspection and evaluation of as-built structures. The concept of the digital twin was first introduced in the early 2000s 19 and
has since expanded to the field of civil engineering, encompassing the monitoring and management of construction 20–22 and the operation and
maintenance of civil infrastructure.23–25 The digital twin, continuously updated by the sensor information and load history from the physical world,
provides predictive information about the physical entity and demonstrates the potential for improved understanding of the physical world. How-
ever, much of the information about the current state of a structure is visual in nature. Thus, the GBDT builds on the work by Hoskere et al. 26–28

2 | DEVELOPMENT OF THE GBDT
This section provides the steps to create the GBDT of the high-rise building, including FE modeling, CG modeling, and the environment in which
the building is located.
2.1 | Target structure
The target structure of this study is a residential building located in Guangzhou, China. This 45-floor high-rise building, which is one of two twin
towers, is a reinforced concrete shear wall structure shown in Figure 3a. The dimension of the structural plan is 25.0 m /C2 27.2 m, and the total
height is 138 m, including 6 m for the ground floor, and 3 m for each of the remaining floors. The concrete tensile strength of the shear walls on
floors 1–8, 9–21, and 22 –45 are 2.04, 1.89, and 1.71 N/mm
2, respectively, and the concrete tensile strength of beams and slabs is 1.43 N/mm 2.
The layout of the structure is shown in Figure 3b.
2.2 | FE model of the structure
The FE model is a key component of the GBDT, as it not only provides information on structural components for the CG model but also is used to
predict the damage through nonlinear time history analysis. In this study, the structural analysis software SAP2000 is adopted to build the
nonlinear FE model of the target structure. Structural components including the beams, columns, slabs, and shear walls are constructed in the FE
model. The beams and columns are modeled as frame elements, the slabs as thick shells, and the shear walls as nonlinear layered shells. Three
types of nonlinearity are considered for the FE model in this study, including the P –Δ effect for the overall structure, the layered shell arrange-
ment for the shear walls, and the plastic hinge setting for the beams. The plastic hinges are placed at one-tenth locations near the two ends of the
beams. Modal analysis is conducted to examine the natural periods and mode shapes of the structure. The first three natural periods and

2.3 | CG model of the structure
This section describes the construction of both the structural and nonstructural components of the CG model. The structural components are
automatically built based on the FE model, whereas the nonstructural components are manually built in accordance with the architectural draw-
ings. The CG model is then textured using the on-site photos of the real building.
2.3.1 | Structural components of the CG model
The open-source 3D software, Blender, is adopted to build the CG model of the target structure. In the CG model, the structural components are
generated automatically based on the mesh information of the FE model in two steps. First, the mesh information of the FE model, including the
node coordinates, shell element nodes, and frame nodes, is exported and saved using the SAP-Python Application Programming Interface (API);
next, the saved mesh information is imported into the Blender environment using the Blender Python API. The structural components of the CG
model generated by the above process are shown in Figure
5a.
2.3.2 | Nonstructural components of the CG model
The nonstructural components on the exterior surfaces of the high-rise structure include the balconies, glass windows, and shutters. Since these
nonstructural components are configured in different ways, modeling with the Blender Python API would be time-consuming and labor-intensive.
Therefore, this study uses the graphical user interface (GUI) to manually build the nonstructural components of the CG model. Cube units are cre-
ated and reshaped in Blender according to the dimensions in the architecture drawings to obtain a refined 3D replica of the nonstructural compo-
nent. Figure
6a shows the 3D model of the balcony part by the above method. The finished nonstructural components are then assembled into
the structural components to obtain a complete CG model of the high-rise structure (see Figure 5b).
2.3.3 | Texture of the CG model

textures are prepared including the base color map, displacement map, normal map, and roughness map. Those textures are combined through

on site, and a high-quality point cloud model of the as-built structure is constructed, which serves as a reference for validation of the CG model.

more effective UAV flight path.

3.3 | Dimension validation of the CG model
The high-quality point cloud model of the as-built structure, which was obtained in the previous section, is now being used as a reference to vali-
date the dimensions of the CG model. The dimensions of the CG model are directly exported from the Blender (not from rendered images), and a
quantitative assessment is made between the CG model and the as-built structure by Hausdorff distance. Hausdorff distance is a widely used per-
formance measure in computer vision to calculate the distance between two point sets. 33 The two sets are close in the Hausdorff distance if
every point of either set is close to some point of the other set. Therefore, the CG model is considered accurate with a large quantity of points
located in the range of a small Hausdroff distance. Using the physical dimension as the scale for the Hausdroff distance, the proportion of points
reaches 86.01% in the Hausdroff distance range less than 100 cm. This difference of 100 cm is equal to a percentage error of less than 4% in ref-
erence to the dimensions of the structure (25.0 m /C2 27.2 m /C2 138 m), which indicates a good agreement between the CG model and the as-built
structure.
4 | DEVELOPMENT OF THE DAMAGED CG MODEL
The damaged CG model which serves as a testbed for UAV-aided structural inspection is built. To place more emphasis on potential damage loca-
tions, FE analysis is conducted to determine the damage locations under a specific earthquake. Those damage locations, referred to as damage
hotspots, are then marked in the CG model with an additional texture layer.
4.1 | Seismic nonlinear analysis of the FE model
In practical applications, the actual excitation should be used to acquire damage information of the structure by FE analysis. For illustration pur-
poses, the seismic ground motion of the 1940 El Centro earthquake is adopted as the excitation in this study. The peak ground acceleration (PGA)
of the seismic excitation is deliberately scaled to a large value of 0.22 g so that significant damage can be detected in the structure. This PGA cor-
responds to the rare earthquake of intensity seven according to the Chinese seismic code. 34
Time histories of structural responses are obtained through the nonlinear time history analysis under the seismic excitation. Interstory drift is
an important index to evaluate the safety condition of structures. Cai et al. 35 suggested that interstory drift can be divided into harmless and
harmful interstory drifts. The former is caused by the pure bending of the structure while the latter is caused by the shear reaction between the
floors which is the main cause of damage in the structure. Figure
11a shows the maximum total and harmful interstory drift ratios (interstory drift
divided by the height of the floor) at each floor of the structure under the scaled (0.22 g) El Centro earthquake. The interstory drifts may follow a
different pattern under earthquakes with different frequency characteristics. The elastic limit of the interstory drift ratio (1/1000 according t o the
Chinese seismic code 34) is marked with a vertical line as a reference. Note that this elastic limit is a conservative guideline for engineering design,
and thus, damage is not expected on floors with interstory drift ratios below this limit. By referring to this limit, the maximum total interstory drif t
ratios (all exceed the elastic limit) suggest that all the floors have structural damage whereas the maximum harmful interstory ratios suggest that
floors from 15 to 17 are not damaged. By checking the stresses of the shear walls, the concrete condition is found to be consistent with the harm-
ful interstory drift ratios rather than the total interstory drift. Specifically, the shell stresses on floors 14 –21 are less than the tensile strength of
the concrete, indicating that the concrete is intact without any cracks. Therefore, the harmful interstory drift is used to determine the floors
where damage is most likely to occur. Take floor 6 for example, Figure
11b shows the harmful interstory drift time history, 3.07 and 4.56 s
(labeled as A and B, respectively) are marked with red dots for analysis in the subsequent section.
4.2 | Damage hotspot

are further examined. From Figure 11b, the two points in time with the largest magnitudes (i.e., 3.07 and 4.56 s) are selected to explore further.
The stress color maps for the time point A (3.07 s) are shown in Figure 12, for example, where the color bar of the stress color map is adjusted so
that the minimum tensile stress displayed is the same as the ultimate tensile strength (2.04 N/mm 2) of the concrete on those floors (floors 1 –8).

5 | GBDT-BASED POST-EARTHQUAKE UAV FL IGHT PLANNING FOR FIELD INSPECTION
To ensure the efficiency of the UAV-aided post-earthquake inspection in practical implementation, the synthetic environment of the GBDT is
applied as the testbed to compare different inspection strategies. First, restrictions imposed by the physical environment including obstacles,
weak satellite signal, wind speed, and lighting conditions are considered for the CG model. Based on these environmental considerations and the
results of the FE analysis, the flight path and camera setting are designed. Subsequently, the designed flight schemes are carried out in the syn-
thetic environment, and images rendered in the view of the UAV are obtained. Finally, different UAV flight schemes are evaluated, and the most
appropriate scheme is determined.
5.1 | Environmental restrictions
For illustration purposes, non-ideal environmental conditions that may affect path planning and camera settings for the UAV are assumed, serving
as an example of the necessary considerations in UAV flight planning.
Satellite signal interference can jeopardize UAV flight stability and accurate positioning, especially in complex urban environments. In this
study, the target building is located a mere 15 m away from the twin building (143 m tall and situated on the west side of the target building). The
satellite signal can be significantly disturbed in the area between the two adjacent high-rise buildings, which may increase the risk of flight-path
failure and potential drone accidents. Moreover, strong winds resulting from the channeling effect between tall buildings can also destabilize UAV
flights. The wind forces between parallel buildings can reach twice the normal level,
37 due to increased wind speeds in the narrow passage. There-
fore, avoiding the narrow passage between the adjacent buildings when planning the flight path under strong winds is advisable. Additionally, the
randomness of earthquakes requires UAV inspections to be carried out regardless of time, including times with poor lighting conditions such as
dawn or dusk. In this study, poor lighting conditions akin to dusk are simulated in Blender using a sunlight strength of 500 W/m
2. The lighting
condition will impact the selection of camera parameters.
While the aforementioned environmental restrictions are set for this specific example, adjustments can be easily made to explore the effects
of various factors on path planning within the synthetic environment. For instance, simulating different lighting conditions can be achieved by
modifying the light source and its intensity, introducing a dusty environment can be simulated by adding noise to the texture, and a significant
change in wind speed can be replicated by randomly altering the pose of the camera in the synthetic environment.

5.2.1 | Flight path
Two distinct flight paths, including a circular path and a parallel path, are considered for the high-rise structure (Figure 14). The two trajectories
are inherently simple, as there is no need to account for collisions between multiple UAVs, and the presence of obstacles around the building is
limited. The circular flight path takes the structure as the center of the circle. Such types of trajectory plans, as seen in Cheng et al, 38 provide a
unified algorithm applicable to buildings of various sizes, ensuring sufficient coverage. In contrast, the parallel path is more intricate, as it ad opts
the structural surface with damage hotspots as a point of interest. Therefore, the normal distance from the UAV to those critical shear walls is set
uniformly. The flight paths of different surfaces are connected by curved paths at the corners. Combining factors including obstacles, satellite si g-
nal, and wind speed, both flight paths are set to avoid the area between the target building and the adjacent building.
5.2.2 | Camera setting
Camera settings determine the quality of the images and 3D reconstruction of the structure. A synthetic movable camera consistent with the DJI
Phantom 4 Pro V2.0 UAVused in the original survey is chosen for simulated image collection. In the Blender, the built-in camera uses the aperture
parameters to adjust the camera exposure. Two very different aperture sizes, f/2.8 and f/5.6, are selected. The purpose is to illustrate that the
quality of UAV-collected images can be evaluated through trials in the synthetic environment. For the physical camera, the exposure can be
adjusted by changing the shutter speed, and the conversion between the aperture size of the synthetic camera and the shutter speed of the phys-
ical camera should be acquired for practical implementation. In the practical application, camera parameters need to be determined considering
the UAV's distance from building surfaces and resolution requirements.

5.3 | Evaluation of UAV flight schemes
In this section, the three UAV flight schemes are evaluated to determine the most appropriate scheme for practical application. The resulting
inspection strategy should meet the following requirements: (1) Photos of potentially damaged parts (damage hotspots) must be clear to deter-
mine the extent of damage on critical components. (2) The generated point cloud model of the overall structure must be accurate to judge the
location and distribution of damage. (3) The UAV captures photos in as short a time as possible. It is worth mentioning that the time required to
compare different flight paths using the GBDT primarily depends on the rendering of images from the CG model and the construction of the point
cloud model. For the 45-floor high-rise building, the total time required for these two tasks is approximately one and a half hours. However, to
assess the quality of photos and point cloud models, focusing on representative sections of the building (e.g., three to five floors) is sufficient.
Consequently, the assessment time for one flight path can be limited to less than 10 minutes. Assessing different flight paths and choosing the
appropriate one for the field inspection can therefore be implemented in a relatively short time.

5.3.3 | Time of flight
The inspection time for potentially damaged structures in post-earthquake scenarios should be as short as possible as the battery life of most
commercial UAVs is approximately 30 min. 32 The single-lap flight distance is approximated to be 146 and 124 m for the circular and parallel paths,
respectively, and the number of laps covering all the damaged floors (floors 1 –13 and 22 –45) throughout the height of the building is 43. The
speed of the UAV is assumed to be 3 m/s, a value lower than the maximum UAV speed of 5 m/s as adopted in Nagasawa et al. 40 This intentional
reduction is implemented to ensure the safety of the flight, particularly under the challenging conditions of strong wind and poor lighting that are
considered in the current example. Thus, the total time consumptions for the circular path (Scheme (a)) and parallel path (Schemes (b) and (c))
are 2093 and 1777 s, respectively. The flight schemes with the parallel path require a shorter time than that with the circular path, and the image
collection using the parallel path can be completed within the battery life of the UAV. In the case where the inspection time is beyond the battery
life of the UAV due to the large volume of the structure, change of battery should also be accounted for in the inspection time.
Based on the evaluation of the visual check, quality of the point cloud reconstruction, and time of the flight, Scheme (b) outperforms the
others. This flight scheme is recommended for the assumed field UAV-aided post-earthquake inspection of the high-rise structure. It is important
to note that the conclusion drawn is specific to this particular example. Alterations in environmental restrictions and UAV speed may result in a
different optimal inspection scheme.

6.1 | Component-level FE analysis
In this study, only the global FE analysis of the high-rise structure is conducted and the CG model with damage hotspots is constructed accord-
ingly. Extending the previous work about PBGM, 18,28 the responses of the high-rise structure from the global FE analysis will be used as the
boundary conditions to carry out a more detailed study on the structural and nonstructural components. The damage patterns resulting from
the finer component-level FE analysis will be used to render realistic damage textures including cracks, spalling, and exposed rebars for the CG
model.
6.2 | CG model construction
Because the nonstructural components and the building materials of high-rise structures for a certain function are limited to just a few types
(e.g., apartment and office), a library of building parts and textures should be established. In addition, CG algorithms will be developed to obtain
ready-to-use damage textures from the stress color maps of both global and component-level FE analyses. Consequently, the whole building pro-
cess of the CG model will be automated to further raise the modeling efficiency, especially when creating the GBDT for a community of buildings.
6.3 | UAV path planning
In addition to guiding UAV path planning, the damaged hotspots identified by the FE model of the GBDT can be utilized to further optimize the
inspection strategy. A detailed examination of the damaged areas, achieved by capturing additional photos, can enhance the accuracy of damage
detection and evaluation. Furthermore, while UAV paths were tested for inspecting a single building in this study, the potential for multiple UAV
path planning for a cluster of nearby buildings, as demonstrated in Nagasawa et al,
40 can be implemented. The quality of UAV-collected images
can then be evaluated with the assistance of the GBDT.
6.4 | Damage detection
Given the ease with which UAV inspections can be conducted in the synthetic environment, automated scene mapping and feature recognition
using advanced machine learning, as discussed in Kerle et al, 41 can also be conveniently implemented based on the virtually realistic images col-
lected by the fictional UAV. The GBDT will be applied to test and train machine learning algorithms for automated detection of damage and
assessment of structural conditions. In particular, the CG model with detailed damage patterns (a realistic visual representation of the as-built
structure) will be used to generate synthetic data for the purpose of seismic damage detection and identification.
42,43 The information obtained
from the visual damage will then be trained to infer the structural condition after the earthquake, making the post-earthquake inspection and
evaluation of structures more intelligent and efficient.
7 | CONCLUSION
In this study, a GBDT framework is developed for UAV-aided post-earthquake inspection of high-rise buildings and validated using a real-world
high-rise building in Guangzhou, China. The GBDT is comprised of an FE model and a CG model, with the former simulating the physical proper-
ties and the latter being the photorealistic virtual representation of the as-built structure. The virtual representation of the structure combine d
with environmental considerations from the physical world provides a synthetic testbed in which every step of the post-earthquake inspection
procedure can be planned and evaluated virtually.
To avoid the cumbersome nature of constructing the numerous components in the CG model of the high-rise building, structural components
are automatically imported from the FE model, whereas the nonstructural components are added according to the architectural drawings. Visual
checks and quantitative comparisons using the point cloud distribution are made between the CG model and the as-built structure. The high con-
sistency found between the CG model and its physical counterpart validates the fast modeling process as well as the accuracy of the virtual
representation.
To demonstrate the efficacy of the GBDT framework in assisting practical applications, the validated GBDT of the high-rise building is used
to evaluate UAV flight schemes for post-earthquake inspection. To place more emphasis on potential damage, FE analysis is conducted to deter-
mine the damage locations under the earthquake, and consistent damage hotspots are then marked on the CG model. Environmental restrictions
such as obstacles, weak satellite signals, wind speed, and lighting conditions are also considered, further enhancing the authenticity of the
