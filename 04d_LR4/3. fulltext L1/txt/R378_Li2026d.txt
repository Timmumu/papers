Fatigue cracking threatens the safety of orthotropic steel decks (OSDs) in long-span bridges, yet
current manual inspection lacks predictive depth. We present a closed-loop framework integrating
autonomous robotic inspection, vision-based quantiﬁcation, and ﬁnite-element fracture mechanics to
enable adaptive fatigue prognosis. In laboratory validation, the robotic platform achieved a mean
localization accuracy of 2.7 ± 0.8 cm, meeting structural precision requirements. Field deployment on
an in-service cable-stayed bridge demonstrated that automated inspection reduced average time per
girder from 124.6 to 50.4 minutes—a 59.6% reduction. Identiﬁed cracks were assimilated into a digital
twin for adaptive state updating. Analysis of discrepancies between simulated and observed
propagation paths—interpreted via stress intensity factor ﬁelds—highlighted signiﬁcant mixed-mode
fracture effects, particularly elevated Mode-II (shear) contributions, as a primary source of predictive
uncertainty under in-service conditions. This integration of robotics and digital twins provides a
scalable solution for automated maintenance. Beyond labor reduction, the framework establishes a
data-driven path toward proactive life-cycle management, enhancing the structural resilience and
long-term safety of critical transportation infrastructure.
Orthotropic steel decks (OSDs) have become the preferred structural form
for long-span steel bridges owing to th eir high strength-to-weight ratio,
modular construction efﬁciency, and favorable structural performance 1.
However, welded joints in OSDs are highly susceptible to fatigue damage
under repetitive traf ﬁc loading, posing a serious threat to structural
integrity2. Fatigue cracking at critical connections, particularly deck-rib and
diaphragm-rib welds3,4, has emerged as one of the predominant failure
modes in aging infrastructure. Field investigations indicate that steel bridges
with over 10 years of service can develop more than 2000 fatigue cracks per
structure. Such extensive damage directly undermines load-bearing capacity
and has become a critical bottleneck for safe and reliable service
5.
Conventional structural health monitoring (SHM) systems focus on
global bridge responses such as displacement and vibration6. While effective
for detecting global anomalies, they a re limited in identifying localized
fatigue damage in OSDs, where the initiation sites are spatially unpredictable
and temporally uncertain
7. Fixed sensor networks for full-bridge coverage
are often economically infeasible and still fail to ensure complete detection8.
Consequently, engineers still rely heavily on manual inspection for fatigue
evaluation. This process requires temporary scaffolding, visual crack iden-
tiﬁcation by trained inspectors, and manual documentation. Within con-
ﬁned steel box girders, such procedures are inefﬁcient, prone to subjective
error, and limited in inspection frequency9.
Recent advances in robotics have created new opportunities for auto-
mated bridge inspection. Various robotic platforms have been proposed,
including bio-inspired climbing robots
10, unmanned aerial vehicles 11,
ground vehicles12, and magnetic adhesion climbing robots13. Among them,
magnetic wheeled robots are particularly promising for navigating inside
steel box girders due to their stability and access to weld details14.T oe n h a n c e
detection accuracy and reduce operator dependence, camera-based
inspection has been increasingly adopted
15, applied to quantify crack
geometry16,17. Robotic inspection has further beneﬁted from the integration
of high-resolution cameras and NDT devices18,19, enabling systematic data
collection. Despite these advances, existing studies primarily aim to improve
crack recognition precision rather than integrating results into system-level

Finite element modeling (FEM) has long played a central role in

growing interest in fatigue life prediction, underscoring its utility and
accuracy

have been developed to simulate and assess the fatigue performance of
OSDs

models, DTs enable dynamic interaction between physical structures and
their virtual counterparts

for crack detection, performance asse ssment, lifecycle management, and

accurate DT reconstruction and the information provided by inspections.
This gap necessitates manual intervention for DT updating

Fatigue damage in OSDs has been widely reported inﬁeld observations
and experimental studies. Typical cracking locations include welded joints
such as the deck-rib joint and diaphragm-rib joint31,a sw e l la so t h e rg e o -
metric discontinuities32, where local stress concentrations are pronounced33.
Crack initiation and propagation are commonly attributed to wheel-load-
induced local bending and secondary stresses, weld joint stress concentra-
tion, geometric stiffness discontinui ties, and interactions with welding-
induced residual stresses and fabrication imperfections
34. While extensive
work has been conducted on fatigue hotspot identi ﬁcation35,l o c a ld e t a i l
modeling36, and fatigue assessment of OSDs 37, practical in-service man-
agement is still challenged by limited inspection frequency and the difﬁculty
of continuously updating crack geometry and loading conditions in a
physics-based framework

To address these challenges, we propose and validate a closed-loop
fatigue evaluation system tailored for in-service steel bridges. The system
integrates three synergistic laye rs: autonomous damage acquisition,
dynamic DT updating, and decision-support interaction. Guided by fatigue
hot-spot statistics, the robotic inspector performs coverage-based inspec-
tions of fatigue-critical details, quantiﬁes cracks from the acquired images,
and outputs pose-referenced crack skeletons in the global structural frame.
These OBS cracks are embedded into high-ﬁdelity FEM models, coupled
with traf ﬁc load spectra to simulate crack propagation and predict
remaining fatigue life. Maintenance decisions are triggered based on the
predicted remaining life. Meanwhile , deviations between predicted and
observed crack morphologies are quan titatively analyzed to inform the
model, forming a closed adaptive loop that reﬁnes both fatigue assessment
and monitoring strategies.
Methods
System architecture
To evaluate the fatigue performance of in-service OSDs, a three-layer DT
framework was implemented, comprising: (1) damage acquisition through
robotic inspection, (2) digital-twin updating viaﬁnite-element simulations,
and (3) fatigue state assessment, as shown in Fig.1.T h ef o l l o w i n gs u b s e c -
tions present its quantitative validation from laboratory toﬁeld scale.
Damage acquisition (physical layer): an autonomous robotic platform
equipped with a manipulator and high-resolution camera navigates inside
the steel box girder along prede ﬁned paths. The robot acquires weld-line
images, performs real-time crack segmentation, and registers three-
dimensional crack skeletons ½x
i; yi; zi/C138 through multi-sensor localization,
thereby enabling automated, quantitative fatigue damage sensing.
DT updating (virtual layer): a high-ﬁdelityﬁnite element (FE) model of
the OSDs is constructed from as-builtgeometry. Crack skeletons extracted
from the physical layer are mapped into the model, followed by sub-model
partitioning and mesh reﬁnement around crack tips. Trafﬁc loading spectra
are applied, and fracture simulations are conducted to propagate cracks and
evaluate structural response in the damaged state. The model architecture is
open and extensible, allowing th e integration of additional in ﬂuencing
factors such as residual stresses or material heterogeneity.
Fatigue state assessment (interaction layer): by combining real fatigue
damage observations with DT crack predictions, the framework enables two
levels of decision support: (i) maint enance decision-making, where DT
crack evolution informs targeted repair or strengthening actions; and (ii)
feedback correction, where deviati ons between predicted and observed
crack paths are quanti ﬁed in terms of stress intensity factor differences,
guiding model calibration and the optimization of monitoring strategies.
This paper discusses three types of crack morphologies: (a) ground
truth (GT): manually measured labels used only for algorithm validation. (b)
observation baseline (OBS): geo-registered crack geometry obtained from
robotic inspection. (c) digital-twin predicted (DT): crack geometry pre-
dicted DT-based model.
Fatigue-informed inspection strategy
A clear understanding of actual fatigue crack distributions in OSDs requires
real-world data collection39. Given that more than 95% of fatigue cracks in
OSDs occur at the deck-rib and diaphragm-rib welded joints40,w ed e s i g n e d
the inspection strategy to target these fatigue-prone regions, as shown in
Fig. 2a. Accordingly, the inspection paths of the robot covered both deck-rib
weld lines and diaphragm-rib welds. Routes along adjacent U-ribs allowed
full coverage of deck-rib welds, while diaphragm paths provided access to
diaphragm-rib joints and also served as transition routes between ribs, as
s h o w ni nF i g .2b. Unlike conventional path planning that primarily mini-
mizes travel distance or time, weﬁrst deﬁne the fatigue-prone welded details,
and then design the inspec tion route under the robot ’s reachability con-
straints inside the closed box girder. The resulting route is therefore
coverage-driven rather than globallykinematics-optimal, and may be fur-
ther optimized in future work. This prioritization does not imply that other
crack types cannot occur; rather, deck-rib and diaphragm-rib welds are
targeted ﬁrst due to their dominance in observed cracking, and the same
inspection and localization pipeline can be extended to additional details by
adding corresponding target regions.
To implement the above strategy, we developed a magnetic-wheeled
robotic platform capable of auto nomous movement and crack image
acquisition within closed steel box girders, as shown in Fig.2c. The system
consists of ﬁve main modules: chassis, loco motion, localization, image
acquisition, and control/processing. The chassis was fabricated from 2 mm-
thick aluminum with front and rear guiding frames to facilitate wall-to-wall
transitions. The locomotion system employed six independently driven
NdFeB permanent-magnet wheels, providing stable adhesion and maneu-
verability on steel surfaces. Each wheel was powered by an individual motor,
enabling forward, backward, and turning motions.
The robot dimensions were optimizedto traverse both deck plates and
diaphragms. The localization module combined an inertial measurement
unit (IMU), lidar, and wheel encoders,while the imaging system integrated a
three-degree-of-freedom robotic arm carrying a high-resolution RGB
camera. Each joint of the robotic arm provided ﬁne angular control,
allowing the camera to orient toward weld lines at designated inspection
positions. The control and computation system included an STM32
microcontroller for motion control and a Raspberry Pi for image processing
and localization. Together, these modules enable autonomous execution of
the inspection route and automatic geo -tagging of the collected images/
videos using the estimated robot pose. Crack locations are not assumed to be

Fatigue crack quantiﬁcation method
Mapping crack pixels to global coordinates required calibration of the
robotic arm and onboard camera, as shown in Fig. 4a. First, the transfor-
mation from the robot base to the arm end-effector was solved using for-
ward kinematics based on Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) parameters
41.T h e
camera, rigidly mounted to the arm, was related to the end-effector through
a translation matrix. Next, Zhang ’s checkerboard method was used to
calibrate intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of the RGB camera 42,l i n k i n g
image pixel coordinates (u, v) to camera coordinates (xc, yc, zc). By further
applying rotation and translation m atrices, cracks in image space were
mapped into global structural coordinates. For the deck-rib welded joint, the
acquisition direction is constrained to be approximately perpendicular to
the weld. This constraint provides a consistent external reference axis (the
weld direction) to de ﬁne the image coordinate origin and thus localize
cracks, given the limited visual references at this detail. For diaphragm-rib
cracks, the captured images typically contain sufﬁcient reference features to
deﬁne the image origin reliably; therefore, the same perpendicular-to-weld
constraint is not required to determine crack coordinates.

DT model updating
The DT was updated by embedding observed cracks into a high-ﬁdelity FE
model of the OSDs. The model geometry was based on a half-span segment
of an in-service cable-stayed bridge, with boundary constraints applied at
the tower base and beam ends, as shown in Fig. 5a. Material properties
corresponded to Q345qD steel (elastic modulus 2.06 × 10^5 MPa, Poisson’s
ratio 0.3). Before crack insertion, the FE model uses a locally reﬁned mesh
around the weld region with an element size of 1 mm. After crack insertion,
FRANC3D automatically reﬁnes the mesh and performs local remeshing
around the crack front at each propagation increment, further reﬁning the
discretization in the vicinity of the crack front. The applicability of the
FRANC3D-based crack-growth simula t i o nf o rf a t i g u ec r a c k si nO S Dw e l -
ded joints has been validated in our previous work
43.
For thin-walled steel structures of OSDs, the deck-rib cracks initiate at
the weld root and extend to the weld surface44, and the diaphragm-rib cracks
are usually through-going cracks45. Therefore, the surface two-dimensional
cracks are reconstructed into three-dimensional cracks, as shown in Fig.5b.
We reconstructed crack surfaces from skeleton lines. For diaphragm-
through cracks, nodes were projectedto the opposite surface and connected
into a continuous crack face. The continuous crack face is a reconstructed
geometric representation obtained by interpolating the discrete crack-front
points extracted from inspection data. For deck-rib weld cracks, skeletons
were projected to the weld root and connected. FRANC3D was then used to
remesh crack regions with quarter-point wedge elements near crack tips and
brick/pyramid transitions for compatibility. This procedure enabled rea-
listic crack-insertion and propagation analysis.

Discussion
The present study establishes a quant itative framework to evaluate the
predictive accuracy of DT for fatigue cracks in in-service steel bridges by
integrating robotic inspection, high-ﬁdelity FEM, and FRANC3D-based
crack-propagation analysis. The reduced per-cycle time enables more fre-
quent, risk-informed inspections that can be integrated into the standard
inspection-assessment-maintenance workﬂow: crack geometry acquired at
fatigue-critical details is used to update the DT, prioritize follow-up
inspections and repairs, and tighten inspection intervals when rapid growth
is indicated. DT updating is current ly semi-automated, with inspection-
derived crack geometry imported via scripts and minor manual checks;
a ~ 10 mm propagation update typically requires 3-10 hours depending on
model size and computing resources. This closed-loop process shortens
detection latency and projects interval growth, reducing the risk of cracks
growing unnoticed between scheduled inspections. In the tested cases, the
DT reproduced crack morphologies wit h millimeter-level precision and
provided physically interpretable uncertainty indicators, though broader
validation across more bridges is still needed.
We found a strong link between morphological deviation and mixed-
mode fracture behavior. Importantly, this relationship is not used as a
correction mechanism for the model, but as a physics-based indicator of
where DT predictions lose reliability under mixed-mode dominance. For

In summary, the proposed morphology-SIF analysis quantitatively
links mixed-mode fracture mechanics to DT reliability, providing both a
diagnostic foundation and a pathway toward self-evaluating DTs for
infrastructure life-cycle management.
Future work
Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. The
ﬁeld validation was conducted on two representative cracks due to acces-
sibility constraints. Although the se cases encompass both Mode-I-
dominant and mixed-mode geometries, broader statistical validation will
require more datasets across different bridges and load environments.
Furthermore, the current DT assumes deterministic material parameters;
incorporating stochastic material variability and measurement noise will
improve uncertainty quantiﬁcation. Further automation and computational
acceleration will be pursued to reducethe update turnaround time. Beyond
the fracture-mechanics metrics usedhere, the updated DT can incorporate
additional health indices such as strain ranges, traf ﬁc spectra, global
vibration features, and deterioration indicators. Assimilating these data
would reduce model uncertainty and enable more robust risk-based
maintenance decisions, which we leave for future work. Future work
should couple this physics-based con ﬁdence metric with Bayesian
updating or data-assimilation frameworks, enabling real-time optimization
of inspection frequency and maintenance strategies for long-span steel
bridges.
