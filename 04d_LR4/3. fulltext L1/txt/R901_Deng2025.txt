ABSTRACT
Bridges are susceptible to severe damage from natural disasters, heavy traffic loads, and material degradation, 
necessitating timely and accurate information for effective emergency response. Current bridge disaster man -
agement systems often fail to meet real-time requirements due to interoperability challenges and fragmented 
functionalities across different phases. This paper systematically reviews 146 research articles on bridge disaster 
management, summarising the key challenges and potential disasters that bridges may face during their oper -
ational lifespan and highlighting the technological applications and decision-making requirements for effective 
disaster management. Key limitations in existing systems include inadequate technology integration, delayed 
response times, and insufficient coordination. To address these gaps, this paper proposes a Real-time Bridge 
Disaster Management (RtBDM) framework, which integrates advanced digital technologies to enable real-time 
monitoring, data analysis, and adaptive decision-making. The proposed framework offers practical solutions 
to enhance bridge resilience and safety during disasters and provides valuable insights for future research in this 
field.
1. Introduction
As critical components of transportation infrastructure, bridges are 
highly susceptible to damage due to external or internal factors such as 
natural disasters, heavy traffic loads, or material degradation, which can 
result in serious casualties and economic losses [ 1 ]. Historical disasters, 
including the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, the 2023 Pazarcik earth -
quake, and the 2020 Medicane Ianos, as well as the flood in the Western 
Black Sea Basin of Turkey in 2021, have severely disrupted regional 
highway transportation systems, highlighting the vulnerability of 
bridges [ 2 – 5 ]. From 1996 to 2017, typhoons caused 83 % of Taiwan ’ s 
bridges to collapse [ 6 ]. In India, the major cause of damage to bridges is 
natural disasters, with 80.30 %, of which hydraulic-induced causes 
51.19 %. Another major cause is the deterioration of materials (10.10 
%), followed by design and construction (4.13 %), overloading (3.28 %) 
and human factors at 2.19 % [ 7 ]. These incidents underscore the urgent 
need for comprehensive disaster management strategies for bridges, 
encompassing pre-disaster warning, emergency response, and post- 
disaster recovery [ 8 ].
Despite significant advancements in disaster prevention and 
mitigation technologies, such as satellites, drones, and sensor networks, 
their integration and implementation across different disaster manage -
ment phases of bridges remains fragmented. This fragmentation limits 
the acquisition, transmission, and management of real-time data, which 
is essential for informed decision-making during disasters. Current sys -
tems often fail to provide the real-time, dynamic information needed for 
effective disaster management, resulting in delays in response and re -
covery. Unlike other infrastructures, such as urban buildings or water 
networks, bridges face unique challenges in disaster management due to 
their critical role in maintaining connectivity [ 9 ]. For instance, flooding 
and earthquakes can compromise bridge stability and functionality, 
leading to severe consequences for rescue operations and regional 
mobility. These challenges highlight the need for a unified real-time 
disaster management system tailored specifically to bridge 
infrastructure.
The digital transformation of infrastructure management and the 
adoption of emerging technologies have opened new avenues for real- 
time disaster management [ 10 ]. Among these technologies, Building 
Information Modelling (BIM) and Digital Twin (DT) have demonstrated 
significant potential. BIM serves as a management platform for various

This paper aims to address these gaps by pursuing the following 
objectives: 
1) To identify and categorise the types of disasters that bridges may 
encounter during their operational lifespan.
2) To evaluate the applicability and limitations of current real-time 
disaster management technologies for bridges.
3) To propose a comprehensive Real-time Bridge Disaster Management 
(RtBDM) framework that integrates BIM and DT technologies for 
enhanced timeliness, coordination, and efficiency across all disaster 
phases.
The rapid development of information and communication tech -
nologies (ICT), such as the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence 
(AI), data analytics (DA), laser scanning (LS), machine learning (ML), 
and virtual and augmented reality (VR and AR), has made it possible to 
gather disaster-related data for real-time disaster assessment, emer -
gency response, and post-disaster recovery [ 13 – 16 ]. For instance, Ford 
and Wolf [ 15 ] developed a smart city model based on digital twins, 
enabling closed-loop management of community disasters and timely 
communication by integrating community-level data, images, and 
models. Similarly, Cao et al. [ 17 ] reviewed the integration of BIM and 
Geographic Information System (GIS) in urban disaster management, 
highlighting the potential for real-time data acquisition, interopera -
bility, and data utilisation. Cheng et al. [ 18 ] proposed a spatially linked 
continuous image (SLSI) method, which allows real-time 3D recon -
struction of disaster areas using optical data from UAVs, addressing the 
delays often associated with post-disaster image processing.
While these technologies have shown promise, most studies on 
bridge disaster management primarily focus on specific phases of the 
disaster cycle, often utilising bridge information systems, structural 
health monitoring (SHM) systems, or other inspection applications 
[ 19 – 22 ]. Most of this research emphasises either enhancing the perfor -
mance of bridges before a disaster occurs or facilitating rapid rescue and 
recovery efforts after a disaster. Bridges are load-bearing structures 
where minor damages can quickly escalate into critical failures under 
disasters. This highlights the importance of real-time, dynamic data 
integration to prevent potential collapses. Moreover, research on real- 
time disaster management has predominantly focused on urban envi -
ronments, with limited in-depth studies focusing specifically on bridges 
[ 10 ]. This points to a critical gap in the integration of advanced tech -
nologies within bridge disaster management, which is necessary to 
ensure comprehensive coverage across all phases of bridge disaster 
management — from real-time disaster warning and emergency response 
to disaster assessment and recovery.
Although BIM and DT technologies are commonly utilised during the 
design, construction, operation, and maintenance phases of bridges 
[ 23 – 25 ], some research has explored their role in assessing bridge 
disaster resilience [ 26 ]. For instance, Lin et al. [ 27 ] proposed a method 
for evaluating the collapse susceptibility of large-span cable-stayed 
bridges under seismic activity using DT models. Their findings demon -
strated that the DT approach closely aligns with experimental results, 
providing accurate predictions of earthquake intensity and collapse 
probability. These studies suggest that leveraging BIM and DT technol -
ogies can enhance the timeliness, coordination, and efficiency of bridge 
disaster management by unifying technology systems and improving the 
flow of critical information across all disaster phases.
By examining these gaps, this paper highlights the urgent need for a 
comprehensive real-time disaster management system for bridges. It 
reviews the types of disasters that bridges may encounter, summarises 
the current application status of real-time disaster management across 
different phases of the disaster cycle, and introduces the RtBDM 
framework. This framework aims to achieve technology interoperability 
and information sharing in bridge disaster management, facilitating 
iterative and adaptive management practices throughout all phases of 
the disaster cycle to ensure bridges are more resilient and responsive to 
disasters. The paper is structured as follows: Section 1 provides the 
introduction, followed by the overall research methodology in Section 2 . 
Section 3 categorises the types of bridge disasters and identifies the need 
for real-time disaster management. Section 4 summarises the applica -
tion of real-time disaster management across each disaster phase of 
bridges. The proposed RtBDM framework is discussed in Section 5 , and 
the conclusion is presented in Section 6 .
2. Methodology
In this paper, a systematic review methodology was employed, 
adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and 
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework, to critically evaluate and synthe -
sise existing literature on real-time bridge disaster management. The 
PRISMA framework provides a structured approach for conducting and 
reporting systematic reviews, ensuring transparency and reproducibility 
in the research process [ 28 ]. Data were systematically extracted and 
analysed from publications focusing on disaster types, enabling tech -
nologies, and application frameworks. The findings were synthesised to 
provide a holistic understanding of current advancements and gaps in 
the field, ultimately informing the development of a conceptual real- 
time bridge disaster management framework. The process of article 
retrieval and screening, as illustrated in Fig. 1 , further supports the 
systematic approach taken in this research. 
• Identification . The process of selecting articles for the systematic 
review on bridge disaster management began with a comprehensive 
search using the Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus as the 
target databases for literature search and analysis. For Web of Sci -
ence, the search utilised “ TS ” (Topic) as the main field, encompassing 
the subject, abstract, and keywords of the paper, while for Scopus, 
the equivalent field “ TITLE-ABS-KEY ” was employed. Advanced 
Boolean operators ( “ AND ” and “ OR ” ) were used to generate various 
keyword combinations, including “ Bridge ” and “ Disaster ” , “ Disaster 
management ” and “ Real-time ” , “ Digital Twin ” and “ Bridge ” , “ Digital 
model ” and “ Bridge ” , “ Bridge ” and “ Performance ” and “ Response ” .

In this paper, bridge-related disasters are classified into three main

Real-time disaster management provides a systematic framework for 
managing disasters and their consequences. It comprises four key pha -
ses: Pre-Disaster Warning, Real-Time Disaster Response, Immediate 
Post-Disaster Response, and Post-Disaster Recovery. Each phase lever -
ages advanced monitoring, data collection, analytical tools, and 
decision-making technologies to mitigate damage, enable emergency 
response, and support recovery efforts. The following subsections 
explore the types of disasters, their associated bridge defects, and the 
role of real-time disaster management in addressing them.
3.1. Natural disasters
Earthquakes represent one of the most significant natural disasters 
affecting bridges globally, characterised by their suddenness and 
destructiveness. In recent decades, frequent large earthquakes have 
caused severe damage to bridge structures, including tilting or collapse, 
damage to piers and abutments, foundation settlement or lateral shift, 
cracks or collapse of decks, and displacement of connecting parts. 
Among these, substructure damage is one of the most commonly re -
ported issues [ 31 , 32 ].
Seismic forces generated by earthquakes often result in shear or 
flexural cracks in piers and abutments due to horizontal ground accel -
eration [ 33 ]. Inadequate seismic joints may lead to bridge girder 
misalignment, while poor foundation stability can cause lateral shifts or 
settlements [ 34 ]. For instance, during the Wenchuan earthquake, 23.5 
% of bridges sustained moderate to severe damage or complete failure, 
with substructure damage being one of the most significant issues 
( Fig. 4 ). Monitoring these defects requires technologies such as accel -
erometers and tilt sensors to detect real-time displacement, as well as 
strain gauges for measuring stress concentrations at critical points. In 
real-time disaster management, earthquakes primarily require technol -
ogies for the real-time disaster response and immediate post-disaster 
response phases. During the real-time response phase, accelerometers 
and GPS systems monitor real-time structural displacement, enabling 
emergency managers to assess immediate risks and guide evacuation or 
reinforcement measures. In the immediate post-disaster response phase, 
tools like finite element modelling (FEM) and digital twins (DT) are 
employed to evaluate the residual strength of damaged components, 
simulate structural performance, and prioritise repair strategies.
Moreover, earthquakes often trigger cascading hazards, such as 
landslides, floods, or tsunamis, which exacerbate the initial damage. For 
example, landslides triggered by the Wenchuan earthquake caused 
additional foundation instability in many bridges. This highlights the 
importance of integrating multi-hazard monitoring systems that inte -
grate seismic, geotechnical, and structural data to account for both 
primary seismic events and secondary hazards.
Rapid urbanisation, global warming, and erratic climate change have 
dramatically increased flooding, which now accounts for one-third of 
the total economic losses from natural disasters [ 35 , 36 ]. During floods, 
bridges experience a progressive sequence of defects, including: (1) 
foundation scour caused by high-velocity water flows, which is the 
primary trigger for structural instability, (2) reinforcement corrosion 
accelerated by prolonged water exposure, (3) abutment erosion from 
sustained water pressure, and (4) deck damage from debris impact. 
These defects often occur progressively, with scour and corrosion 
creating conditions for more severe structural damage [ 37 ]. Scour is the 
leading cause of flood-induced bridge failure, as it undermines the sta -
bility of foundations, potentially leading to collapse. For example, Kim 
et al. [ 38 ] evaluated the vulnerability of bridges under floods by ana -
lysing failure modes such as reinforcement fracture and abutment 
instability. Monitoring these defects requires sonar imaging systems to 
assess scour depth and electrochemical sensors to detect early signs of 
reinforcement corrosion.
Floods emphasise the importance of the pre-disaster warning and 
post-disaster recovery phases in real-time disaster management. In the 
pre-disaster warning phase, real-time monitoring of rainfall intensity, 
water levels, and scour depth using IoT-based systems and sensor net -
works can provide predictive insights. For example, advanced multi- 
source data fusion systems integrate geological, meteorological, and 
bridge health data to inform managers about potential flood risks and 
enable proactive measures. During the post-disaster recovery phase, 
hydrodynamic models and BIM-DT integration are used to simulate 
foundation damage, assess long-term risks, and optimise rehabilitation 
plans. Despite advancements, gaps remain in integrating real-time 
monitoring into comprehensive flood models. Current research lacks 
systems that effectively combine real-time environmental and structural 
data for immediate risk assessment and response [ 39 – 41 ].
During hurricanes, bridges are exposed to concurrent hazards from 
strong winds, waves, and storm surges, posing major threats to the 
coastal transportation system. Typical damage includes deck misalign -
ment caused by wind uplift forces, displacement of girders under wave 
impact, and bearing damage caused by simultaneous wind and wave 
forces [ 42 ]. For example, Hurricane Katrina damaged nearly 45 bridges, 
primarily affecting the superstructure, including shifting or dislodging 
of main girders, damage to guardrails, and falling bearings [ 43 ]. 
Monitoring these defects requires wind tunnel simulations to predict 
wind-induced forces and displacement sensors to measure girder shifts.

ment across all four phases. In the pre-disaster phase, predictive ana -
lytics using wind tunnel simulations and weather data help anticipate

3.2. Human-caused disasters
Apart from natural disasters, bridges are affected by human-caused 
disasters such as traffic accidents, terrorist attacks, or fires. Traffic ac -
cidents primarily cause damage to the bridge members, induce vibration 
effects from vehicle impacts, support system damage, and ancillary fa -
cilities damage. For example, heavy vehicles colliding with bridge 
components can lead to localised structural damage, requiring strain 
sensors and impact detection systems to monitor and assess the effects. 
In real-time disaster management, traffic accidents are primarily 
addressed during the real-time disaster response phase, where UAVs, 
impact sensors, and mobile monitoring tools are deployed to assess 
immediate damage and guide emergency repairs. Current real-time 
monitoring systems for traffic-related damage remain underdeveloped, 
with most assessments relying on post-accident inspections.
Transportation infrastructure is also a common target of terrorist 
attacks; for instance, passenger trips decreased by 6 % in the two years 
following the 9/11 attack [ 46 ]. Terrorist attacks involving explosives 
often result in structural cracks, pier fractures, or loss of load-bearing 
capacity. Monitoring for terrorist-related damage requires blast sen -
sors and structural health monitoring (SHM) systems integrated with 
emergency response protocols. Terrorist attacks emphasise the need for 
real-time disaster response and post-disaster recovery phases. SHM 
systems provide critical data for assessing structural safety immediately 
after an attack, while BIM-DT integration supports long-term recovery 
planning. The ongoing process of urbanisation and industrialisation has 
resulted in the construction of more bridges, increasing the risk of such 
disasters.
Bridge fires are mainly caused by car accidents and gasoline com -
bustion rather than disasters such as earthquakes or ship collisions 
[ 47 , 48 ]. Additionally, the increased transportation of flammable mate -
rials has led to a subsequent increase in the frequency and severity of fire 
accidents, potentially leading to bridge collapse. The effects of fire- 
induced damage include loss of concrete strength, reinforcement 
degradation, and deformation of structural components, all of which 
significantly reduce load-bearing capacities [ 49 ]. For instance, the I-85 
bridge fire in Atlanta caused severe structural damage and prompted 
significant transportation disruptions. Common post-fire assessment 
methods include visual inspection and material strength testing, with 
various manuals and guidelines available for post-fire assessment and 
rehabilitation strategies [ 50–52 ]. However, real-time fire monitoring 
systems remain underdeveloped, with no standards for developing pre- 
fire prevention and emergency response plans for bridge fires. Fires 
highlight gaps in pre-disaster prevention and real-time response phases. 
Addressing these gaps is important for minimising fire-related damage 
and ensuring timely recovery. Current monitoring, warning, and 
assessment of bridges mainly focus on natural disasters and structural 
deterioration, leaving gaps in effective countermeasures for traffic ac -
cidents and relative damage. Discrepancies in standards and codes 
across different regions further complicate efforts to harmonise practical 
solutions.
3.3. Structural disasters
Structural disasters are mainly caused by design defects and material 
deterioration, both of which lead to reduced mechanical properties and 
disaster resistance [ 53 ]. Design defects can stem from irrational design, 
poor construction quality, or substandard material. For instance, insuf -
ficient reinforcement ratio or suboptimal concrete quality can lead to 
early-stage cracking under operational loads. Moreover, improper pier 
alignment during construction may result in long-term structural 
deformation [ 54 ]. During construction, structural imbalances can cause 
accidents, while in the operation and maintenance phase, bridges can 
suffer from cracks, deformation, and corrosion, potentially leading to 
partial or total collapse. Addressing damage caused by bridge structural 
design defects requires a comprehensive approach. BIM-based bridge 
management systems (BMS) provide decision support throughout the 
bridge’s lifecycle [ 12 ]. BIM technology facilitates collaborative design, 
conducts thorough structural safety inspections, simulates construction 
processes, and formulates response measures. Additionally, when com -
bined with advanced inspection technologies, BIM enhances the evalu -
ation and analysis of the bridge performance, thereby mitigating 
disasters caused by structural design defects and material deterioration 
[ 55 , 56 ].
Material deterioration manifests as a gradual decrease in bridge 
strength and stability due to factors like reinforcement corrosion or 
concrete ageing. Corrosion reduces the reinforcing steel’s area, weakens 
the bond strength between concrete and steel, and causes concrete 
spalling. These issues are often compounded in marine environments, 
where saltwater accelerates the deterioration process, resulting in pro -
gressive structural instability. This, in turn, exposes more steel to 
external environments, accelerating the deterioration process. If not 
maintained promptly, structural damage accumulates and intensifies, 
increasing vulnerability to extreme loading conditions [ 57 , 58 ]. 
Addressing bridge corrosion involves both prevention and reparative 
measures, including using corrosion-resistant materials and coatings 
during design and construction and repairing corrosion-damaged areas 
through coating repairs and reinforcement. Real-time monitoring sys -
tems, such as SHM integrated with DT models, can provide continuous 
updates on structural integrity, enabling early detection of material 
deterioration and preventing catastrophic failures. Table 2 illustrates the 
relationship between different disaster types and associated bridge 
damage. Understanding these disaster-defect relationships is critical for 
selecting appropriate sensing technologies and decision-making 
strategies.
3.4. Need for real-time disaster management
Disaster management involves managing the risks and consequences 
of disasters, typically divided into four phases: mitigation, preparedness, 
response, and recovery. This process includes activities such as risk 
assessment, pre-disaster preparedness, emergency response, rescue op -
erations, and post-disaster reconstruction [ 59 , 60 ]. Callaghan [ 61 ] 
highlights that the field of disaster management is a crucial area of 
development for achieving real-time capabilities, emphasising the 
importance of “real-time” in saving lives and reducing losses. In the 
context of bridges, real-time disaster management is particularly 
important for ensuring life safety, preserving bridge structures, and 
maintaining their functional use. This approach leverages advanced 
sensor technologies, communication networks, data analysis and pro -
cessing systems, and automated decision-making mechanisms to enable 
rapid and effective responses during disasters.

process under real-time disaster management is divided into four pha -

The pre-disaster warning phase consists of mitigation and preparedness. 
Mitigation refers to preventing a disaster or reducing the impacts should 
one occur. Preparedness is in terms of pre-disaster planning, training, 
and preparation. In the pre-disaster warning stage, real-time disaster 
management leverages advanced sensor networks and multi-source data 
fusion technology to integrate geological, meteorological, and bridge 
health monitoring data. This integration enables the real-time warning 
system to provide accurate predictive information before a disaster oc -
curs, aiding managers in taking preventive measures [ 62 ]. For real-time 
disaster response, the management emphasises the rapid acquisition and 
processing of disaster data to support real-time decision-making and 
emergency actions. Technologies such as drones, robots, and mobile 
monitoring equipment are employed to swiftly gather real-time data 
from disaster sites. Combined with BIM and DT technologies, this data 
facilitates dynamic analysis and evaluation of bridge structures, 
providing emergency decision support and temporary reinforcement 
solutions [ 63 ].
In the immediate post-disaster response phase, real-time disaster 
management focuses on detailed damage assessment and preliminary 
repair of bridges. Utilising damage detection technology and data 
analysis tools, it quickly assesses the extent of bridge damage and per -
formance status, subsequently developing preliminary repair plans [ 22 ]. 
During the post-disaster recovery phase, real-time disaster management 
emphasises long-term rehabilitation and functional recovery of bridges. 
Through virtual simulation and optimisation, it formulates a more sci -
entific and efficient long-term rehabilitation plan, ensuring that bridge 
performance levels are restored to pre-disaster conditions or higher 
[ 64 ].
Overall, real-time disaster management enhances the resilience and 
efficiency of bridges by implementing comprehensive and dynamic 
monitoring and management at all stages of a disaster. However, ac -
cording to existing research, further advancements are needed in tech -
nical tools related to data integration, real-time analysis, and decision 
support to achieve more efficient life cycle management. Existing real- 
time systems face challenges such as data integration from diverse 
sources, latency in decision-making, and the high costs of implementing 
advanced monitoring technologies, which must be addressed to ensure 
comprehensive disaster management solutions.

4. Technological review for bridge disaster management
The preceding discussion in Section 3 categorised disasters and their 
corresponding bridge damage, providing a foundational understanding 
of the scenarios in which specific technologies are applied. This section 
expands on those insights by aligning the disaster management phases 
with appropriate technological responses. Real-time disaster manage -
ment for bridges involves a systematic approach that spans all phases of 
the disaster management cycle, from pre-disaster warning to post- 
disaster recovery. This section is divided into four phases: Pre-Disaster 
Warning, focusing on early risk monitoring and predictive analytics; 
Real-Time Disaster Response, which ensures rapid data acquisition and 
emergency decision-making; Immediate Post-Disaster Response, 
emphasising damage assessment and initial repair strategies; and Post- 
Disaster Recovery, targeting rehabilitation and performance restora -
tions for bridges.
It should be noted that while disaster management encompasses four 
phases, not all scenarios fit neatly into all cycles. For instance, sudden- 
onset disasters like earthquakes often lack a significant pre-disaster 
warning phase, whereas long-term recovery tends to dominate in 
flood scenarios. By exploring the application of these advanced tech -
nologies, this section highlights their advantages in enhancing disaster 
management efficiency, evaluating how these technologies and strate -
gies align with the specific phases relevant to each disaster while 
acknowledging existing limitations, such as data integration challenges 
and gaps in real-time analysis capabilities.
4.1. Pre-disaster warning
Pre-disaster warning is the initial phase of the disaster management 
cycle in real-time disaster management, as illustrated in Fig. 5 . The 
phase involves the real-time collection and fusion of monitoring data 
from bridge structures and the surrounding environment, triggering 
alarms when abnormal signs or structural states are detected [ 65 ]. For 
this phase, bridge-specific defects such as scour, corrosion, and struc -
tural deformation caused by slow-developing hazards or predictable 
events (e.g., floods, hurricanes) are the primary targets for monitoring. 
However, sudden-onset disasters like earthquakes, fires, and traffic ac -
cidents often lack an effective pre-disaster warning phase due to their 
unpredictability.
Disaster warning consists of four components: (1) risk knowledge, 
(2) monitoring, (3) communication of warnings, and (4) response 
capability. Risk knowledge involves the systematic collection and 
analysis of historical and real-time data to understand potential hazards 
and vulnerabilities, forming the foundation for informed decision- 
making. Monitoring refers to the dynamic process of collecting real- 
time data and analysing it in the context of existing knowledge to pre -
dict potential risks and trigger timely interventions. Communication of 
warnings focuses on the effective dissemination of data, information, 
and insights related to early-warning situations, ensuring that relevant 
stakeholders are informed promptly and accurately. Finally, response 
capability emphasises the importance of preparedness and the ability to 
take swift, decisive action based on warning signals to mitigate potential 
impacts. These components interact synergistically to form an effective 
warning system [ 66 , 67 ]. The components are defined as shown in 
Table 3 .
Monitoring and predictive analytics are crucial for the pre-disaster 
warning phase, particularly for disasters such as earthquakes, floods, 
and hurricanes, which have varying levels of predictability. For 
example, for flood-induced defects, such as scour and reinforcement 
corrosion, are monitored using sonar imaging and electrochemical 
sensors, while machine learning (ML) models predict potential scour 
based on rainfall and water flow data [ 69 ]. However, disasters such as 
earthquakes, fires, traffic accidents, and terrorist attacks generally lack a 
distinct pre-disaster warning phase due to their sudden nature. Simi -
larly, material degradation represents a slow-developing hazard, and 
early warning focuses on long-term monitoring rather than immediate 
disaster prevention.
Advanced sensor networks and data fusion technologies are exten -
sively employed in bridge pre-disaster warnings. Sensor networks 
collect real-time data, including vibration, displacement, and strain, by 
deploying various sensors at critical points on bridges and transmitting 
this data through wireless networks for analysis [ 70 ]. For example, Jo 
et al. [ 71 ] demonstrated high-precision bridge displacement assess -
ments using relatively low-precision GPS sensors, while Hou et al. [ 72 ] 
designed an IoT-based sensor system for bridge displacement moni -
toring utilising NB-IoT technology. This system enables real-time data 
transmission, analysis, and remote visualisation through a web inter -
face. While IoT-based systems enhance data collection and transmission, 
challenges remain in ensuring data compatibility across heterogeneous 
sensor networks and minimising transmission delays in disaster sce -
narios [ 1 , 73 ]. These limitations highlight the need for robust data pro -
cessing protocols and reliable communication networks to ensure 
continuous monitoring.
SHM system provides real-time data through sensors and visual in -
spection, while BIM simulations of the bridge’s internal structure offer 
condition feedback and timely warnings before disasters [ 74 , 75 ]. Jeong 
et al. [ 76 ], for instance, integrated a BIM-based bridge information 
model (BrIM) with SHM to enhance bridge monitoring by utilising the 
OpenBrIM standard for data reception, storage, and analysis. However, 
the lack of standardised protocols for data exchange between BIM and 
SHM systems often results in integration inefficiencies. Moreover, BIM’s 
reliance on accurate historical data makes it less adaptable to sudden 
and dynamic disaster scenarios [ 19 ].
With the continuous inflow of data, it is essential to delve into the 
vast amounts of collected quantifiable information from the data layer. 
Data-driven analytics can significantly enhance the assessment of 
structural, geographical, and environmental conditions [ 77 ]. For 
example, predictive models based on ML and deep learning can identify 
potential disaster risks through real-time data analysis. Deng et al. [ 78 ] 
proposed a technique that automatically identifies concrete cracks using 
computer vision and machine learning, triggering pre-disaster warnings 
to prevent disasters due to structural damage.
External environment monitoring primarily utilises technologies 
such as wireless sensor networks (WSN), synthetic aperture radar 
interferometry (InSAR), Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), and sat -
ellite systems like GPS and Beidou. Lee et al. integrated [ 70 ] a WSN and 
smart building technologies to develop an IoT-based bridge safety in -
spection system for monitoring and analysing bridge and environmental 
conditions in real-time. Zhang et al. [ 79 ] developed a local wind envi -
ronment monitoring system for large-span bridges in mountainous 
areas, enabling accurate assessment of regional wind field parameters. 
These parameters provide a critical reference for the wind-resistant 
design of bridges. Similarly, Frigerio et al. [ 80 ] developed a web- 
based platform for continuous landslide monitoring that is capable of 
detecting displacements and triggering disaster alerts. Liu et al. [ 81 ] 
established a tsunami prediction system using global satellite posi -
tioning technology.

munications, and other fundamental technologies, DT technology has

Dan et al. [ 20 ] established a DT system for a regional transportation 
infrastructure network that uses measured traffic loads as a link to 
monitor working conditions and issue safety warnings for all bridges in 
the network. Kang et al. [ 23 ] proposed a DT-based bridge health 
monitoring method, synchronising physical and virtual spaces using 
sensor data to update digital models and simulate extreme conditions, 
ensuring bridge health. Despite advancements in monitoring, the 
implementation of digital Twins (DT) often demands substantial 
computational resources and dependable sensor data inputs, which may 
become constrained during large-scale disasters. Integrating DT with 
complementary technologies, such as AI and edge computing, enhances 
its adaptability and efficiency in pre-disaster warnings [ 82 ].
Early warning indicators and thresholds, based on historical data, 
norms, and theoretical analyses, are crucial. They help determine re -
sponses by comparing real-time monitoring data with established 
thresholds. These indicators must be sensitive to structural abnormal 
changes, and the appropriateness of warning thresholds directly affects 
the system’s ability to detect and signal issues [ 83 ]. For instance, Lat -
tanzi et al. [ 84 ] verified the strong correlation between the degree of 
damage to reinforced concrete piers and the pier top displacement by 
combining image segmentation and feature extraction with nonlinear 
regression analysis. They explored the use of computer vision and ma -
chine learning for quantitative assessment of bridge conditions. Simi -
larly, Yan et al. [ 85 ] employed drones with cameras and LiDAR scanners 
to automatically detect and quantify concrete cracks for structural 
assessment. The diversity of warning indicators results in various 
warning methods, which may cause public misunderstanding. Neußner 
[ 86 ] reviewed approximately 110 warning methods used in different 
countries and regions, suggesting that standardised warning signals, 
including colours, numbers, symbols, words, or simple messages, should 
be established.
The insights gained from pre-disaster warnings, such as real-time 
monitoring data and predictive analytics, are foundational for inform -
ing rapid decision-making during the real-time disaster response phase, 
as discussed in Section 4.2 . However, while current early warning 
technologies are effective in predicting and warning of potential disaster 
risks, limitations in data transmission and real-time analysis persist. 
Although BIM and DT technologies have improved pre-disaster warning 
accuracy and response speed by integrating historical and real-time data 
for dynamic simulation and prediction, a systematic real-time manage -
ment framework has not yet been developed [ 87 ]. With the increasing 
demand for accurate disaster prediction, it is crucial to mine the data to 
provide users with timely feedback for prediction and decision-making. 
Therefore, more attention should be paid to integrating BIM and DT with 
technologies like IoT and data mining to enhance real-time early 
warning, enabling closed-loop management and dynamic updating of 
pre-disaster warning information [ 88 , 89 ].
4.2. Real-time disaster response
The survival probability in a disaster area decreases significantly 
after 72 h, making this period critical for disaster response [ 90 , 91 ]. The 
real-time disaster response phase, defined as the 72 h following a 
disaster, forms a crucial component of real-time disaster management. It 
should be clarified that real-time disaster management for bridges em -
phasises the real-time management of the whole life cycle, focusing on 
ongoing monitoring and early warning during the operation and main -
tenance phases, as well as providing integrated emergency response and 
post-disaster recovery management. The goal is to ensure that managers 
can take action based on the most up-to-date analyses at any point in 
time. In contrast, the real-time nature of disaster response specifically 
addresses the immediate aftermath of an emergency event.
Bridge damage can severely disrupt transportation routes vital for 
rescue operations, medical evacuations, and supply chains. This un -
derscores the critical need for rapid and accurate bridge damage 
assessment, utilising methods specifically designed to address the 
dynamic and load-bearing characteristics of bridges under disaster 
conditions [ 21 ]. Historically, post-disaster bridge inspections relied 
heavily on manual investigations, which, although precise, are often 
time-intensive and costly. These traditional approaches fail to meet the 
urgent needs of real-time disaster response [ 92 ]. It should be noted that, 
unlike these sudden-onset disasters, material degradation lacks a real- 
time response phase, and its slow progression requires continuous 
monitoring rather than immediate. Modern technologies, such as sat -
ellites, drones, and rescue robots, have transformed this phase by 
generating vast amounts of real-time disaster-related data, significantly 
improving the speed and efficiency of assessments [ 93 ]. For instance, 
remote sensing technology combines sensors from satellites and ground- 
based platforms to establish a three-dimensional remote sensing 
network capable of acquiring real-time images to analyse the type and 
extent of structural damage [ 94 ]. In earthquake scenarios, high- 
frequency accelerometers and GPS systems monitor real-time pier 
displacement and girder oscillations [ 95 ]. For flood events, sonar im -
aging and LiDAR are employed to evaluate underwater scour and 
foundation erosion. These methods are particularly effective for large- 
scale regional assessments but may lack the resolution required to 
identify fine-scale structural damage in critical bridge components.
Several advanced technologies have been proposed to improve real- 
time disaster response tasks. For instance, Chen et al. [ 92 ] leveraged 
high-resolution optical remote sensing imagery to detect damage in 
water bridges, enhancing the timeliness and accuracy of post-disaster 
assessments. Similarly, Congress et al. [ 96 ] introduced a UAV-CRP 
(Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Combined with Close Range Photogram -
metry) technique for rapid, efficient, and repeatable evaluations of 
bridge damage. UAV-CRP excels in capturing detailed imagery of 
surface-level damage but is limited in assessing internal structural 
conditions, such as force reduction or material degradation. To address 
this limitation, Zhao et al. [ 97 ] developed a smartphone-based cable 
force measurement system that combines GPS data, structural images, 
and portable sensing capabilities, providing critical information for 
decision-making during real-time disaster responses. While these tools 
enable rapid data acquisition and initial damage assessments, they are 
limited by their reliance on discrete data acquisition and static analysis, 
which fail to capture dynamic changes in bridge conditions during a 
disaster.
The constraints of disaster environments necessitate the use of 
portable and easy-to-operate equipment by emergency rescue personnel. 
UAVs equipped with computer vision techniques have proven effective 
for real-time bridge inspections. Khallo et al. [ 98 ] used Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicle (UAV) and computer vision techniques to integrate a 
combination of UAV design, data capture, and data analysis to speed up 
bridge inspection without impeding access. However, the UAV-based 
method typically relies on discrete data acquisition and requires 
manual interpretation, which can delay the integration of findings into a 
comprehensive disaster response plan [ 99 ]. Bridges, serving as lifeline 
routes for post-disaster rescue and relief, are critical for transporting 
search and rescue personnel and medical teams and evacuating the 
injured from disaster areas. Chikahiro et al. [ 100 ] developed a new type 
of emergency mobile bridge, where the components are combined into 
an X-shape by a pivoted pin joint, allowing for rapid transport and the 
construction of a bridge.
These techniques facilitate the rapid acquisition of bridge damage 
data and conditions after a disaster, providing real-time images and 
structural status information. Data collected in these disaster scenarios, 
such as real-time displacement, vibration frequencies, and water flow 
velocities, which feed into Finite Element Models (FEM) for structural 
diagnostics and prognostics. However, they are limited in their ability to 
perceive dynamic changes in bridge status during a disaster. For 
instance, although UAV and smartphone-based techniques effectively 
facilitate immediate post-disaster assessments, they lack real-time 
feedback mechanisms to monitor evolving conditions, such as progres -
sive structural degradation caused by aftershocks or sustained loads

Recent advancements in real-time disaster response have notably 
improved the rapid assessment of bridge damage and data transmission. 
However, these efforts are predominantly confined to the emergency 
phases following a disaster, focusing on the urgent requirements to 
assess and implement emergency measures quickly [ 94 ]. This highlights 
a critical gap in system integration, where real-time data collected 
during pre-disaster monitoring and disaster response is not effectively 
linked to inform recovery strategies. Considering the four phases of real- 
time bridge disaster management, current research reveals significant 
limitations in system integration and information sharing across these 
stages. For instance, the weak linkage between pre-disaster warning 
data and real-time disaster response limits the ability to make informed 
decisions during emergency responses, resulting in a lack of support 
from preliminary data. Additionally, feedback and utilisation of real- 
time data are less considered in the post-disaster recovery phase, lead -
ing to a disconnect between long-term recovery strategies and imme -
diate disaster response.
DT technology-driven emergency response plans can infer the 
evolutionary trend of emergencies through knowledge retrieval based 
on the potential risks perceived in the pre-disaster warning phase [ 21 ]. 
These plans, based on the location of emergency resources, personnel 
deployment, and potentially hazardous areas, are visually presented for 
decision-making [ 103 ]. As disaster events unfold, resource information 
may become obsolete when shared. Utilising disaster information sys -
tems shared by various agencies and organisations, such as the Disaster 
Information Integration and Sharing System (DISIS), GIS, and Shared 
Information Management Platforms (SIMPs), can address this challenge, 
as shown in Table 4 . These disaster information systems are designed for 
distinct purposes yet collectively address the challenges of resource 
obsolescence and dynamic information needs during disasters. By 
enabling data integration, real-time analysis, and effective communi -
cation across agencies, these systems facilitate improved coordination 
and decision-making throughout the disaster management lifecycle. 
Based on this, BIM can serve as a starting point for DT, enriched by real- 
time data to provide accurate decision support for real-time bridge 
disaster response.
In conclusion, real-time disaster response necessitates the timely 
acquisition, integration, and analysis of data from multiple sources, 
including satellites, UAVs, sensors, and social media. While recent ad -
vancements have improved the speed and accuracy of assessments, 
challenges in system integration, data transmission, and real-time 
feedback mechanisms persist [ 106 , 107 ]. Future research should focus 
on developing intelligent management systems that integrate real-time 
data from diverse sources, enabling rapid execution of rescue and re -
lief programs. Enhancing the interoperability of technologies like BIM, 
DT, and disaster information systems will further strengthen the real- 
time response phase and its contribution to overall disaster 
management.
4.3. Immediate post-disaster response
The National Cooperative Highway Research Program (NCHRP) 
report [ 108 ] defines the period from day one to day seven after a disaster 
as the post-disaster response recovery period. Based on the definition of 
the real-time disaster response phase as the 72 h following a disaster, 
this paper defines the period from day four to day seven as the imme -
diate post-disaster response phase. Unlike the real-time response phase, 
which focuses on immediate structural safety, during the post-disaster 
response phase, the main tasks include a comprehensive damage 
assessment of the bridges, temporary transportation services, and pre -
liminary repair work to prevent secondary disasters. The real-time 
disaster response phase emphasises immediate response and pre -
liminary structural safety assessment, necessitating a less comprehen -
sive evaluation than the immediate post-disaster response phase. In 
contrast, the immediate post-disaster response phase focuses on detailed 
damage assessment and preliminary repair, requiring more extensive 
disaster data. This distinction between phases allows for a more sys -
tematic approach to addressing bridge damage, accounting for both 
immediate needs and long-term recovery.

detailed damage assessment of bridges after disasters. Various mea -
surement techniques, such as total station (TS), a differential global 
positioning system (dGPS), and photogrammetry, have been developed 
to rapidly acquire structural geometry and material property parameters 
[ 109–111 ]. TS and dGPS are suitable for small-scale and high-precision

components [ 112 ]. In hurricane scenarios, infrared thermal imaging is
