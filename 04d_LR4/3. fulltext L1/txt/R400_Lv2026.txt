ABSTRACT
Slope safety management is transitioning toward the digital twin paradigm, where digital models serve as a base 
plate for the development of further applications. This paper presents an automated solution to creating digital 
models for engineered slope digital twins. Utilizing city-scale point cloud, the proposed approach creates 
lightweight BIM models of engineered slopes consisting of drainage channels and slope topography. The pro -
posed approach leverages a convolutional neural network (CNN) model, DrainNet, for fine-scale drainage 
channel delineation in images, an automated parametric modeling pipeline for scalable BIM reconstruction, and 
a web-based digital twin for interactive visualization. Experiments demonstrate that DrainNet achieves state-of- 
the-art 81.26 % Dice with only 0.28 M parameters on a domain-knowledge-incorporated dataset containing 1226 
annotated images. The proposed automated workflow utilizing Grasshopper surpasses the Dynamo workflow in 
efficiency by 9 times. The BIM models have been integrated into a web-based digital twin platform, facilitating 
asset management and public engagement.
1. Introduction
Landslides are the most devastating natural hazards in mountainous 
cities like Hong Kong. Since the 1970s landslide mitigation program, 
over 60,000 engineered slopes have been registered in Hong Kong [ 1 , 2 ]. 
However, intensifying rainfall extremes due to climate change challenge 
the safety of these slopes [ 3 ]. Digital twin technology offers trans -
formative potential for landslide risk management by integrating multi- 
source data, physics-based simulations, and public engagement [ 4 – 7 ]. 
While existing digital twin implementations demonstrate slope stability 
analysis capabilities, they are primarily limited to specific sites where 
detailed models are available [ 8 , 9 ]. This contrasts with the current 
landslide management needs for detailed modeling of all slopes across 
urban areas [ 10 – 12 ]. Although Hong Kong mandates Building Infor -
mation Modeling (BIM) for new slopes [ 13 – 15 ], most existing slopes 
lack digital representations. A key barrier to digital twin implementation 
is the resource-intensive creation of accurate three-dimensional (3D) 
models for a large number of slopes.
This research addresses a critical component of the broader chal -
lenge: developing high level-of-detail (LOD) digital models of 
engineered slopes to enable large-scale digital twin implementation, a 
prerequisite for advanced landslide risk assessment. The process ne -
cessitates both up-to-date digital representations of engineered slopes 
and bidirectional real world-digital twin connectivity, requiring sus -
tained maintenance efforts [ 16 , 17 ]. Our study focuses on leveraging 
Hong Kong ’ s open geospatial data to construct foundational slope BIM 
models that align with engineering standards, serving as digital twin 
substrates for visualization and analysis. While prior research has 
automated digital twin creation for infrastructure systems including 
roads [ 17 , 18 ], bridges [ 19 ], and indoor pipelines [ 20 , 21 ], engineered 
slopes present unique modeling challenges due to irregular geometries 
and complex components. Notably, no existing studies demonstrate 
automated generation of foundational digital models for engineered 
slopes at city-wide risk management scales.
This research employs Hong Kong ’ s territory-wide Light Detection 
and Ranging (LiDAR) survey data with up to 0.1 m resolution for its 
superior 3D characterization capabilities compared to traditional im -
agery and photogrammetry. The modeling scope is first defined by 
identifying key engineered slope components that require digital twin 
representation. A typical engineered slope system comprises seven

both technical constraints and functional imperatives. Two critical fac -

geometry of a slope model for stability analysis and landslide runout

engineered slopes [ 23 ]. Evidence from Hong Kong government agencies

step. This approach enables phased integration of additional elements as 
resources permit while immediately addressing the most pressing needs 
of Hong Kong ’ s slope management amidst the digital transformation.
This paper proposes a framework for batch-oriented BIM generation 
of massive engineered slopes. The framework integrates domain-specific 
datasets, specialized deep learning networks, and fully automated 
parametric workflows for scalable BIM reconstruction. The resulting 
models are deployed on a digital twin platform with interactive visual -
ization capabilities, enabling effective risk communication.

2. Background
BIM reconstructions of engineering slopes using LiDAR point cloud 
data involve two critical phases: initial geometric parameter extraction 
of drainage channels through point cloud segmentation, followed by 
parametric BIM reconstruction using specialized software. This two- 
phase method ensures accurate digital representation of structural 
elements.
2.1. Related research
Accurately identifying object boundaries within point cloud data is 
typically achieved through two primary methods: (1) directly seg -
menting 3D point clouds, and (2) mapping 2D segmentation results into 
3D space [ 20 ]. Deep learning has emerged as a dominant approach for 
both 2D and 3D semantic segmentation, offering advanced capabilities 
for object recognition and boundary delineation. These segmentation 
results can then be utilized in parametric modeling methods to automate 
the reconstruction of BIM models.
2.1.1. 3D Point Clouds Segmentation
While pioneering 3D methods like PointNet [ 24 ] introduced 
permutation-invariant networks through multi-layer perceptrons and 
max pooling, subsequent advancements such as PointNet ++ [ 25 ] 
improved local feature capture through set abstraction. Modern archi -
tectures including PointWeb [ 26 ] and RandLA-Net [ 27 ] address com -
plex scene processing through context-aware aggregation and efficient 
sampling. Large-scale benchmarks like SemanticKITTI [ 28 ] and 
KITTI360 [ 29 ] have significantly advanced data-driven segmentation, 
with recent supervised learning approaches achieving notable success in 
multi-object segmentation for indoor and autonomous driving scenarios 
[ 30 , 31 ]. However, challenges persist in processing large-scale, un -
structured point clouds efficiently [ 21 ], particularly for extracting 
slender elements like drainage channels. The alternative 2D projection 
approach, which converts 3D data into 2D depth maps, leverages mature 
image segmentation techniques to significantly reduce computational 
loads compared with direct 3D processing.
2.1.2. 2D Image Segmentation
The evolution of 2D segmentation architectures has progressed 
through three distinct generations: (1) Fully Convolutional Networks 
(FCNs) [ 32 ] or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [ 33 – 38 ] excel -
ling in texture pattern recognition, (2) Vision Transformers (ViTs) 
[ 39 , 40 ] that capture long-range dependencies, and (3) foundation 
models like Segment Anything Model (SAM) [ 41 , 42 ] that enables zero- 
shot adaptation for downstream tasks. These methods have been widely 
applied in tasks related to construction and infrastructure maintenance, 
such as crack segmentation [ 43 , 44 ], road network segmentation [ 45 , 46 ] 
and in-door pipeline segmentation [ 20 , 21 ]. To preserve the connectivity 
of linear objects, some studies focus on refining imperfect road seg -
mentation results to maintain their inherent connectivity [ 47 ]. Notably, 
most existing models are trained on datasets, such as ImageNet [ 48 ] for 
general vision tasks and Chest X-ray8 [ 49 ] for medical imaging, which 
significantly differ from point cloud-derived imagery.
2.1.3. Parametric Modeling
Grasshopper and Dynamo have become pivotal tools in parametric 
design and cross-platform collaboration within engineering sectors. 
Their integration of Visual Programming Languages (VPLs) enables 
automated workflows and complex geometric manipulation [ 50 – 54 ]. 
These tools seamlessly connect conceptual design with technical 
execution through computational logic, supporting applications from 
generative design to data-driven optimization. For instance, Grass -
hopper facilitates AI-enhanced workflows, such as converting 2D 
sketches into 3D structural models via deep learning [ 51 ], while Dy -
namo streamlines BIM data interoperability, enabling efficient data 
transfer between Revit and external platforms like Excel for documen -
tation [ 54 ]. Their adaptability extends to advanced computational sce -
narios, including LiDAR-based point cloud processing for scan to BIM 
[ 50 , 53 , 55 ] and reinforcement learning-driven spatial optimization [ 52 ]. 
Custom workflows are necessary to address slope-specific challenges, 
such as the integration of irregular geometries and domain-specific 
analysis.
2.2. Research gaps
Despite advancements in segmentation and parametric modeling, 
significant gaps remain in addressing the unique challenges of batch- 
oriented BIM reconstruction for engineered slopes. First, computa -
tional bottlenecks persist in processing city-scale LiDAR point clouds, 
where conventional 3D segmentation models struggle to efficiently 
extract narrow elements like drainage channels from millions of un -
structured points. Although 2D projection methods reduce

2.3. Research objectives
To bridge the aforementioned research gaps, this paper proposes a 
framework for batch-oriented BIM generation of engineered slopes. This 
study aims to: 
1. Establish a domain knowledge incorporated dataset comprising 
labeled images of surface drainage channels on engineered slopes.
2. Develop a specialized deep learning model tailored for slope surface 
drainage channel segmentation with high efficiency and low 
computational cost.
3. Design a fully automated parametric workflow to enable efficient 
and scalable reconstruction of lightweight BIM models that enable 
scalable digital twin deployment.
3. Methodology
The proposed framework for digitizing massive engineered slopes 
comprises four principal components ( Fig. 2 ): (1) domain knowledge- 
incorporated image dataset generation, (2) deep learning-based image 
segmentation, (3) Grasshopper-based fully automated BIM reconstruc -
tion, and (4) digital twin platform interactive visualization.
This study employs an image-based deep learning method to delin -
eate slope surface drainage channels. The initial phase involves gener -
ating depth images from LiDAR point clouds, incorporating domain 
knowledge to optimally distinguish the characteristics of slope surface 
drainage channels. Slope surface drainage channels are then delineated 
manually as labels for segmentation.
The segmentation phase implements customized CNN model Drain -
Net to address the challenge of detecting local linear features while 
preserving global contextual awareness. DrainNet integrates directional 
convolution blocks (horizontal and vertical) for local feature extraction, 
dilated convolution blocks for expanded receptive fields, and channel 
attention modules for better generalization on small datasets. DrainNet 
achieves optimal performance in linear structure identification, partic -
ularly for slope surface drainage channel delineation.
The BIM reconstruction phase establishes an automated workflow 
combining Digital Elevation Model (DEM) data with projected 3D 
channel mappings. A Rhino-Grasshopper visual programming pipeline 
processes pre-conditioned topography and channel data through 
customized Python and C# scripts, incorporating parametric channel 
cross-sections to generate lightweight BIM models.
The final integration phase implements a web-based city-scale digital 
twin through Vue.js frontend interfacing with Cesium and Google Earth 
as base maps. Georeferenced BIM models are superimposed on Hong 
Kong ’ s photogrammetric base maps, enabling interactive 3D visualiza -
tion with attribute query functionalities for enhanced slope safety 
management and decision support.
4. Slope Drainage Channel feature dataset generation
3D point cloud data serves as semantic-less raw input for this 
research. It can be directly converted to 2D DEM rasters to enable deep 
learning-based image segmentation. In this section, relevant features are 
extracted from the DEM for drainage channel segmentation, further 
forming a labeled dataset for deep learning.

hand. As depicted in Fig. 3 , the majority of drainage channels on engi -

5. Deep learning-based image segmentation
A novel CNN model is proposed tailored for segmentation of 
drainage channels. Additionally, parametric studies are performed on 
the proposed model to evaluate the contribution of its individual com -
ponents and validate its overall design.
5.1. Model design
As depicted in Fig. 5 , drainage channels on slopes typically exhibit a 
narrow structure along their axis in imaging contexts, leading to a highly 
sparse two-dimensional matrix representation. Only a limited number of 
pixels perpendicular to the axis are relevant for segmentation. The 
connectivity of drainage channels indicates that if the pixels at both ends 
are positive samples, the intermediate pixels are also positive, implying 
that these intermediate pixels can be omitted without affecting the 
segmentation.
Drawing inspiration from these observations and utilizing 1D con -
volutions instead of 2D convolutions to simplify model complexity, as 
implemented in Inception V3 [ 57 ], a CNN model named Drainage 
channel segmentation Network (DrainNet) is proposed for enhanced 
recognition accuracy under limited dataset conditions. As illustrated in 
Fig. 6 , The model begins with a head block that extracts features from 
input curvature data. These features are then processed through direc -
tional blocks, which employ two perpendicular 1D convolutional layers 
to focus on the sparse matrix representation of drainage channels, 
replacing traditional 2D convolutions. Channel attention mechanisms 
integrate the feature maps from these 1D layers. Next, dilated blocks are 
introduced incorporating dilated convolutions, enabling rapid expan -
sion of the receptive field without increasing parameters. The head, 
directional, and dilated block form the encoder of the network, which 
will be detailed in the following section. Finally, an output projection 
block acts as the decoder, restoring the spatial resolution of feature maps 
to their original size and producing the final single-channel output.

straints with lightweight networks like MobileNetV2-Lite and HarDNet- 
Lite, DrainNet delivers far superior performance, even surpassing larger 
CNN architectures. Its lightweight design and efficient feature aggre -
gation mechanisms likely contribute to robust generalization. Among 
the Transformer-based models, SegFormer(b0) achieves the best per -
formance, with an IoU of 62.1 %, a Dice score of 69.0 % and a SCE of 
0.54, while requiring only 1.30 G MACs and 3.71 M parameters. This 
highlights its parameter efficiency compared to larger variants like 
SegFormer(b5) (84.59 M #Params, 19.12 G MACs). However, their 
performance remains suboptimal compared to CNN-based counterparts, 
particularly in scenarios with limited training data. The lack of spatially 
localized feature extraction mechanisms potentially hinders their 
adaptability to pixel-level segmentation tasks. This aligns with prior 
observations that the pure Transformer architecture requires extensive 
pretraining or large-scale datasets to achieve competitive results.
Fig. 8 shows the segmentation results of the best transformer-based 
model, our model, and 5 other CNN-based models on the test set. 
DrainNet demonstrates superior performance in segmentation tasks by 
achieving better completeness and connectivity compared to other 
models. For the Transformer based model like SegFormer, its reliance on 
global self-attention often leads to blurred boundaries in dense predic -
tion tasks. The simplified architecture of DrainNet, leveraging fewer 
parameters and stronger inductive biases through directional convolu -
tions and channel attention, ensures better generalization on small 
datasets than other CNN models. This is particularly useful for tasks like 
drainage channel segmentation, where structures are often elongated 
and have strong spatial orientations.
5.4. Ablation study
To validate the design choices of DrainNet, ablation experiments are 
conducted on two critical aspects: the model configurations in the

the best metrics while maintaining minimal computational cost. 
Expanding the encoder to (16, 32, 64, 128) or (16, 32, 64, 128, 256) 
results in increased MACs and parameters, yet the performance degrades 
slightly. Deeper architecture introduces redundant feature interactions, 
which amplify noise in shallow layers and reduce the model ’ s ability to 
preserve fine-grained spatial details. Moreover, with limited training 
data, deeper networks struggle to generalize.
Table 4 details the impact of the channel attention module. Its in -

effectively enhances the model ’ s focus on salient channel features and 
improves the structural coherence of the segmentation.

providing physically meaningful priors that align with the drainage

less relevant signals, such as non-channel terrain fluctuations, which 
dilute the model ’ s focus on channel-specific patterns.

6. BIM reconstruction and visualization
Using the proposed deep learning model, the 2D distribution of the 
drainage channels on slope surfaces can be readily obtained. In this 
section, the 2D channel distribution and topography data will be 
incorporated into an automated slope BIM model reconstruction work -
flow to support digital twin visualization and interaction.
6.1. Data Preprocessing
The aim is to construct engineered slope BIM models with detailed 
drainage channels that adhere to the slope surfaces. In order to handle 
massive slopes, Grasshopper is used as a parametric modeling tool to 
automate the BIM reconstruction. The data is converted to certain for -
mats compatible with Grasshopper.
6.1.1. Mapping of segmentation results
The 2D binary segmentation results generated by DrainNet lack 
geographical information. To accurately place drainage channels on the 
terrain in Grasshopper, a series of preprocessing steps are applied, as 
illustrated in Fig. 9 .
The original segmentation results are 448 × 448 binary images 
representing portions of slopes. These images are stitched together to 
create a complete segmentation map for a slope. Irrelevant small regions 
or artifacts are removed based on an area threshold. Using OpenCV, the 
contours of continuous positive pixels were extracted, and the ratio of 
the contour area to the area of its minimum enclosing circle was 
calculated. This ratio, empirically determined to range between 0.18 
and 0.20, was used to filter non-channel-like noises. A morphological 
closing operation using OpenCV can be performed to bridge small gaps, 
using a small kernel size of 3 pixels (0.3 m) to bridge gaps without 
significantly altering shapes. Alignment with the built environment is 
verified through overlay on DEM data and visual inspections, though 
complete validation requires refined ground truth data. The geographic 
information from the corresponding high-resolution DEM data is copied 
to the processed binary map. Then binary map is transformed into a 
polygon shapefile, a widely-used format in Geographic Information 
System (GIS). The central lines of the drainage channels and vertices of 
the central lines are extracted. The central lines are divided into indi -
vidual segments using an intersection-based algorithm to ensure 
compatibility with Grasshopper. The pseudocode for this algorithm, 
detailed in Table A1 in Appendix, identifies vertices where randomly 
created buffer circles intersect with the surrounding lines more than 
twice. The resulting segmented line shapefile and DEM coordinates are 
imported into Grasshopper for 3D terrain modeling and channel 
placement.
6.1.2. Drainage Channel cross-section Modeling
The cross-section of drainage channels follow the guidance in the 
Hong Kong Slope Information System (SIS) [ 63 ], which records the size 
of drainage channel at different positions of the slope (crest, toe, berm, 
on slope). The size of these drainage channels varies from 0.2 to 0.4 m. 
Since the SIS only provides the outer diameter and does not detail in -
ternal parameters or cross-sectional profiles, a representative size of 0.3 
m is adopted for modeling purposes. The parametric section is defined 
by three parameters: pipe thickness (a = 0.06 m), inner diameter (b =
0.24 m), and total depth (c = 0.3 m), which are empirically determined 
and applied uniformly for all drainage channels in this research. As 
shown in Fig. 10 , the parametric channel cross-section is defined in 
Rhino using the world origin (0,0,0) as the reference point and the x-y 
plane as the working plane. A clip section is also created to facilitate 
Boolean operations, enabling terrain excavation in areas occupied by the 
channel. The spatial relationships among the world origin, channel 
cross-section, and clipper cross-section are illustrated in the figure. Both 
the channel and clipper cross-sections are saved as separate Rhino model 
files, allowing modular integration into the workflow through direct file 
referencing.
6.2. Workflow design
A task-specific Grasshopper workflow that integrates both commonly 
available tool nodes and custom-designed code blocks is developed, 
enabling efficient and automated batch modeling. Fig. 11 illustrates the 
prepared workflow script. In this workflow, pre-processed DEM coor -
dinate data (describing the terrain) and shapefile data (describing the 
drainage channel distribution) are fed into a custom-designed Data 
Import Module (see Table A2 in Appendix), which parses and matches 
the data based on slope IDs. The module outputs the file paths for terrain 
coordinate CSV files and drainage channel shapefiles, the number of 
slopes to be modeled, and the model IDs. Next, the Topography Read 
Module resamples and smooths the terrain points and converts them into 
a mesh object. Simultaneously, the Channel Read Module passes the 
shapefile data to the Projection Module to transfer the 2D channel 
central lines into 3D curve objects. The Sweep Plane Relocate Module 
then aligns the sweep operation ’ s working plane to the starting point of 
each curve. The Initial Plane Read Module retrieves the origin specified 
during the creation of the channel cross-section and clipper cross- 
section, which is later used to define the Initial Plane. In the Channel 
Creation Module, the channel cross-section is first aligned from the 
Initial Plane to the Sweep Plane, and then a sweep operation is per -
formed along the curve to generate the complete 3D channel geometry. 
The Mesh Join Module combines the individually created channel 
models into a unified mesh model. Simultaneously, the Clipper Mesh 
Creation Module generates a clipping mesh using the same approach, 
which will be used to modify the terrain.
In the Topography Clip Module, a custom-designed code (see 
Table A3 in Appendix) is utilized for batch terrain clipping, removing 
intersecting or overlapping portions of the terrain mesh with the channel 
geometry. Since Grasshopper models cannot be directly exported, the 
completed channel and terrain models are baked into Rhino ’ s workspace 
using the Bake Module (see Table A4 in Appendix). This module assigns 
different material properties to the models by specifying layer names. 
The GUIDs of baked models are then passed into the Model Export 
Module (see Table A5 in Appendix), where the models are combined into 
a single 3DM file. The output file is named based on the model IDs 
generated by the Data Import Module. Additionally, the Model Export 
Module outputs a numerical value in the range of [0,1] to control the 
step size in the Iteration Module and a Boolean value to indicate whether

terrain clipping and model export. By contrast, 136 s is needed for Dy -
namo to process the same task. Further more, Grasshopper successfully 
modeled all 38 separate channels on the slope, including terrain clipping 
and final BIM model export, in approximately 47 s. The gain primarily 
stems from Grasshopper ’ s multi-channel workflow, which enables par -
allel processing of tasks such as terrain clipping and BIM model export, 
distributing computational loads across channels for concurrent execu -
tion. Removing the BIM model export step while retaining terrain clip -
ping reduces the execution time to 32 s, and bypassing both terrain 
clipping and model export further reduces the time to 22 s. Even when 
modeling complexity increases, Grasshopper maintains a significant 
speed advantage over Dynamo. Specifically, for the most basic work -
flow, Grasshopper is approximately nine times faster than Dynamo. The 
experiment highlights Grasshopper ’ s superior computational efficiency 
and scalability in parametric BIM workflows, especially when leveraging 
parallelism for multi-channel operations. This efficiency is primarily 
attributed to Grasshopper ’ s optimized computational kernel and direct 
geometric processing, in contrast to Dynamo ’ s heavier integration with 
Revit ’ s database and API, which introduces additional computational 
overhead.

7.1. Analysis of failure cases in Drainage Channel delineation
As illustrated in Fig. 14 , failure cases in image segmentation fall into 
two main categories: overestimation and underestimation. Both occur in 
areas where the point cloud data is sparse or vacant, leading to unex -
pected topographic variations that disrupt curvature-based analysis. 
This results in either false positives resembling drainage channels or 
large blanks in the image. From a model design perspective, such 
sparsity can amplify noise in feature extraction, causing the network to 
misinterpret subtle edges or fail to connect fragmented structures.
To address these challenges, a twofold strategy is adopted. First, 
during dataset annotation, incomplete or discontinuous drainage 
channels are manually labeled as continuous structures to help the 
model recognize gaps due to data sparsity ( Fig. 14 ). Second, DrainNet 
with perpendicular 1D convolutions and channel attention is developed 
to better capture sparse drainage channel features and improve 
robustness against incomplete data. Nevertheless, data completeness 
remains crucial, as it fundamentally affects segmentation accuracy and 
subsequent modeling.
7.2. Limitations and future work
The method proposed in this research is suitable for drainage chan -
nels on slopes that are exposed and uncovered, which can be detected 
through surface curvature analysis and subsequently segmented using 
image-based methods. The available data from SIS provides only 
approximate cross-sectional dimensions, thus empirically determined 
parameters are applied uniformly for modeling purpose. Using the 
parametric modeling method, the creation of channel section can be 
easily generalized when detailed parameters are available. The current 
segmentation model has limitations in accurately resolving channel 
width parameters. Alternatively, channel geometry could be derived by 
converting deep learning segmentation results into shapefiles, though 
this approach requires further research. Notably, the application of 
closure operations for maintaining hydraulic connectivity is a simplified 
way. This post-processing step, while effective, is a heuristic simplifi -
cation that operates under the assumption that all discontinuities are 
segmentation errors. Consequently, it may erroneously connect chan -
nels that are geomorphologically distinct or introduce non-physical al -
terations to the channel ’ s shape. The performance of this operation is 
also contingent upon a relatively complete initial segmentation. Future 
work should explore more sophisticated approach to establish connec -
tivity, which would more robustly handle complex drainage networks 
and reduce the reliance on such geometric heuristics.
Furthermore, the current static digital representations of slopes will
