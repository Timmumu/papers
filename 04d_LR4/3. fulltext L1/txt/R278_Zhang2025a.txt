Digital Safety Navigation for Tower Crane Climbing
Process from a Systematic Perspective: Risk
Identification, Safety Information Requirements,
and Related Intelligent Platform
Chong Zhang 1; Wei Zhang 2; Yudong Shi 3; Tingsheng Zhao 4; and Yaoting Zhang 5
Abstract: The tower crane climbing process is one of the most hazardous operations due to its complexity and technical challenges, making
it difficult to ensure both structural and worker safety. To address this issue, this study proposes a systematic control framework based on
digital navigation. This framework integrates theoretical risk analysis, safety information requirements, and the practical implementation of a
safety intelligent navigation platform (SINP). Key safety-critical points in the tower crane climbing process are identified through process
analysis, and worker situation awareness requirements are derived using goal-directed task analysis (GDTA). The SINP is designed to dig-
itally capture safety-related data, assess safety conditions intelligently, and provide automated safety guidance. The platform enables sys-
tematic control of tower crane safety through three key functions: (1) real-time navigation and alarms for workers via an edge intelligent
control device (EICD); (2) synchronous monitoring and interaction for on-site supervisors using mobile devices; and (3) remote inspections
for management through cloud-based platforms. Two experimental scenarios were conducted to simulate real-world engineering conditions
and evaluate the framework ’s effectiveness. Results show a significant improvement in worker performance and process safety, which are
attributed to the advantages of SINP , which include (1) timely and efficient information sharing; (2) accurate and orderly safety guidance;
(3) real-time and effective alarms; and (4) positive psychological reinforcement for workers. This study introduces a proactive safety man-
agement approach that can prevent accidents at their source and enhance worker performance throughout the tower crane climbing process.

Introduction
The construction industry serves as the backbone of the national
economy and plays a critical role in the long-term development of
both society and the economy. In 2022, the industry ’s gross domes-
tic product (GDP) reached 8.3 trillion yuan, contributing 6.9% to
China’s overall GDP , marking a 5.9% increase compared to 2021
(National Bureau of Statistics 2022 ). The workforce in this sector
totaled 18.35 million, representing 11.0% of the country ’s total
labor force. By the end of 2022, the number of special equipment
units had reached 19.55 million, including 2.79 million units of lift-
ing machinery, accounting for 14.3% ( Chinese SMAR 2023 ). As
shown in Fig. 1, 108 safety incidents related to special equipment
were reported in 2022, with 25 involving cranes, representing
23.1% of the total. These crane-related accidents led to 101 fatal-
ities, of which 25 (22.8%) were directly linked to crane operations,
predominantly in the construction sector. These statistics underscore
the persistent and serious safety challenges associated with crane
operations in the construction industry.
Tower cranes are widely used in construction for lifting materi-
als due to their adjustable height and powerful lifting capacity ( Wu
et al. 2020). However, accidents involving tower cranes remain fre-
quent and severe due to their unstable structural characteristics and
the inherent risks of their operational tasks. Notably, the installation
(including assembly and climbing) and dismantling of tower cranes
are particularly high-risk activities, often leading to fatalities (Zhang
and Zhang 2019). According to the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA), crane accidents resulted in over 400 non-
fatal injuries in 2020, with an average of 33 fatalities annually over
the past decade ( US BLS 2023 ). In Korea, 245 fatal tower crane
accidents were reported between 2007 and 2016, with human error
and crane malfunctions identified as the primary causes ( Lee et al.
2022). Additionally, incomplete statistics on tower crane accidents in
China from 2010 to 2019 indicate that 39.8% of the 344 reported
incidents occurred during installation or dismantling, with 47 of
these taking place during the climbing process. As construction

Related Work
Tower Crane –Related Safety Management
Tower cranes play a crucial role in construction projects, serving as
indispensable tools for lifting building materials. Research on tower
crane safety has spanned decades, primarily focusing on two key
areas: the identification and assessment of safety risks and the im-
plementation of monitoring measures to ensure safety.
Two primary methods are commonly used to identify and assess
tower crane risks: statistical analysis and interview studies. Through
analyzing large volumes of accident data, researchers have found
that complex procedures and poor worker performance are the main
contributors to high risks, particularly during the installation phase
(Aneziris et al. 2008 ; Beavers et al. 2006 ; Swuste 2013). This con-
clusion is supported by several studies on tower crane collapses,
where worker errors in understanding crane status and executing
operations were frequent causes of failure ( Frendo 2013; Marquez
et al. 2014 ; McDonald et al. 2011 ; Zrnic et al. 2011 ). Additionally,
a quantitative analysis of 212 incidents confirmed that the most haz-
ardous factors are technical and human errors (Raviv et al. 2017a, b).
In fact, 148 operator errors were identified, with risks quantified
accordingly (Borgheipour et al. 2020). Interviews and surveys have
further highlighted and ranked the factors influencing tower crane
safety. These include inadequate worker training, complex proce-
dures, deteriorating equipment, insufficient supervision, and poor
working conditions ( Kim and Singhose 2010 ; Sertyesilisik et al.
2010; Sharma et al. 2019 ). It is widely acknowledged that these is-
sues play a major role in incidents occurring during installation and
dismantling ( Shin 2015 ; Tam and Fung 2011 ). Moreover, several
studies have explored broader factors contributing to tower crane
accidents, with findings largely consistent with earlier research
(Zhang et al. 2020 , 2021; Zhou et al. 2018 ).
In terms of monitoring measures, passive monitoring is widely
used to gather information on the status of hooks, lifting capacity,
and limiting devices, with extensions to static monitoring of struc-
tural safety indicators such as stress, displacement, and wind speed.
Condition monitoring systems provide detailed real-time data on
factors like lifting moment, swing amplitude, and lifting weight,
using sensing, signal processing, and wireless technologies
(Zhang et al. 2013 ). Sensor data, including lifting weight, boom
inclination, rotation angle, and wind speed, are captured and used
in real-time FEM analysis to assess the structural safety of the tower
crane ( Guanghui et al. 2020 ). Key parameters monitored for
structural safety include stress, deformation, temperature, wind load,
and acceleration (Chong et al. 2021 ; Zhang et al. 2021 ). Structural
stress, verticality, and wind speed are compared against predefined
thresholds to determine warning levels ( W e ie ta l .2 0 2 1). By
monitoring hook height, trolley position, and jib angle, visual
monitoring systems can analyze driver behavior through a scoring
mechanism to improve operational safety ( Yannian et al. 2022 ).
Additionally, this monitoring data can be utilized for model analysis
and simulation of crane operations and accidents. The safety level of
tower cranes at individual sites can be predicted using an integrative
model that analyzes the impact of various overall safety indices
(Shapira et al. 2012). Additionally, a multiuser virtual safety training
system has been developed to simulate real-world conditions for
workers involved in tower crane dismantling, offering a practical
and immersive learning environment ( Li et al. 2012 ).
Machine learning (ML) has been applied to safety hazard iden-
tification, error detection, and the recognition of unsafe behaviors.
A smart packaging method was proposed to enable adaptive
decision-making and path replanning in dynamic crane operation
environments ( Li et al. 2020 ). An audio-based safety recognition
system was also developed to continuously monitor and manage
safety risks by detecting abnormal events ( Lee et al. 2020 ). Addi-
tionally, a deep learning algorithm has been used to predict oper-
ator fatigue during crane operations ( Liu et al. 2021 ). Further
studies have expanded these efforts ( Jiang and Ding 2024 ; Wu
et al. 2022 ). With advancements in computing and storage capa-
bilities, there has been increasing integration of traditional meth-
ods with emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI),
blockchain, and digital twin (DT). A blockchain-based framework
was developed to enhance the effectiveness of on-site inspections
of tower cranes ( Wu et al. 2022 ). Using DT technology, a general
framework was proposed to analyze unsafe behaviors and improve
human-machine interactions in construction activities ( Jiang et al.
2022). Moreover, a DT-driven model was created to predict the
reduced lifting capacity of aging tower cranes using real-time data,
thereby enhancing safety ( Hussain et al. 2024 ).
In summary, extensive research has been conducted on the iden-
tification and assessment of safety risks in tower crane operations.
It is widely acknowledged that complex procedures and inadequate
execution are the two most significant factors contributing to tower
crane incidents, particularly during installation and dismantling.
Most existing safety management approaches rely on passive mon-
itoring and issuing warnings, which often prove ineffective in emer-
gency situations. While emerging technologies have been explored
to improve safety, most of these efforts remain in the experimental
phase and have not yet been implemented in practical projects.
Furthermore, there is still no efficient solution for managing the
safety risks associated with complex procedures and inadequate op-
erations during the installation process.
Information System for Construction Safety
Management
Information systems are widely used to enhance construction safety
management across various areas, including safety in design (Zhang
and Hu 2011), site safety planning ( Cheng and Teizer 2013), safety
information management (Shi and Xu 2021 ), safety knowledge da-
tabases ( Zhang et al. 2015 ), safety monitoring ( Jiang 2020 ), and
safety training (Park and Kim 2013 ). Among these, safety informa-
tion management systems are the most common, utilized by project
departments, enterprises, and government agencies. Detailed re-
cords of daily operations and accident-related events are crucial for
addressing on-site accidents ( de la Garza et al. 1998 ; Hinze and
Russell 1995 ). For instance, accident data is recorded and stored

Goal-Directed Task Analysis for Safety Information
Eliciting end users ’ information requirements is a critical aspect of
the information system (IS) development cycle, aiming to identify
the data users need to make informed decisions. Goal-oriented
analysis has been proposed in requirements engineering to capture
the intent behind software requirements, providing an intuitive ap-
proach to eliciting and analyzing user needs. Among these meth-
ods, goal-directed task analysis (GDTA) was developed to enhance
situation awareness (SA) for end users, particularly in aviation and
military domains, and has been widely adopted for its efficiency
and ease of use, as shown in Fig. 3.
GDTA is a cognitive task analysis method that provides a
detailed description of the SA requirements of human operators
within a system ( Endsley et al. 1998 ; Endsley 2001). It is an effec-
tive tool for identifying the necessary information operators need to
understand the current situation and make decisions to achieve their
task goals (Bolstad et al. 2002). Goal-directed information analysis,
based on GDTA, has been proposed as a protocol to capture the
specific information needs in high-risk and emergency-related do-
mains (Prasanna 2009). This method has been successfully applied
in industries such as maritime navigation ( Cordon et al. 2017 ) and
firefighting ( Bearman and Bremner 2013 ), both of which involve
planned procedures, unpredictable events, and significant risks. For
instance, GDTA was used to define goals and information require-
ments for maritime pilotage, contributing to the design of operating
procedures and improving the understanding of maritime accidents
(Sharma et al. 2019 ). Similarly, it was applied to fire emergency
response systems to improve decision-making, especially in situa-
tions where firefighters lacked the skills and training needed to
manage emergencies ( Rezaeifam et al. 2023 ). In aviation, GDTA
has been combined with FCM (fuzzy cognitive maps) to establish
situation awareness models for flight crews, ensuring sufficient SA
during operations ( Li 2022; Lijing et al. 2022 ). A modified GDTA
method has also been used in goal –decision–process interviews to
clarify submariners’ SA information requirements during sampling
tasks ( Wang et al. 2024 ). Moreover, GDTA has been employed to
identify key goals (e.g., work process, quality, cost, safety, and
communication), subgoals, decisions, and the necessary informa-
tion, helping to reveal critical requirements for future developments
and illustrating the relationships between goals and information
(Pillajo et al. 2023 ).
In tower crane installations, while workers are provided with
prescheduled procedures and standard operation instructions, the
specific information requirements for various scenarios are often
unclear, leading to errors. GDTA is a highly suitable and effective
tool for addressing this issue.
Methodology
The proposed systematic control framework consists of three key
components: risk identification for process safety, information re-
quirements for risk control, and the development of a safety intelli-
gent navigation platform. As illustrated in Fig.4, the first component
identifies potential risks arising from complex procedures and inad-
equate operations. The second component defines the information
requirements for risk control through a hierarchical decision-making
process analysis. The final component involves building a safety in-
telligent navigation platform that integrates a physical model, virtual
model, real-time database, and service core. This platform creates
a closed-loop system that provides safety navigation and real-time
alarms to ensure the safety of the tower crane climbing process.
Risk Identification and Safety Information
Requirements
To ensure the safety of the tower crane climbing process, it is es-
sential to identify the factors that influence safety and determine the
necessary information for risk control. The structure of a tower
crane is dynamic and unstable during the climbing process, with the
internal forces between the steel framework and the tower constantly
shifting. This requires workers to perform precise operations and
maintain crane stability at all times. A detailed analysis of the climb-
ing process is conducted to identify risks that could lead to hazardous
situations, with operational guidelines clearly defined for managing
these risks at each stage. Based on the identified risks, a GDTA is
performed to determine the safety information requirements for risk
control. This information is crucial for workers to understand the
quality of their operations and the current safety status of the tower
crane, allowing them to respond effectively to inadequate operations
and abnormal conditions.

As a large-scale steel structure and specialized mechanical equip-
ment, tower cranes have complex designs and inherent safety risks.
During jacking operations, the crane undergoes multiple structural
transitions, each requiring precise and tightly controlled operations
to ensure the crane remains balanced and stable. This places high
demands on both the overall process order and the quality of local
operations. Maintaining a stable crane is not only the outcome of
proper operations but also a prerequisite for the next phase of work.
However, it is difficult to perceive the real-time safety status of the
crane during the jacking process. The orderly execution of opera-
tions relies heavily on effective information exchange and collabo-
ration among workers. However, workers often operate at high
altitudes on multiple platforms, which severely hinders timely com-
munication and coordination. The quality of local operations also
depends on worker performance, but factors such as limited visibil-
ity, restricted working space, and varying levels of safety awareness
make it challenging to consistently meet operational standards and
accurately assess conditions. Additionally, the physical and tempo-
ral separation between safety supervisors and operators reduces the
effectiveness of traditional safety supervision. These challenges
pose significant threats to tower crane safety and must be addressed,
as referenced in the Introduction section.
Given the constant dynamic changes in the crane ’s structural
system, it is challenging to directly monitor and assess its overall
safety status. The safety performance of the tower crane throughout
the entire climbing cycle hinges on several critical risk points. The
operational guidelines for controlling these risks at each step are
outlined in Table 1.
Safety Information Requirements for Risk Control
To identify the safety information required during the tower crane
climbing process, a series of interviews, theoretical analyses, and
case studies were conducted using GDTA. Based structured and
semistructured interviews, we summarized and validated the safety
information requirements (see Tables 2 and 3) with various stake-
holders, including operators, safety supervisors, manufacturers, and
safety experts (see Table 4). The overall goal was defined, along
with five categories of decisions (D1, D2, D3, D4, and D5) neces-
sary to achieve it. For each decision, information was categorized
into three levels: perception level (lv.1, e.g., I1.1.1), comprehension
level (lv.2, e.g., I1.1), and projection level (lv.3, e.g., I1).
The overall goal of tower crane climbing is to complete the
climbing task while ensuring occupational safety. Achieving this
goal requires five basic safety decisions: (1) stop unsafe behaviors
of operating staff, (2) check safety status and address safety haz-
ards, (3) confirm initial conditions, (4) verify performance at each
step, and (5) intervene in operations. Safety information is needed
at three levels to support these decisions. To make safety decision
D1, identify and stop unsafe behaviors of operating staff, it is nec-
essary to assess their safety status (I1 at lv.3) based on the following
factors: guidelines for safe behavior (I1.1 at lv.2), state of health
(I1.2 at lv.2), and compliance with safety behaviors (I1.3 at lv.2).
The comprehension of staff behavior compliance (I1.3 at lv.2) is fur-
ther determined by the status of safety hardhats (I1.3.1 at lv.1), safety
belts (I1.3.2 at lv.1), and physical behaviors (I1.3.3 at lv.1).
Throughout the entire tower crane climbing process, numerous
decisions must be made at the right moments to ensure safe and
orderly operations, as shown in Fig. 7. Decisions such as D1, D2,
D3, D4, and D5 (Table 4) are crucial for achieving the overall safety
and performance objectives. Initially, D2 is employed to establish
a secure foundation for subsequent climbing tasks. At each step in
Fig. 4, D3 is needed to confirm operational readiness, including
personnel, crane, and environmental conditions. D4 ensures proper
task execution, minimizing risks associated with inadequate oper-
ations. Additionally, D1 or D5 may be activated by specific safety
risks, such as unsafe behaviors, operational errors, or hazardous
conditions at critical points. These events disrupt the normal climb-
ing process, requiring a corrective phase before operations can re-
sume. After all major goals are completed, D2 must be executed to
perform a comprehensive inspection of the tower crane, ensuring it
is safe and stable before workers leave the site. To support the timely
and effective execution of these decisions, a substantial amount of
safety-related information must be efficiently gathered, transmitted,
processed, and applied.

Physical Tower Crane for Operation
The physical model is a fundamental component for risk monitor-
ing and data acquisition, equipped with a variety of sensors and
monitoring devices to capture real-time risk data, as shown in Fig.8.
This model replicates the power components and movable struc-
tures of a real tower crane, effectively simulating all climbing op-
erations with realistic precision.
V arious sensors are deployed to monitor critical risk points: a
wind speed sensor mounted at the top of the crane measures wind
speed, ensuring safe operations under adverse weather conditions.
Multiple position sensors (#1, #2, #3, #4, #5, and #6) are strategically
placed at different locations on the crane to monitor the position
and status of mechanical components, ensuring all parts function
correctly. A tilt sensor measures the tilt angle of the crane structure,
preventing operations beyond safe limits, while a displacement
sensor tracks the movement of crane components to ensure precise
control during lifting operations. A mobile central unit acts as the
data processing hub, collecting and transmitting data from all sen-
sors. Additionally, a video camera provides visual monitoring, ena-
bling remote supervision and quick identification of any operational
anomalies.
Together, these sensors and components allow the physical
model to simulate real-world operations on-site, collect and trans-
mit real-time data, and provide the virtual model with accurate op-
erational states. This creates a closed-loop system that enhances
the safety and efficiency of crane operations.
Virtual Model for Mapping Physical Status
The virtual model is a digital twin of the physical entity,
integrating and combining four layers of models: geometric,
physical, behavioral, and rule-base d. Specifically , the geometric
model describes geometric parameters such as dimensions,

Real-Time Database for Digital Twin Exchanging
To effectively monitor and manage the real-time status of the tower
crane during the climbing process, advanced data integration and
processing technologies are essential. This involves seamlessly han-
dling data from various sensors, operational data, and simulation
outputs to provide a comprehensive view of the crane ’s condition
throughout the climbing process.
In the tower crane digital twin model, a real-time database is
employed to manage this diverse data. The database integrates and
processes sensor data from key points such as the cross-beam,
climbing claws, tilt angle, and wind speed. This information is criti-
cal for assessing safety and guiding operations. The presentation of
multisource real-time data is shown in Fig. 11. The duration of this
abnormal state has already exceeded the maximum allowed time for
the step, thus it transitions to a dangerous status. Specifically, after
entering the first climbing phase, the previously free beam must
be locked to provide structural support, which requires some time
to complete. However, the process of collecting sensor data and
determining the beam ’s status takes less than 1 second. During this
time, the operation might still be in progress, and thus cannot be
classified as dangerous. However, if the state persists beyond the
maximum allowed time, it indicates an unusual issue, and therefore
the status transitions to dangerous to trigger an alarm. By leverag-
ing the capabilities of a real-time database, the system efficiently
manages data from multiple sources, ensuring accurate and timely
updates. This integration enhances both the safety and efficiency of
tower crane operations, providing a robust solution for real-time
monitoring and management.
Core Service Realization
The workflow for providing safety navigation and real-time
alarms is illustrated in Fig. 12. Sensors monitor the status of
key points, and the collected data is transmitted to the system
platform. Relevant modules use this real-time data to assess
the status of these key points, evaluating them against estab-
lished safety protocols. The re sults from multiple sources are
combined using a Boolean AND operation to generate an overall
output. Two judgment criteria —“Is it regular? ” and “Abnormal
time consumption? ”—classify the overall output into three cat-
egories: safe, abnormal, or dangerous. For example, in the initial
stage, one of the standard procedures is to disconnect the tower
body from the lower rotating. Failing to perform this operation
may result in the bolts being suddenly ejected when the hy-
draulic cylinder extends, leading to imbalance of the framework
and the potential collapse of the tower crane. From the system ’s
perspective, the initial state will be identified as abnormal, wait-
ing for the operator to promptly d isconnect the bolts. If this state
persists beyond a certain time threshold, it indicates that the op-
erator likely failed to perform the regular procedure and may
proceed to the next step, which requires the system to change
the status to dangerous and trigger an alarm. This output is then
combined with the safety status of hardhat, determined by a sep-
arate module that utilizes a computer vision algorithm, for a
final safety classification. The hardhat detection module is de-
veloped using the Hikvision AI Open Platform version 2.0 and
incorporates an object detection model. An on-site camera
equipped with a hardhat detection algorithm transmits workers ’
hardhat usage status back to the monitoring platform to support
upper-level services.
Based on the final safety classification —safe, abnormal, or
dangerous —the warning system (including alarms, horns, and
terminals) responds accordingly. This ensures that operation
staff can follow navigation prom pts during climbing procedures
or take immediate corrective a ctions when unsafe conditions are
detected.

Scenario 2: Providing Real-Time Alarm for
Unsafe Status
In this scenario, workers deliberately created unsafe situations
to trigger the system ’s alarms. Unsafe behaviors and substandard
operations, which are commonly encountered on real-world con-
struction sites, were selected for simulation.
1. Status 1: Unsafe Behavior
At the start of the climbing process, the supervisor removed
his hardhat. The system quickly detected this unsafe behavior
using video data and triggered a light and sound alarm: “Some-
body isn ’t wearing a hardhat, please adjust immediately! ” The
alarm persisted until the hardhat was put back on, as shown in
Fig. 17.
2. Status 2: Inadequate Operations
Three substandard operations were simulated: failing to sup-
port the climbing claw with the ear plate, failing to support the
cross-beam with the ear plate, and failing to deploy the new sec-
tion, as shown in Fig. 18.
For example, in Step 3 (see Table 1), the operation staff failed to
notice that the climbing claws had not been lifted onto the ear
plates. This posed a serious risk, as proceeding to Step 4 without
proper support would mean there was no stable force transmission
path between the climbing claws and the ear plates. The embedded
switch sensor detected this issue and alerted the safety intelligent
device. The device recognized the hazardous situation and issued a
real-time light and sound alarm: “The climbing claws have not yet
been set into position, please check! ” The operation staff quickly
received the message, made the necessary adjustments, and the
alarm was deactivated once the climbing claws were securely posi-
tioned. The interrupted process then resumed safely.
The system performed similarly in the other two inadequate
operation scenarios: failing to support the cross-beam with the ear
plate and failing to deploy the new section.
