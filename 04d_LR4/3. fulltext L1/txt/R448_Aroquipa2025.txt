ABSTRACT
This study proposes a methodological framework for integrating structural health monitoring (SHM) systems
with numerical digital twin (DT) models for the seismic assessment of heritage structures. This approach is being
implemented within smart cities, focusing on intelligent circular resilience. The process begins in the physical
world by recording data through strategically placed sensors distributed throughout the evaluated structures.
Afterwards, the virtual world refers to the development of numerical models that capture the structural per-
formance of the building while considering specific features to simulate the original one. The interconnection of
both sides requires exhaustive and detailed cross-validation to ensure an established level of reliability, often
based on the expected accuracy and limitations . Nevertheless, extending this process to heritage buildings de-
mands a more sophisticated approach, designed to identify and classify discrete damage levels, and involves
establishing proper threshold limits for an early warning/alarm system. This paper presents a methodology
focused on the virtual implementation of these processes, aiming to integrate such technologies effectively. The
case study is a religious monument that has been standing for over a century, located in a high seismic hazard
zone in Lima, Peru. Numerical models were developed using 3DEC and SAP2000 software to capture the
building ’ s dynamic response, including modal and time-history analyses for a set of 34 seismic ground motion
records. The results were used to identify susceptible zones and the location of sensors . Further recommendations
address the challenges encountered in implementing this approach and outline the next steps, including sensor
deployment and validation against experimental models.
1. Introduction
In modern engineering, the characterization and estimation of both
the seismic response and the performance of structural and non-
structural systems have evolved through the incorporation of innova-
tive technologies. These tools allow users to capture and integrate all
building components into a virtual model through numerical models,
which are meant to approximate the behavior of structures and on un-
certainties on the level and frequency of extreme loadings. However,
these representations are constrained by the scope of work, the reli-
ability required for each particular project, the level of detailing of the
model, and the capabilities/limitations of the software for the simula-
tion. In addition, access to specific building features related to geometry,
material properties, damage reports from past events, and interventions
plays a key role when determining the model ’ s adequacy and accuracy
in properly capturing the seismic response of the construction. Never-
theless, this practice has nowadays been widely standardized, to some
extent, in national and international codes and standards for common
practice [1,2] , under what has been cataloged as seismic vulnerability
assessment for existing buildings.
Special attention has been given to buildings classified as heritage
buildings, which present a unique structural behavior when subjected to
external loadings. In fact, many seismic vulnerability studies have
demonstrated that, despite having similar features from a geometric and
material standpoint, their seismic response cannot be grouped into a
generic category [3 – 7] . Moreover, features related to construction

Despite all these challenges faced by structural engineers in char-

projects have been developed worldwide to preserve their historical
integrity [18,19] , leading to the development of alternative techniques
integrating technologies that feed traditional numerical modelling.
Nowadays, Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) is one of them, focused
on capturing the building ’ s state by installing sensors to measure
real-time parameters [20 – 24] . The sensor locations are based on expert
judgement and results from Finite-Element Method (FEM) simulations,
which focus on components highly susceptible to seismic actions [21,
25 – 27] . One of the main goals of SHM is the creation of systems capable
of predicting damage levels. In this context, the digital revolution 4.0
offers a new solution called digital twin (DT) models [28 – 32] , meant to
connect the real and virtual worlds by creating a digital model that
represents the actual asset, while in the physical world, there are sensors
placed strategically, streamlining the real-time flow of information
[33 – 36] .

At this point, the Intelligent Circular Resilience (ICR) is proposed as a
novel methodological framework aimed at improving DRR management
by extending the concept of dynamic circular seismic resilience through
the integration of SHM, real-time data analysis, and active
public-private participation. The framework operates across three
stages — understanding seismic risk, implementing mitigation strategies,
and responding to seismic events — within a continuous, circular process
that identifies blind spots in risk perception, institutional coordination,
and infrastructure planning. ICR is designed to function on three inter-
dependent levels: institutional expertise, infrastructure risk manage-
ment, and real-time monitoring, each supported by the adoption of
emerging technologies toward the development of intelligent and
resilient urban environments [43] .
As a result, the authors proposed a novel methodological approach
focused on dynamic ICR- refer to Fig. 1 , which emphasizes the devel-
opment of DT models from the virtual world standpoint. On one hand,
this approach addresses the challenges and limitations encountered in
heritage buildings by identifying critical features such as geometry
configuration, material properties, and available historical records. On
the other hand, it deals with the duality of the physical and virtual world
through the selection and installation of sensor devices and the devel-
opment of a trustworthy DT model for continuous feedback. This
research delves into the Building Virtualization and partially on Data
Gathering and Analysis from Sensors phases outlined in Fig. 1 (i.e., steps
02 and 03, respectively). The broad development of the methodology
will be complete in further publications, incorporating experimental
models and sensor deployment, covering phases 3 – 6.
In this study, the DT is defined as a dynamic numerical representa-
tion of the heritage building that replicates its behavior under seismic
loading, continuously updated and cross-validated through real-time
data from strategically placed sensors. This virtual replica integrates
geometric fidelity, material heterogeneity, historical performance data,
and predictive deep learning (DL) algorithms, enabling proactive
structural assessment and decision-making. Geometrical accuracy is
critical in the development of DT models, especially when dealing with
heritage buildings whose non-linear seismic behavior is highly sensitive
to local geometric irregularities. Recent studies (e.g., [44,45] ) demon-
strate the power of combining 3D scanning technologies and photo-
grammetric methods to reconstruct detailed digital geometries suitable
for structural analysis. In parallel, recent work by [46] outlines a sys-
tematic pipeline for generating discrete element models (DEM) directly
from imagery, reinforcing the potential of image-to-DEM workflows for
data-driven seismic assessment.
The analyzed phases were achieved through three main steps; the
first provides guidance on the available and recommended field survey
methods to characterize the geometric configuration and material
properties using a Building Information Model (BIM). The second fo-
cuses on the creation of the virtual model using a finite element method
(FEM) model and covers key aspects such as software selection based on
its features, capabilities, and limitations; the linking process between the
BIM and FEM model, modeling techniques, and seismic hazard defini-
tion are also well-thought-out . Finally, the last phase highlights the
challenges that arise during the post-processing of results and highlights
the available tools to analyze massive data by suggesting a novel idea
that incorporates the intensity-duration-frequency plots for the engi-
neering demand parameters (EDP). Therefore, all these steps lead to the
integration process between the physical and virtual world under the
ICR framework (see Fig. 2 ).
The process deals with aspects related to the deployment of a reliable
SHM while focusing on the Early-warning system (EWS) and its con-
nectivity between both worlds in real-time. Specialized sensor devices
installed on the building will cover the physical world mapping
response, while a FEM model is meant to represent the DT model in the
virtual world. Thus, sensor software and hardware requirements, as well
as the location selection for installing those, are defined using the
performance-based assessment from a non-linear FEM model. In addi-
tion, these devices measure movement in terms of displacement,

Recent developments in artificial intelligence have introduced
advanced techniques for crack detection using DL and computer vision,

sensor-based modeling, these techniques are highly relevant and may be
integrated into the early damage identification process or serve as
validation tools for physical damage post-event.
The case study corresponds to a heritage building located in Lima,
Peru, specifically a religious monument that was built a century ago . A
field survey was conducted to portray the geometric configuration
through a point-cloud survey using a 3D laser scanner (3D LiDAR
technology) that was then converted into a BIM, while the definition of
the mechanical properties of the materials was based on previous studies
and archives. In addition, detailed research into the building ’ s perfor-
mance during previous earthquakes was addressed by checking existing
damage reports, including the 2007 Pisco earthquake (moment magni-
tude, Mw = 8.0). Subsequently, the virtual model was developed using
the software 3DEC Version 9.0 [49] , based on an intermediate calibra-
tion and validation process, which consisted of approximately 4500
block elements and 161,000 contact zones, and analyzed a total of 5
seismic records through time-history analysis (THA). The results were
used to identify the monument ’ s seismic response, compare it with the
previous damage report, and define the best spots for installing the field
sensors that would be integrated into the physical world via the DT
model.
The main novelty of this research lies in the development of a
methodology integrated with the ICR framework for heritage buildings.
This methodology leverages SHM through a DT model, combined with a
numerical one that focuses on the virtualization of the building. The
technical aspects are addressed using a performance-based assessment,
which captures the seismic structural response through a novel way of
using the intensity-duration-frequency (IDF) for characterizing EDPs ’
results. This approach condenses the vast data generated from the THA
results, allowing the identification of critical distribution patterns for the
selection, placement, and calibration of sensors on the building, as well
as TF for estimating damage on masonry walls. Finally, the methodology
proposes action strategies to overcome the challenges encountered
throughout the entire implementation process. Furthermore, it can be
extended to other heritage buildings.
2. Characteristics of the heritage building: Maria Auxiliadora
Basilica
A religious monument was selected as the case study heritage
building: the Maria Auxiliadora Basilica, located in the Bre ˜na district of
the city of Lima, Peru (i.e., a very-high seismic hazard zone, refer to
Section 3.2 ). The construction process lasted around 8 years, from May
1916 to March 1924, which was initially labeled as a sanctuary until
1962, when it was recognized as a basilica. Fig. 3 shows some pictures of
the monument, while a detailed description of its main features can be
found in [50] . Since its construction, the building has not undergone
major modifications to its original structure, except for two in-
terventions due to restoration works after specific earthquake events.
The main features related to the geometric configuration and con-
struction materials (i.e., brick masonry units, mortar, concrete, steel,
and stone masonry for the foundations) were obtained from the Maria
Auxiliadora archives [50] , which also provided some useful insights
regarding the construction process and techniques. These records clar-
ified many of the connections between different materials, such as truss
support on masonry walls and encasement of steel profiles in the main
columns, details that are sometimes difficult to identify through field
surveys without performing destructive explorations. As a result, all the
information provides valuable insight into the DT model by enhancing
the accuracy in the representation of the physical world in the virtual
one and reducing the uncertainty related to building configuration and
construction techniques lost over time, as evidenced in other studies
[51 – 54] . This translates into a more reliable numerical model,
increasing the accuracy of the structural representation and its inter-
action with the different materials comprising the building. The next
subsections present the characteristics of the mechanical material
properties, geometric configurations, and a summary of the performance
from previous earthquake events that were used to calibrate FEM
models.
2.1. Materials
The heritage building comprises several materials, including ma-
sonry, concrete, steel, and mortar. The foundation system consists of
masonry with clay brick units and cement mortar joints. For the columns
of the nave, some utilize concrete-encased steel H-shaped profiles, while
all external and internal walls are made of masonry finished with a
concrete mortar layer. The remaining columns are unreinforced brick
masonry, confirmed through architectural plans and visual inspection.
As for the domes, they were built using several layered materials,
including a reinforced concrete slab and brick arches. Furthermore, the
roof consists of cast iron trusses with L-shapes, holding the clayey
shingles that are supported on the perimeter walls. The connections
between steel elements were built using rivets, as this was the latest
technique at the time of construction.
Direct field characterization through destructive testing was not
possible due to preservation regulations for heritage buildings. While
non-destructive dynamic techniques such as ambient vibration testing
(AVT) are viable alternatives (see [55] ), the present study relied on
archival and prior technical data due to the scope of this methodological
phase. AVT-based calibration will be considered in future work during
the sensor integration phase. Table 1 summarizes the mechanical
properties of the materials used for the FEM model, which were derived
from previous studies and detailed research in archives [56] . That
highlights one of the main challenges faced when assessing heritage
buildings [8,15,17,24,57] , which forces engineers to rely on alternative
sources or similar buildings built during the same period [15] .
2.2. Geometry
The geometric characterization of the Basilica María Auxiliadora was
conducted using state-of-the-art terrestrial LiDAR technology to capture
the macro-geometric configuration of the monument with the highest
possible spatial fidelity. A high-density 3D laser scanning system was
employed, capable of acquiring up to 976,000 points per second, with an
effective operating range from 0.6 m to 120 m, suitable for both interior
environments with low ambient lighting and reflective exterior surfaces.
The system offers a maximum distance measurement error of ± 2 mm at
100 m, validated for surfaces with both high and low reflectivity,
ensuring accuracy even under complex architectural and ornamental
conditions typical of historical structures.
The scanner integrates a dual-axis compensator with an angular
precision of 0.015
◦
, allowing automatic correction of leveling deviations
during field acquisition. Additionally, an electronic barometric height
sensor and digital compass provided georeferenced orientation and
vertical positioning for each scan station, facilitating global alignment
and registration of the point cloud dataset. The device also includes 70-
megapixel color resolution, enabling the generation of textured and

Based on the processed point cloud, illustrated in Fig. 4 — a BIM was
developed, which was subsequently validated through an exhaustive
comparison with the topographic records, following the methodology
detailed in Fig. 1 . This verification confirmed the unaltered spatial
layout of the original architectural configuration since the building ’ s
construction, as documented in historical archives and the original
design plans by the Italian architect Ernesto Vespignani [50,56] . This
workflow ensured a dimensionally accurate and verifiable geometric
base for numerical modeling, establishing a robust foundation for sub-
sequent structural assessments under both static and dynamic loading
conditions.
The building exhibits the typical spatial organization of religious
architecture in the Byzantine-Romanesque style, including a single-
cuspid front, central nave, lateral aisles, presbytery, side chapels, a
subterranean crypt, and an auxiliary service area located on the east side
(see Fig. 5 ). The overall footprint of the structure spans approximately
2000 m², with dimensions of 70 m in length, 30 m in width, and a front
tower rising 56 m.
Subsequently, the final geometric configuration used for the FEM
model creation is depicted in Fig. 6 , aiming to illustrate some of the main
dimensions, while Table 2 summarizes the typical cross-sections for the
most relevant structural elements. The main structural system is
comprised of masonry bearing walls on the façade, while the inner walls
are supported on reinforced concrete columns and masonry arches that
interconnect with the outer walls. Likewise, the roof is comprised of a
steel truss supported on the load-bearing walls as well as the reinforced
concrete vaults.
2.3. Building performance from previous earthquakes
Extensive research was conducted to understand the building ’ s
behavior during past earthquake events by consulting existing damage
record reports. A detailed repository with records of the main events
affecting the monument (held by the Salesian community) was reviewed
to establish the date of each record and obtain a brief description. From
the whole, a total of 5 earthquake-related episodes were relevant [58] .
One of these events was the earthquake on May 25th, 1940 , with its
epicenter in Lima and a moment magnitude of Mw = 7.90. Furthermore,
an intervention process was performed in 2012 due to damage sustained
by the Ica earthquake (Mw = 7.90) on August 15th, 2007, which caused
considerable damage to the building components, as indicated in the
report prepared by “ Universidad Nacional de Ingeniería ” (UNI-CISMID)
and “ Pontificia Universidad Cat ´olica del Perú ” (PUCP) in 2009 [50] . A
summary of the effects and the retrofitting campaign is presented in
[59] . From the author ’ s side , a technical analysis was conducted by
revising historical photographs [59] , which allowed identifying minor
crack patterns throughout various components ranging from 0.01 mm to
4 mm. However, there was a particular element with a 7 mm crack
width. The main findings are summarized below:
• Apse: vertical cracks between the wall joints and minor cracks on
vaults.
• Central tower: vertical cracks in the central tower and west façade.
• Main access arch at central tower: vertical cracks in the main access
arch.
• Lateral left tower: vertical and horizontal cracks, with no clear
patterns.
• Exterior vault: cracks at joints and longitudinal minor cracks along
its perimeter.
• Interior vault: cracks at joints between vaults ’ arches and minor
cracks in the longitudinal vault in the main nave.
• Main columns: cracks concentrated at the column base up to the first
meter and spreading above with no clear pattern.

4. Resilience and performance-based assessment
SHM and DT models were integrated through an EWS, which will be
deployed in future research once the completion of methodology pre-
sented in Fig. 1 is completed. The inputs required for implementing the
EWS follow a three-stage process, starting from a performance-based
assessment of the heritage building, followed by defining the best
location for installing the sensors. Lastly, the implementation of TF was
performed to convert the movement that will be gathered by the sensors
into structural damage on the masonry walls. The overall process is
shown in Fig. 11 . A detailed FEM model was developed considering the
main findings depicted in the BIM, the expected non-linear response of
all materials comprising the structural components, and the loading
conditions for gravitational and lateral loads. Intermediate calibration
was conducted to validate the results of the model, followed by
analyzing the seismic response through 5 THA.
Then, the results from the FEM model were utilized as input to define
the sensor position. During this process, several regions of interest (RoI)
were defined to assess the most representative zone for installing them.
Likewise, the IDF plots allowed for the identification of the range of
operation and frequency sampling held in these devices. Finally, a set of
TFs for each sensor was estimated using the Multiple-Input-Single-
Output (MISO) approach and ML algorithm (see Eq. 1 ).

4.1. Model calibration
A numerical model was set up to capture the proper dynamic
response of the heritage building, considered as a mixed structural
system with masonry walls and interior reinforced concrete columns as
well as reinforced concrete vaults. The calibration process included the
development of two different FEM models to idealize the building ’ s
response. The first was a multi-degree-of-freedom (MDoF) elastic linear
model based on finite elements (i.e., shell and frame elements)
implemented in the software SAP2000 [61] . The second was a detailed
MDoF non-linear model implemented in the software 3DEC Version 9.0
[49] ; for the sake of visualization, Fig. 12 presents screenshots of both.
The 3DEC model was constructed using deformable blocks with a
finite-deformation FEM formulation. The structure was discretized into
heterogeneous zones with distinct material properties and not treated as
a homogeneous or rigid discrete system. The “ block ” element represents
solid or volume elements with interactions at joints (i.e., contact zones)
that are divided into tetrahedral zones to allow deformability [49] . The

The linear-elastic MDoF model developed in SAP2000 was used as a
preliminary benchmark to calibrate global dynamic behaviors such as
mass distribution, stiffness, and modal characteristics of the more
detailed non-linear FEM model implemented in 3DEC. The calibration
process involved matching the fundamental vibration periods and modal
shapes obtained from both models, ensuring consistency in mass
participation factors and overall structural behavior. Adjustments were
made to the material stiffness and boundary conditions in the 3DEC
model to align its dynamic response with the results of the SAP2000
model. Although 3DEC is capable of both FEM and DEM analysis, the
model used in this study was developed entirely using FEM-based
deformable block elements with contact interface behavior, and no
distinct element formulation was applied.
In the next phase, the BIM creation required an extra-mile effort
before migrating the model into the final software, despite the numerous
bridging BIM -FEM plugins available in the market. Some challenges
included unifying edges and completing missing boundary conditions
between the multiple elements, and then properly setting assumptions,
capabilities, desired accuracy levels, and limitations related to the FEM.
4.2. Modal analysis results
The dynamic response of the building was assessed by means of a
modal analysis, applied to the MDoF model (i.e., model 1 - linear elastic
model). An eigenvalue analysis was conducted for a total of 220 vibra-
tion modes, which achieved at least 80 % of mass participation in each
main direction (i.e., longitudinal (UX) and transversal (UY) direction.
The seismic mass of the structure, as derived from the FEM models, is
approximately 21,400 kN, which corresponds to a total structural mass
of about 2180 metric tons. This value was independently verified
through a manual estimation based on volumetric measurements
extracted from the BIM model and archival material density data for
masonry, concrete, and steel elements. The comparison between both
methods yielded a discrepancy of less than 5 %, confirming the reli-
ability of the modeled seismic mass. Fig. 13 displays the response for the
first six modes of vibration extracted from the SAP2000 linear-elastic
model, illustrating both translational and torsional behavior along the
main structural axes, while Table 3 contains the mass participation
factors and structural periods for the fundamental modes in the three
translational and rotational directions. It is important to note that the
structural periods differ by less than 5 % between the models. Finally,
the fundamental structural periods in the longitudinal (UX) and trans-
verse (UY) directions of the building are 0.39 sec and 0.38 sec, respec-
tively (refer to modes 3 and 4 in Table 3 ).
4.3. Time history analysis results
The building ’ s dynamic response was evaluated using THA after
validating the detailed model (i.e., model 2 – non-linear elastic model),
by applying the set of seismic ground motions selected in Section 3.1 .
The damage to the structural components was evaluated by means of a
comparison between their capacity and demands (unreduced and
unfactored). In the case of the masonry walls, concrete columns, and
cast-iron trusses, the unreduced or nominal capacity was used. More-
over, horizontal and vertical displacement limits were considered as part
of the performance-based assessment conducted on the heritage build-
ing. Fig. 14 displays the results for the most critical seismic ground
motion record, highlighting the axial stresses and shear force demands
acting on the masonry walls under dynamic excitation.
The vast volume of data generated by the THA implied a complex
and time-consuming processing task. To address this challenge, an
innovative approach systematizes the data through intensity-duration-
frequency (IDF) plots, organized according to key structural elements,
such as wall typologies. The first step consists in defining the structural
element under consideration; subsequently, the intensity is identified
and classified into a discretized range of relevant parameters for eval-
uation (i.e., stresses, displacements, velocities, or accelerations); the
discretization of these ranges is based on control points according to the
specific objectives expected by the authors from the THA. Once this has
been defined, the duration is calculated based on the time a given

4.4. Comparison between the numerical model and the historical damage
reported
The FEM model performance, subjected to a subset of seismic ground
motion records (related to the ICA Earthquake event – 08.15.2007), was
compared against the aforementioned damage report. In general, it
accurately confirmed the crack ’ s distribution over the barred vault
reinforced concrete elements (refer to Fig. 18 ) and corroborated the
most vulnerable areas for the real structure. The cracks reported in the
numerical model corresponded to the regions of the reinforced concrete
vaults that surpassed the concrete tensile stress threshold (i.e., modulus
of rupture for concrete with a value of 2.84 MPa). Some isolated zones
also exceeded this limit in the FEM, even though the damage report DB
did not report the same.
4.5. Recommended control points for implementing the digital-twin model
This stage aims to guarantee real-time data acquisition to link the
physical and virtual worlds by addressing the optimal sensor location.
The distribution of the control points where the in-field sensors were
meant to be installed was based on the identification of the highly
sensitive regions prone to damage by seismic earthquakes, as well as
those of primary interest that will trigger the EWS. Their final location
accounts for the seismic structural response acknowledged by the nu-
merical models, and the building ’ s performance observed from past
earthquake events.
During this process, a primer analysis for the masonry walls con-
sisted of tracking the normal to bed joints axial stress as an EDP. In this
case, only the main structural walls were considered, as highlighted in
Fig. 19 . Fig. 20 illustrates the EDP intensity-frequency results from the
THA for the main wall in the longitudinal direction (i.e., Wall IDs WL01-
P1 and WL01-P2), discretized by height ranges varying from 0 to 24 m,
with a 2-meter delta. Appendix C contains the complete set of figures for
the other walls. Finally, Fig. 21 presents the summary of the vertical
axial stress intensity-frequency results for all the piers and the whole set
of seismic ground motions.
From Fig. 20 and Fig. 21 , it is observed that around 50 % of the walls
sustained a vertical axial stress between   0.50 and 0.26 MPa, con-
firming they were subjected to considerable compression and tensile
stress. In addition, a small percentage of the longitudinal piers reached
values greater than 1.00 MPa (i.e., around 0.4 %), while for the trans-
verse ones, this value increased up to 2.6 % of the area exceeding this
threshold. Conversely, compression stresses greater than 0.50 MPa
occurred for a maximum of 2 % and 6 % of the wall area for longitudinal
and transverse walls, respectively.
Then, four regions of interest (RoI) were established based on pre-
vious results for mapping the masonry wall performance at different
range height levels (see Fig. 22 ). It includes the main tower, two portions
of the masonry walls in the central nave extending one-half of the dis-
tance between columns in the longitudinal direction, and the outer
lateral walls in the ambulatory region. EDPs plots, including displace-
ment and acceleration vs axial stresses (i.e., normal to bed joints di-
rection), were analyzed to characterize the wall ’ s performance using the
results obtained from the THA for the non-linear MDoF model (i.e.,
model 2). Fig. 23 depicts the results of these RoI covering the main tower
(i.e., RoI #1) while the central nave and ambulatory region are provided
in Appendix D.
From Fig. 23 it is observed that displacement and acceleration show
an increase with the height level, where their behavior can be grouped
into three levels, i.e., from 7 to 20.31 m, 20.31 – 39.65 m, and
39.65 – 51.5 m. Likewise, stresses are higher at lower levels; nonetheless,
the last two top levels display an increase in their values. In the case of
Level 6, this increase is related to the bell weight, which is supported by
it. Similar trends were depicted for the other RoI on the inner and outer
walls. As a result, Fig. 24 illustrates the suggested points for positioning
the sensors at each RoI identified.

(LSTM) model, a type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), was selected.
This model is well-suited for sequence-to-sequence regression tasks and
can effectively leverage past time steps [62] . Input signals for acceler-
ation, velocities, and displacement were analyzed, considering two
movement direction scenarios: i) horizontal movement (UX and UY) and

subsequently divided into training (70 %), validation (15 %), and
testing (15 %) sets. Furthermore, each time series was segmented using
a time window of 4.0 s with an overlap of 0.5 s.
The entire process was implemented and solved using a MATLAB-
based workflow [63] (as detailed in Appendix E). This methodology
was applied to the main tower at control points “ Group 1a ” and “ Group
1b ” (see Fig. 24 ), excluding the top level (51.42 m height). Fig. 27 il-
lustrates the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) performance evolution
for the training and validation sets when the LSTM model was trained
using acceleration input signals. Finally, Fig. 28 presents the overall
performance, in terms of RMSE, for the 24 LSTM models evaluated on
the validation and testing sets (resulting from 4 control points x 2 di-
rection scenarios x 3 movement components).
The results illustrated in Fig. 27 show the evolution of RMSE over
training iterations for two input signal scenarios. Four sensor heights
were analyzed: 9.38 m, 20.31 m, 36.15 m, and 39.65 m. The continuous
lines represent training RMSE, while dashed lines indicate validation
RMSE. A general trend observed across both cases is the decreasing
RMSE over iterations, confirming successful convergence of the LSTM
model. Moreover, the model performs significantly better when the
sensor is placed at lower heights (i.e., 9.38 m and 20.31 m), where both
the training and validation RMSE remain below 0.1. This suggests that
signal quality and information content are richer at these locations,
likely due to higher energy response concentrations and better observ-
ability of local deformation patterns, which align with the nonlinear
FEM-predicted damage zones.
Furthermore, Case 2 consistently outperforms Case 1, with reduced
RMSE levels across all control points. This confirms the added predictive
value of the vertical motion component (UZ) in estimating axial
stress — an important insight, as vertical accelerations are typically
underestimated in masonry monitoring due to assumptions of high axial
stiffness. The results here emphasize that vertical ground motion, while
small in magnitude, carries critical information regarding through-
thickness cracking and stress redistribution, especially in complex ge-
ometries such as towers.
Fig. 28 presents a summarized comparison of RMSE values obtained
from the 24 LSTM models applied to validation and testing datasets. The
lowest RMSEs are again concentrated in sensors located at 9.38 m and
20.31 m, supporting the findings from Fig. 27 . Notably, the RMSE values
remain under 0.1 in most test cases, which reflects a high level of
generalization and robustness of the LSTM models. However, a slight
increase in error variability is seen at the higher elevations (36.15 m and
39.65 m), particularly under Case 1 scenarios. This suggests that
elevated regions of the tower present higher complexity in dynamic
behavior due to boundary condition interactions, modal coupling, and
potentially unmodeled phenomena like torsion or out-of-plane flexure.
In summary, the figures provide empirical support for the effec-
tiveness of LSTM-based models in predicting nonlinear axial stress re-
sponses in heritage masonry walls using real-time acceleration, velocity,
and displacement inputs. The results emphasize the importance of
optimal sensor placement (lower elevations yield better signal fidelity),

5. Conclusions
Based on the structural seismic response assessment on an existing
heritage building, a methodological approach was proposed by the au-
thors to standardize the ideal distribution of sensors devoted to real-time
data feeding of digital twins. This methodology involved the identifi-
cation of its main characteristics, including materials and geometry,
followed by the idealization of the structural response through the
development of numerical models. Then, the calibration and validation
required a performance-based assessment of the monument, by means of
linear and non-linear multi-degree-of-freedom models and a modal-
response analysis to identify its dynamic response and behavior under
static and dynamic loading conditions. The output allowed the re-
searchers to tackle some of the main challenges faced when dealing with
Building Virtualization for the integration of the numerical model into
the Digital Twin model.
Subsequently, a time-history analysis was performed for a set of
seismic ground motions retrieved from real events recorded at different

showed a trend where most of the records did not induce significant
damage to the structural components of the building and confirmed its

In the case of the few records that triggered damage, the cracking pat-
terns also matched with the outputs from the numerical models and
documentation (highlighting the most vulnerable areas of the building).
For these specific cases, special attention needs to be given, and a deeper
sensitivity analysis should be conducted to enhance the updating of the
numerical model due to uncertainties associated with the modeling
process related to mechanical material properties.
Furthermore, the authors proposed a novel way for visualizing the
vast amount of data generated by the time-history analysis through an
intensity-duration-frequency (IDF) plot. This novel proposal was also
used to identify the intensity ranges of the engineering demand pa-
rameters that later would be correlated with the selection of sensors

physical and virtual worlds. The validation process depicted critical
displacement values up to 0.40 m in the horizontal direction, which
were, on average, way lower (i.e., 3.0 cm for 60 % of the entire duration
of the seismic records).
