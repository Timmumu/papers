Highlights: 
⚫ Anti-collision system is developed to improve safety for HRIs. 
⚫ DT system with “perception, analysis, decision-making, execution” is proposed. 
⚫ Multi-sensor fusion approach is designed for comprehensive on-site perception. 
Abstract: The construction industry is one of the most perilous sectors globally, with significantly 
higher fatality rates compared to other industries. Collision with heavy construction machines is a major 
cause of worker injuries and fatalities. This research proposes a digital twin -enabled anti-collision 
system to provide collision warnings and improve construction safety. A five-dimensional digital twin 
model encompassing the physical layer, digital data layer, virtual entity layer, service layer, and 
connection layer is established. The physical layer designs multi-sensor fusion approach to respectively 
collect image data, depth data, and motion data , achieving comprehensive perception of construction 
sites. The digital data layer designs multi-modal analysis methods to obtain on-site information such as 
category, position and motion status. The virtual entity layer integrates geometry model, physical model, 
behavior model, and rule model to achieve the synchronization between physical entities and digital 
entities. It also divides construction sites into dangerous zones, warning zones, and safe zones to perform 
collision warnings based on the status of construction robots and workers. The services layer provides 
supervision, motion states monitoring, zone prediction, and data management for the DT system. The 
connections layer establishes the information interconnection and synchronization between these layers. 
Case study is conducted to verify its performance in visualization, motion states monitoring and collision 
warning. It is proven that the proposed method can achieve a prediction accuracy of over 96% for 
dangerous zone s and over 95% for warning zone s. The proposed digital twin -enabled anti -collision 
system can greatly improve the safety for construction sites. 
Keywords: digital twin; collision warning; smart construction

fatalities [1]. As statistics from the Hong Kong government in 202 3, the construction industry has the

Automation and robotics are considered alternatives to alleviate safety concerns in construction [2].

The safety monitoring methods for HRIs have been extensively studied in various industries 
such as manufacturing, construction, and agriculture to improve their efficiency and safety [5,6]. 
Multi-source sensors [7] such as cameras, Bluetooth devices, Inert ial Measurement Units (IMUs), 
and Ultra-wideband (UWB) sensors can be used for data collection in HRIs. RGB-D cameras and UWB 
sensors can be employed for proximity estimation to prevent potential accidents between heavy 
machines and workers. Machine learni ng methods [8] have also been discussed for the processing, 
fusion, and prediction of multi -source data. Computer vision methods [9] have been studied for the 
location, pose estimation, and activity recognition of robots and humans. Intelligent decision -making 
methods such as space partitioning and bounding volume hierarchy have also been studied for collision 
detection of crane -lifts in modular integrated construction [10]. Besides, digital systems [11] that 
combine various sensors have also been designed for proximity estimation, activity recognition, and 
collision warnings in HRIs. Based on predicted collision events, these systems will take corresponding 
actions (e.g., giving warnings to humans, stopping robots, or modifying robots’ paths) to ensure safety.

Although significant achievements have been made, the safety monitoring methods for HRIs in 
complicated and dynamic construction sites remain challenging: 
HRIs with more uncertainties : Compared with the manufacturing industry, HRIs in construction 
sites are characterized by significant uncertainty [12]. In manufacturing workshops, robots are installed 
in standard production lines, where they perform operations efficiently with fixed HRI modes. However, 
in some construction projects (e.g., demolition projects, road projects), robots need to move proactively, 
adjust their postures dynamically, and carry out operations until the projects are completed. Therefore , 
HRIs in construction sites do not follow fixed modes and involve more uncertainties. The comprehensive 
perception of on-site robots and workers is the basis for safety monitoring of HRIs in construction sites. 
High risks of collision: Collision risks in construction are particularly prominent, and collisions with 
construction robots—often heavy machines—are major causes of fatalities [13]. For instance, statistics 
show that in Germany last year, collision -related incidents accounted for 36% of all fatal accidents on 
construction sites. On a global scale, collision-related accidents make up 22% of all construction accidents, 
solidifying collisions as a major contributor to on-site incidents. In road construction projects specifically, 
over 50% of fatal injuries to road workers result from collisions with motor vehicles [14]. Therefore, 
precise anti-collision methods are considered key to improving the safety of HRIs with high risks. 
Complex HRIs system: Although robots can greatly improve the level of automation in construction, 
they also introduce new challenges to HRIs. The occlusion and noise generated by robots can affect 
workers’ perception of the environment, potentially leading to safety hazards. Additionally, there is 
information that workers are unable to perceive, such as the planned motion paths of robots and their 
next actions. As a result, HRI systems are characterized as complex systems that involve the 
synchronization of heterogeneous data, the integration of analysis algorithms, information 
communication between different roles, seamless decision-making, and services for various stakeholders. 
In conclusion, the main objectives of this research are as follows: 
(1) To develop multi-sensor perception and analysis methods which can realize comprehensive 
perception of machines, workers, and construction sites. 
(2) To design on-site collision prediction and warning methods for intelligent decision-making, so 
as to improve the safety of HRIs in construction sites. 
(3) To propose an efficient HRIs system which can realize the seamless integration of the 
“perception, analysis, decision-making, execution”. 
As a rapidly evolving technology, the digital twin (DT) [15] is widely regarded as one of the most 
promising technologies for achieving real -time information synchronization in dynamic and complex 
scenarios. DT refers to a virtual model that provides a com prehensive description of a physical entity, 
enabling the seamless integration of virtual space with physical space. Multi-source data collected from a 
physical entity is analyzed and mapped to the virtual entity for simulation, monitoring, and maintenance [16]. 
DT can also be applied in the safety monitoring system of HRIs, enabling real-time perception and analysis 
between physical and virtual construction sites, as well as timely decision-making and monitoring. 
To achieve these objectives, this paper combines artificial intelligence (AI) technologies and 
digitalization techniques to develop a digital twin -enabled anti-collision system. Firstly, a multi -sensor 
fusion approach is proposed to realize a comprehensive understanding of entities in physical construction 
sites. Besides, a multi -modal analysis model is developed to enable real -time and comprehensive 
monitoring of HRIs in smart construction sites. Moreover, a 5 -Dimensional DT system is designed to

2. Literature review 
This section provides a comprehensive review of recent research works, focusing on collision warning 
methods for HRI, digital twin in smart construction. 
2.1. Collision warning methods for HRI 
Collision early warning in HRI primarily comprises three interconnected components: perception, 
analysis, and decision-making, each playing a critical role in ensuring operational safety. 
Perception serves as the foundational step, involving the collection of environment and object-related 
information through diverse sensing technologies. Ultra -Wideband (UWB) and Global Positioning 
System (GPS) [17] are commonly deployed to obtain real-time location data of objects. Cameras capture 
visual image data, while thermal sensors acquire temperatur e-enhanced images to distinguish humans 
from inanimate objects [18]. Additionally, RGB -D cameras and LiDARs provide 3D point cloud data, 
enabling detailed spatial reconstruction [19]. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are mounted on objects 
to collect moti on parameters such as velocity, acceleration, and angular rates [20]. Wearable devices 
have also been extensively studied for collecting both physical and mental data of workers [21]. 
Analysis focuses on interpreting the collected data to comprehensively understand the motion states 
of interacting entities. This step relies on advanced algorithms, including deep learning and computer vision 
techniques [22], to extract meaningful features. For instance, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are 
designed to classify, detect, and segment workers and robots from images or videos [23]. Temporal 
features are often integrated into Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network to analyze motion 
trajectories and patterns, facilitating activity recognition and continuous trajectory monitoring [24]. 
Moreover, 3D CNN-based methods can detect proximity between robots and workers, a key indicator 
for triggering early collision warnings [25]. 
Decision-making involves risk identification and the formulation of safety actions based on 
analyzed data and predefined rules. Core tasks include collision warning activation, path planning, and 
trajectory adjustment. For example, an integrated octree and bounding box strategy has been applied to 
collision detection in crane-lift path planning, enabling efficient spatial conflict checks [26]. In dynamic 
construction sites, occupied areas of workers and hazardous objects can be mapped onto a lane map, 
with collision warnings triggered by monitoring proximity thresholds. Collision warning rules have 
also been studied as varied scenario and application requirements , including static distance (SD) rules, 
time-to-collision (TTC) rules, spatial occupancy (SO) rules. The SD rules are a common approach in 
industrial settings where a fixed safety threshold (e.g., 2 meters) is defined . Warnings are given when 
the distance between a robot and a worker falls below this threshold [2 7]. The TTC rules are used in 
Smart Constr. Article 
5 
high-speed environments (e.g., autonomous forklifts). TTC calculates the time until a potential collision 
based on relative velocity and distance, with warnings adjusted dynamically (e.g., an alert is triggered when 
TTC < 3 seconds) [28]. The SO rules are also used in confined spaces, such as construction tunnels. Here, 
warnings are activated when a worker enters a predefined “dangerous zone” around a robot regardless of 
distance, so as to account for limited working space [29]. Furthermore, uncertainty factors (e.g., sensor noise, 
sudden human movements) are increasingly incorporated into decision -making models to enhance 
accuracy [30]. Knowledge graph methods have also been widely adopted for risk identification and safety 
management, enabling structured reasoning about complex interaction scenarios [31]. This multi-layered 
framework highlights the importance of aligning perception accuracy, analytical depth, and context-specific 
warning rules to address the dynamic and uncertain nature of HRI environments. 
2.2. Digital twin in smart construction 
With the rapid development of information technology, digital systems [32,33] such as cyber-physical 
system (CPS), edge-cloud system, and DT system have been widely applied in the construction industry. 
Cyber-physical synchronization between the ongoing construction progress and building 
information models (BIM) can be realized via the Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, and 5G 
technology, allowing stakeholders to share on-site information and make more efficient decisions [34]. 
Besides, multi-dimensional information can also be integrated with BIM platforms for various tasks such 
as scheduling, planning, cost estimation, and daily storage. This provides more comprehensive and 
extensive information synchronization and integration for construction projects [3 5]. Liu et al . [ 36] 
developed a CPS for real-time monitoring and visualization of greenhouse gas emissions in prefabricated 
construction. The operating states of cranes, transfer vehicles, and construction machines are monitored 
using acceleration sensors, GPS, and Wi -Fi to calculate greenhouse gas emissions. Machine model 
numbers, quantities, power, and greenhouse gas emission factors are stored in the cyber database. 
Furthermore, workers can also be incorporated to design human -cyber-physical systems (HCPS) [3 7], 
aiming to improve workers safety and well-being. 
DT systems [38] are digital representations of physical systems that possess the same properties 
and synchronized information as real -world entities . DT systems can be used for full lifecycle 
operations, simulations, and management, ultimately enabling better decision making. The 
applications of DT in construc tion have also been widely studied. Five-dimensional digital twin models 
are utilized for closed-loop control of the “hanging-adjusting angles-fine positioning-piling” process to 
avoid collisions [39]. The biggest advantage of DT is its ability to integrate with various technologies 
including AI, cloud computing , machine learning, and communication technologies to realize more 
accurate status monitoring and information synchronization. Facilitated by these advanced technologies, 
physical entities can be coordinated with virtual entities to improve the precision of automatic assembly 
of bricks and components with the collaboration of DT systems [40]. In practical applications, the 
accuracy and real-time performance of DT models depend on sensor technology and data transmission 
efficiency. As construction progresses and the environment changes, how DT models can efficiently 
adapt to and reflect the situation remains a major challenge. Furthermore, integrating safety rules and 
mechanisms into DT models to address the significant uncertainties in the construction process is also a 
key focus in the design of DT systems [41].

2.3. Research gaps and motivations 
Although great achievements have been made in AI technologies and digital systems, several research 
gaps for HRIs collision warning in construction sites still remains. 
Firstly, the collaborative analysis of multi -modal data for HRI collision warning is still limited. 
While current studies can achieve location tracking, pose estimation, and activity recognition of on-site 
objects, they fail to fully leverage multi -modal data from diverse sensors. This deficiency hinders a 
comprehensive understanding of on-site entities, particularly in addressing blind spots, which often results 
in workers failing to receive warnings about impending collisions. Without integrated multi-sensor data to 
realize comprehensive perception and address blind spots of construction sites , the accuracy and 
timeliness of collision warnings will be significantly compromised. 
Secondly, most existing research has focused on conceptual investigations of DT, with insufficient 
exploration into the anti -collision system . A DT model capable of realizing closed -loop control 
encompassing perception, analysis, collision warning and control remains underdeveloped. Such a model 
is vital for dynamically responding to dangers, including sudden movements near blind spots, and ensuring 
that warning information is not only generated but also effectively transmitted to workers in real time. 
Thirdly, there is a lack of robust digitalization systems specifically tailored for HRI collision 
warning in construction sites. In practical applications, the integration of DT with other technologies 
faces issues such as compatibility and data security. Barriers exist in data interaction and sharing between 
different technical platforms, restricting their wider and deeper application in smart construction. 
Additionally, current research on maintenance and update mechanisms for DT during long-term construction 
processes is inadequate. As construction progresses and environments change, DT models struggle to adapt 
and reflect actual conditions quickly and effectively, impacting their continuous functionality. 
Therefore, this paper presents a digital twin -enabled anti-collision system designed to efficiently 
integrate the “perception, analysis, decision -making, and action ” cycle for HRI. By addressing these 
gaps, especially for multi-modal data integration and closed -loop control , this system aims to 
significantly improve construction safety.

3.1. Physical entity layer 
The PE layer is the foundation of the DT system, which is responsible for the perception and control of 
on-site entities. PE consists of three parts: smart entities, data acquisition and transmission system, and 
smart construction site. 
3.1.1. Smart entities

facilities. Their real -time IDs, categories, statuses, positions, and working information will be 
synchronized to the DT system. Smart entities share and visualize their information within the DT 
system, as well as receiving and executing control instructions from the DT system. 
Smart entities can be divided into two types: passive entities and active entities. Passive entities 
typically have a specific function and do not actively move, such as fences and warning signs found in 
construction sites. Key information of passive entities can be sensed and uploaded to the DT system for 
simulation, including their ID, categories, positions, and dimensions. Active entities encompass workers

3.1.2. Data acquisition and transmission system 
Data Acquisition and Transmission System (DATS) is deployed in construction site to collect on-site 
information from smart entities, as well as to facilitate the real-time transmission and synchronization 
of on-site information and control instructions between the PE and the DT system. DATS utilizes smart 
sensors such as cameras, IMU sensors, and RGB-D cameras for on-site information collection, enabling 
comprehensive perception of smart entities in construction sites. Besides, DATS also utilizes wireless 
communication technologies such as Bluetooth and Wi -Fi to achieve communication and interaction 
between different smart entities in the PE layer, as well as betwee n the PE layer and different layers in 
the DT system. Which provides the foundation for achieving real-time visibility, synchronization, and 
control between the PE layer and the DT system. 
3.1.3. Smart construction site 
The smart construction site is the integration of smart entities and construction environment , which is 
responsible for modeling th ese smart on-site entities into an integrated system. The collected on -site 
information about construction machines, workers, and other entities will be considered as a group and 
uploaded to the DT system for analysis. Based on the perception and analysis of each on-site smart 
entity, the interactive relationships between each smart entity will be established to monitor the safety 
of HRIs in construction sites. Moreover, the smart construction site will also be synchronized within the 
DT system for visualization and further analysis. The automatic perception and remote monitoring in 
the smart construction sites can effectively prevent collisions and improve the construction safety. 
3.2. Digital data layer 
The DD layer plays the role of analysis in the DT system. It mainly consists of three parts: multi-modal 
physical data, multi-modal analysis model and virtual data. The multi-modal physical data is collected from 
PE layer in the construction sites. The multi-modal analysis model can accurately analyze multi-modal 
physical data to generate virtual data that can comprehensively describe the HRIs status. The analyzed 
virtual data is utilized to drive the virtual entity in the DT system , so as to achieve the information 
synchronization between digital space and physical space. 
3.2.1. Multi-modal physical data 
The multi -modal physical data is collected to realize the comprehensive perception of machines and 
workers in construction sites. RGB -D camera is utilized to capture both the 2D images and 3D depth 
images, which are respectively used to represent the absol ute positions of on -site objects in 2D space 
and 3D space. They can also be fused. The RGB pixel values of 2D image are assigned to depth image

3.2.3. Virtual data 
The categories, bounding boxes, and key points detected from the multi-modal analysis model for on-site 
machines and workers are considered as virtual data. This data will be transferred to virtual entity layer 
and used to drive the motion of virtual models. 
In addition, the DD layer also includes more types of data, such as algorithms, detection models, 
performance data from the SS layer, and historical performance data from the PE layer, simulation data 
and historical decisions from the VE layer. All the data in the DT system will be stored in the DD layer. 
By continuously interacting between physical entities and virtual entities, more data can be obtained to 
construct a more trustworthy DT system. 
3.3. Virtual entity layer 
The VE layer facilitates the integration of physical information and virtual information in the DT system. 
It consists of a geometry model, a physical model, a behavior model, and a rule model, which are 
responsible for decision-making. 
3.3.1. Geometry model 
In the DT system, geometry models are utilized to represent the geometric features (shape, size, position) 
and relationships (assembly) of physical entities. Detailed construction machines, facilities, and standard 
worker models will be generated in respective positions of construction sites. This provides geometry 
information of physical entities , allowing for the simulation, analysis, and prediction of HRI s in 
construction sites within the digital environment. Generally, AutoCAD, SolidWorks, 3D Max tools are 
employed for the 2D and 3D modeling. 
3.3.2. Physical model 
The physical model incorporates physical properties such as constraints, material, gravity, and joints to 
the geometry model. In this research, Unity is utilized to simulate physical behavior and scenes. Unity 
can set objects as rigid bodies and connect them using joint s system, enabling the simulation of the 
physical structure, mechanical systems, motion, and constraints of construction machines. In addition, 
Unity also provides a robust collision detection system that can simulate contact, collisions, and 
corresponding events. Unity will be used to conduct process simulation, behavior simulation, evaluation, 
analysis, simulation, and other activities of the DT system. 
3.3.3. Behavior model 
In the DT system, behavior model refers to a model that simulates the behavior, operations, and function 
of physical system entities. Behavior models are constructed based on physical principles and 
algorithms. The behavior model (BM) utilizes accurate physical principles to predict the movement and 
changes of each entity from this second to the next, serving as feedback for decisions of the DT system.

3.3.4. Rule model 
The Rule Model evaluates the collision risks of HRI s to make decisions using both TTC and SO 
strategies. As the “Code of Practice for Safe Use of Construction Machines” issued by the Labour 
Department of Hong Kong, a minimum clear distance of 600 mm should be maintained between working 
machines and fencing. Besides, construction sites also implement additional warning distances to ensure 
safety during operations. Therefore, a safe model divides the construction site into three zones: the 
dangerous zone, the warning zone, and the safe zone. Construction workers are only allowed to work in 
the safe zones, different levels collision warning will be trigg ered if any personnel enter the dangerous 
zone or the warning zone. 
In this research, the excavator is used as the subject for the zone division of HRIs in construction 
site. During the operation, its movement mainly involves two parts: the body path and the trajectory of the 
arm, boom, and bucket. Both the moving body and working components (arm, boom, bucket) are considered 
in zone division. Notably, unlike conventional rule -based models that often restrict collision warning 
calculations to 2D planes, this research extends the spatial scope to a 3D workspace. To achieve this, the 
excavator 3D bounding box is first detected, which fully encompasses its body, arm, boom, and bucket in 
3D space. Based on this 3D detection, dangerous, warning, and safe zones on construction sites are predicted, 
as illustrated in Fig ure 6. This 3D expansion enables more accurate and realistic zone delineation by 
accounting for vertical height differences (e.g., boom and bucket lifting/lowering)—critical for avoiding 
collisions in complex construction environments.

3.4. Services layer 
The SS layer provides is responsible for the execution in the DT system. It receives data, analysis, and 
decisions from the DT system and offers visualization services for all users. Site managers can monitor 
the detailed status of the construction site in the SS layer, so as to review historical decisions, adjust 
algorithms, and train models. The services contain supervision, zone prediction, collision warning , 
motion monitoring, and historical data management. These services can be provided to all users (clients, 
site manager, workers) through website. The layout of service website is shown in the Figure 7. 
Smart Constr. Article 
15 
 
Figure 7. The layout of service website. 
The supervision provides on-site information such as the categories, quantities, and positions of on-site 
objects. This can help site managers quickly understand the information of the construction site. 
The zone prediction visually presents the dangerous zones, warning zones, and safe zones of the 
construction site, enabling all users to see the zones where workers are located. These zones can also be 
adjusted by users based on actual conditions. Additionally, collision warnings can be implemented by 
comparing the positions of workers with dangerous zones and warning zones in the future. 
Motion monitoring provides detailed records of the motion status of construction machines, which 
includes absolute and relative positions such as acceleration, velocity, angle, and angular velocity. Not 
only site managers can have a detailed understanding of the motion status of construction machines, but 
operators can also have knowledge about the absolute position of machines in construction site as well 
as their relative positions to surrounding objects. 
The historical data management handles various data from all layers, including models, algorithms, 
decisions, simulations, and detection results. Managers can analyze the motion status, simulation results 
and detection results of the DT system at any time. When errors are found in the simulation, 
modifications can be made to the geometry model and physical model of VE layer. When false collision 
warning happens, the behavior model and rule model should be adjusted. Additionally, the multi-modal 
analysis algorithms in the DD layer can also be optimized as detection results. Specific data can be 
selected to for the training of new detection model. 
3.5. Connections 
CNs are responsible for the interconnection and information sharing between the PE layer, DD layer, 
VE layer, and SS layer, as shown in Figure 8. Therefore, the closed-loop control of “perception, analysis, 
decision-making, execution” in the DT system can be achieved. 

Smart Constr. Article 
16 
 
Figure 8. The connections for the DT system. 
(1) Connections between the PE layer and other layers can enable accurate perception and 
information synchronization in the DT system. The static data and dynamic data from the PE layer will 
be used for data analysis, model driving, and decision execution. Moreover, the simulation results and 
decisions will be transmitted back to the PE layer for dynamic control, analysis, and evaluation. 
(2) Connections between the DD layer to other layers support real-time storage and analysis in the 
DT system. The on-site data from the PE layer will be stored and analyzed in the DD layer. Besides, all 
data such as detection model, decisions and operation data will be continuously accumulated in the DD layer. 
These historical data stored in the DD layer can also be used to optimize the operations in the PE layer. 
(3) Connections between the SS layer to other layers can provide visualization and execution for the 
DT system. The detailed on-site information from the PE layer, the VE layer and the DD layer will be 
visualized in the SS layer. Besides, the decisions and solutions will also be executed in the SS layer. The 
SS layer can analyze the performance of decisions and models, thereby selecting suitable solutions for 
optimizing the performance of the PE layer. 
(4) Connections between the VE layer and other layers can enable the physical-virtual synchronization 
of the DT system. The static data and dynamic data from the PE layer and the DD layer will be utilized to 
drive the digital models in the VE layer. Simulation results and decisions from the VE layer will be sent back 
to the PE layer and the SS layer for visualization and dynamic control in the DT system.

5. Conclusion and future work 
In this research, a digital twin -enabled anti -collision system is designed to improve the safety for 
human-robot interactions in smart construction sites . This research has the following scientific 
contributions. (1) Multi-sensor fusion approach and multi-modal analysis model are developed to realize 
a comprehensive understanding of physical entities construction sites. (2) Geometry models, physical 
models, rule models and behavior models are designed to realize precise synchronization and collision 
warning in virtual space. (3) A 5D digital-twin enabled anti-collision system is proposed to achieve the 
closed-loop optimization of “perception, analysis, decision -making, execution” for HRIs in smart 
construction sites. 
Although great progress has been achieved, there are still some limitations to be addressed in the 
future. When dangerous or warning zones are misjudged as safe ones, the system lacks a dynamic 
response mechanism (such as secondary verification, hierarchical emergency strategy) which may lead 
to hidden safety hazards being covered up. In addition, the “black box” nature of deep learning models 
will increase the uncontrollability of misjudgments, and the limitations of sensor s may also affect the 
recognition accuracy. To solve the above challenges, future work will focus on the following directions: 
(1) Improve the misjudgment response mechanism: Design a dynamic verification module. When 
the system judges a zone as safe, it will automatically trace back the sensor data and model reasoning 
process. If abnormal features are found, secondary monitoring will be triggered. At the same time, 
establish a multi -level response strategy, dynamically adjust the warning levels according to the 
misjudgment probability, and push manual review requests when necessary. 
(2) Expand sensors and improve model interpretability: Introduce infrared thermal sensors, 
ultra-wideband sensors, and lidar to enhance the accuracy of entity recognition . Besides, attention 
mechanisms, model distillation and other technologies can also be studied to parse the decision logic of 
deep learning networks and reduce the “black box” risk. 
(3) Strengthen system reliability: In response to the high safety requirements of the construction industry, 
optimize the real-time performance and robustness of the DT system, improve the anti-interference ability 
in complex scenarios through edge computing and cloud collaboration, and ensure the consistency of 
virtual-real mapping and the accuracy of early warning.
