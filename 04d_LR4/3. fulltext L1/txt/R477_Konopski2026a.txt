Abstract
This study presents a comparative analysis of six DFS implementations representing differ-
ent maturity levels and investigates the systemic gap between technological capabilities
and regulatory approaches. A structured narrative review with case-based analysis was
conducted using the Scopus database (2015–2026) with six targeted queries. The case
selection followed the PICo protocol. An original ten-criterion DFS maturity assessment
rubric—grounded in the Technology Readiness Level (TRL), Integration Readiness Level
(IRL), and Digital Twin Maturity Model frameworks—was applied to all six cases. Inter-
rater validation yielded substantial agreement (κw = 0.797; unweighted κ = 0.674 [95% CI:
0.509, 0.839]). The results indicate a clear maturity gradient (Dimension X: 4–9 points;
Dimension Y: 2–8 points). Benefits reported in the analysed primary studies include up to a
55 s reduction in evacuation time, a 72% improvement compared with static signage, and a
34-percentage-point increase in evacuation success rate under simulation-based conditions.
Five normative recommendations are proposed to address the structural regulatory gap
between current prescriptive frameworks and DFS deployment in Poland and the EU. This
study argues that prescriptive rules should remain the baseline, whereas complex facilities
may adopt performance-based DFS solutions, provided that equivalence to conventional
protection levels is rigorously demonstrated. From a sustainability perspective, the study
frames DFS as a dynamic safety layer that supports occupant protection, operational re-
silience, and lifecycle adaptability in complex buildings exposed to uncertain fire and
crowd conditions.

1. Introduction
1.1. Digital T ransformation of Fire Safety
Contemporary buildings function as complex technical systems in which fire alarm
systems (FASs), voice alarm systems (VASs), building management systems (BMSs), smoke
Sustainability 2026, 18, 5920 https://doi.org/10.3390/su18125920
Sustainability 2026, 18, 5920 2 of 29
extraction ventilation, and video surveillance are expected to operate as a single coherent
entity during an emergency. The traditional RBS paradigm was founded on static fire
scenarios developed in the design stage—documents that are rarely revised after the
building is handed over—while the actual behaviour of the building during a fire incident
progressively diverges from the original design assumptions.
Building information modelling (BIM) has become an established design standard;
however, once a building is handed over, the BIM model is typically no longer maintained.
This is particularly important because BIM was originally conceived as a lifecycle-oriented
approach that can support planning, design, construction, and facility operations within
a single digital model [1]. This limitation is consistent with the broader literature, which
shows that the implementation of BIM in existing buildings remains constrained by the
high effort required for model creation, the difficulty of updating information, and the un-
certainty of available building data [2]. The proliferation of Internet of Things devices and
continuous operational data streams has created the conditions necessary for the concept
of a living Digital Twin (DT)—a model continuously fed by sensor data. Integration of BIM
with IoT technologies enables real-time data exchange between a physical asset and its
digital representation, forming the technological foundation of Digital Twin systems [3].
This trend is consistent with recent review research showing that IoT-enabled fire safety sys-
tems increasingly integrate detection, localisation, and evacuation functions through sensor
networks, real-time data handling, and advanced analytics [4]. Artificial intelligence adds
predictive capability and dynamic evacuation routing. Recent studies have demonstrated
that intelligent evacuation guidance systems can dynamically generate safer and faster
routes in large buildings by combining hazard intensity, congestion data, and distributed
intelligent indicators [5]. This observation is consistent with recent research showing that
digital twins can support building safety throughout the lifecycle by integrating sensors,
3D visualisation, and data analytics for threat detection and monitoring [ 6]. Together,
these technologies constitute a responsive architecture: a building that actively responds to
hazards rather than passively awaiting the activation of predefined static scenarios. This
fragmentation of systems becomes particularly problematic under emergency conditions,
where coordinated and timely decision-making is critical to ensure occupant safety.
The tension between technological capability and regulatory constraints constitutes a
critical and insufficiently addressed gap in contemporary fire safety engineering, particu-
larly in the context of integrating real-time data-driven evacuation systems into existing
prescriptive regulatory frameworks. This gap is particularly consequential in complex and
high-occupancy buildings, where static evacuation assumptions may not reflect dynami-
cally evolving fire conditions and occupant behaviour.
The sustainability dimension of this problem should not be reduced to environmental
indicators alone. In complex buildings, sustainable performance also includes the capacity
of the built environment to maintain safe, reliable, and adaptable operation throughout its
life cycle. From this perspective, dynamic fire safety systems contribute to sustainability by
transforming static fire safety provisions into an operational layer capable of responding
to changing hazard development, occupant distribution, and system conditions. Such an
approach links human-centred safety, resilience, and adaptive facility management within
a performance-oriented framework for sustainable building operation.
1.2. Objective, Scope, and Research Questions
Despite rapid technological advances, there is a lack of structured comparative analy-
ses that link the maturity, operational performance, and regulatory applicability of DFS
solutions. This study aims to demonstrate that DFS solutions are already being tested
or deployed in existing buildings and built infrastructure and to identify the structural
https://doi.org/10.3390/su18125920
Sustainability 2026, 18, 5920 3 of 29
regulatory discrepancies that impede their wider adoption. Three research questions were
formulated: (RQ1) How does the sensor → model → decision → actuator chain function
in the six cases analysed? (RQ2) Which technological components are essential for a func-
tioning DFS? (RQ3) How can DFS solutions be transposed into the Polish legal system?
The original contribution of this study is the development of a structured DFS maturity
assessment rubric and its application to real-world case studies, enabling a systematic
comparison between technological integration and operational effectiveness in the context
of regulatory constraints. By doing so, the study also positions DFS maturity assessment
as a tool for evaluating how digital fire safety systems may contribute to sustainability-
oriented building performance through resilience, adaptive operation, and improved life
safety reliability.

2.1. The DFS Concept
The DFS constitutes a closed-loop cyber–physical architecture composed of six inter-
connected layers [7], as illustrated in Figure 1.

2.2. BIM and Digital Twins in Fire Safety Engineering
Recent studies indicate a clear transition in fire safety engineering from static,
document-based approaches to integrated, data-driven decision environments. Almatared
et al. [ 7] emphasised that conventional fire safety management still suffers from frag-
mented information, limited interoperability, and insufficient real-time support, while
digital twin-based frameworks can improve coordination between BIM, IoT, AI, and facility
management processes. Lin et al. [8] further show that the evolution of emergency evacua-
tion research increasingly relies on digital twins that integrate buildings, occupants, sensing,
simulation, and decision-making within a continuous feedback loop. Furthermore, Zhang
et al. [9] demonstrated that AIoT-enabled digital twin systems can extend this concept to
operational fire safety management by supporting real-time fire recognition, prediction,
visualisation, and emergency response.
Against this background, Tables 1 and 2 synthesise the main differences between
traditional Rule-Based Safety (RBS) and dynamic fire safety systems (DFSs), as well as
between successive digital modelling approaches, from static BIM to living Digital Twins.
The comparison highlights a consistent pattern reported in the literature: as integration,
real-time data exchange, and adaptive control increase, the practical relevance of the model
for evacuation support and fire safety management increases. This distinction is consistent
with studies showing that dynamic personnel guidance can improve evacuation efficiency
compared to static signage [10].
Within this framework, real-time multimodal sensor fusion can be understood as
an enabling mechanism of the six-layer DFS architecture because it supports the integra-
tion of thermal, optical, gas, and occupancy data streams within a unified digital model;
moreover, it may improve fire localisation accuracy, situational awareness, and evacuation
decision latency.

2.3. Paradigm Shift: RBS → PBD → DFS
Meacham [11–13] identified the asymmetry of expertise between FSE practitioners and
regulatory bodies as the primary institutional barrier to performance-based design (PBD):
engineers possess considerably greater technical knowledge than the regulators responsible
for approving their submissions. Chow et al. [ 14], employing the Cynefin framework,
demonstrate that prescriptive requirements are applied mechanically, without a critical
assessment of their suitability to specific building configurations. This critique is consis-
tent with recent simulation-based research showing that an overreliance on prescriptive
evacuation definitions can reduce the effectiveness of the design and lead to exit capacity
provisions that are more conservative than necessary under comparable conditions [15].
W˛ egrzy ´ nski and Sulik [16], writing in the Polish academic context, document how the
existing legal framework itself impedes the diffusion of FSE advances into professional
practice. A similar relationship between regulatory evolution and the development of
fire engineering practice has also been documented in other national contexts, including
China [17]. Abualdenien et al. [18] note that FSE was one of the disciplines that remained
long outside the integrated digital approaches, despite building fire safety being fundamen-
tal to building function. This larger shift is also reflected in recent bibliographic research
showing that machine learning in fire safety is increasingly applied to evacuation, early
warning, fire detection, and predictive risk assessment in the built environment [19].
3. Research Methodology
3.1. Study Design and the PICo Framework
This study employs a structured narrative review with a case-based analysis method-
ology . The searches were conducted in Scopus using six targeted query strings (2015–2026).
The selection of cases followed the PICo framework: Population—buildings of occupancy
categories ZL I–III (Polish classification) or critical infrastructure; Interest—DFS implementa-
tions that incorporate at least two components of the set BIM/DT/IoT/AI/dynamic signage;
Context—the structural tension between the RBS and PBD/DFS paradigms; andOutcomes—a
systemic gap matrix, evidence of DFS effectiveness, and normative recommendations. To
ensure reproducibility , the six Scopus query strings used in the literature search are listed in

Table A1 (Appendix A). The total number of records retrieved was approximately 81 unique
records (after deduplication of~147 raw results in all six strings); 27 met the PICo inclusion
criteria in the title/abstract screening; six case studies were selected for full-text analysis based
on the presence of at least two components of DFS, the availability of quantitative performance
data, and peer-reviewed publication status. This selection process is summarised in Figure A1
(Appendix A). The methodology adopted in this study is illustrated in Figure 2. The structured
narrative review methodology was selected over a full systematic review Torgerson [20] on
the basis of methodological fit: the study objective is conceptual framework development and
comparative case analysis rather than effect estimation in a homogeneous pool of interventions.
Accordingly , the objective of the search strategy was not to identify and quantitatively syn-
thesise all publications on BIM, Digital Twins, IoT, AI, or evacuation modelling in fire safety .
Instead, the searches were designed to identify a focused set of information-rich, peer-reviewed
implementations in which at least two DFS components were sufficiently documented to allow
reconstruction of the sensor–model–decision–actuation chain. The six included cases should
therefore be interpreted as analytically selected exemplars representing different maturity levels
of DFS development, rather than as a statistically representative sample of the entire research
field. This approach is consistent with established practice in fire safety engineering, where
frameworks are developed through structured case comparison rather than meta-analytic
aggregation Almatared et al. [7]; Chow et al. [14]; Nasery et al. [21]; Wang et al. [22].

3.2. DFS Maturity Assessment Rubric—Scoring Criteria and Methodological Foundations
Purpose and general structure: The DFS maturity assessment rubric (Table 3) was
developed as an original instrument by synthesising the established frameworks of Tech-
nology Readiness Levels (TRLs), Integration Readiness Levels (IRLs), Systems Readiness
Levels (SRLs), and the Digital Twin Maturity Model. Each scoring threshold (0 = absent,
1 = partial, 2 = full/mature) was operationalised with explicit definitions directly linked
to the implications of fire safety performance. For example, a score of 2 in X5 (physical
actuation) requires automated, immediate control of dynamic exit signs without opera-
tor intervention—a feature that directly reduces Required Safe Egress Time (RSET) and
prevents occupants from being directed toward routes that have lost tenability. Similarly,
a score of 2 in Y2 (response latency) requires a system reaction time of ≤10 s, which is
consistent with the ASET margins documented in the analysed case studies; a score of 2
in Y4 (validation scope) corresponds to full-scale physical validation, reflecting the TRL
7–8 threshold for regulatory acceptance of non-standard fire safety solutions. The rubric
was developed, applied, and interpreted by Przemysław Konopski. This limitation is
methodologically significant and is discussed in Section 3.3.
The methodological basis of the rubric combines Technology Readiness Level
logic [23,24], Integration and System Readiness Level concepts [ 25], and Digital Twin
maturity modelling [26].
The full scoring matrix is presented in Table 3. Each criterion has explicitly defined
thresholds for each of the three possible scores (0, 1, 2). Table 3 provides complete oper-
ationalisations together with methodological justification that refers to TRL, IRL, or the
Digital Twin Maturity Model. Dimension X captures the level of technological integration
(sensing, modelling, analytics, decision-making, and actuation), while Dimension Y reflects
operational performance (timing, latency, feedback, validation, and autonomy).
Each criterion is evaluated independently using explicitly defined thresholds corre-
sponding to absent (0), partial (1), or full/mature (2) implementation. The physical meaning
of each threshold is anchored to fire safety performance requirements: a score of 2 on X1
requires a sensor density sufficient to localise fire events within 10 s; a score of 2 on X5
requires automated actuation without operator intervention; a score of 2 on Y2 corresponds
to a system reaction time of 10 s or less, which is consistent with ASET margins in the
analysed cases; a score of 2 on Y4 requires full-scale physical validation, reflecting the TRL
7–8 threshold for regulatory acceptance of non-standard fire safety solutions.
Following joint discussion of all 12 discrepancies in the assessments, the primary
rater’s scores (P .K.) were retained in all cases, as the source descriptions did not provide
sufficient evidence to confirm the higher score assigned by the second rater. The consensus
discussion was conducted as an evidence-tracing procedure rather than as a numerical
averaging exercise. For each of the 12 discrepant scoring cells, both raters returned to
the relevant primary source and compared the reported technical description with the
operational thresholds defined in Table 3. A higher score was retained only when the
source explicitly documented the required functionality, validation level, response latency,
or actuation mechanism. Where the available description was ambiguous or did not
directly confirm the higher threshold, the more conservative score was maintained. This
explains why all discrepant cases were ultimately resolved in favour of the primary rater:
not because the second assessment was disregarded, but because the available source
evidence did not justify increasing the score under the predefined rubric criteria. The

inter-rater study thus serves as a validation of the rubric instrument rather than a basis for

The inter-rater reliability of the DFS maturity assessment rubric was tested by two
independent experts (P .K. and W.B.) on all 60 scoring cells. Exact agreement was achieved
in 48 cells (80.0%). These results confirm the acceptable to good reliability of the rubric,
although some subjectivity remains in borderline cases, particularly regarding the degree
of physical actuation (X5) and real-time analytics (X3). The obtained level of agreement
supports the use of the rubric as a structured comparative tool, while highlighting the need
for further refinement and broader multi-rater validation in future studies.
Dimension Y (Operational Level)—alignment with TRL: Criteria Y1–Y5 are concep-
tually aligned with the Technology Readiness Level (TRL) scale introduced by Mankins [23].
TRL is a nine-level ordinal scale ranging from TRL 1 (observation of basic principles) to TRL
9 (system proven in an operational environment). Héder [24] documents its evolution from
a NASA instrument to a standard of the European Commission in Horizon Europe research
programs. The central dimension of TRL progression is the validation environment and the
degree of system autonomy, which is precisely what the criteria Y4 (validation scope) and
Y5 (system autonomy) measure. TRL is not applied directly in this study; rather, its logic is
adapted to the DFS domain: the three-level scale 0–1–2 replaces the nine-level TRL scale,
and the criteria are tailored to fire safety system specifics.
Dimension X (Technological Integration)—alignment with IRL and SRL: Whereas
TRL assesses the maturity of a single technology, the DFS is by definition a multi-component
system. Sauser et al. [ 25] proposed the Integration Readiness Level (IRL), a scale that
evaluates the degree to which two or more technologies have been successfully integrated
into a functioning subsystem. The combination of TRL and IRL yields the system readiness
level (SRL), defined as the normalised product of TRL and IRL. SRL measures the overall
maturity of a composite system, accounting for both the maturity of the components
and the quality of their mutual integration. Criteria X1–X5—covering sensing, building
model, analytics/AI, decision algorithm, and physical actuation—operationalise the IRL
logic: each criterion measures not the mere presence of a component, but the depth of its
integration with the remaining DFS layers. A system with advanced IoT sensors (X1 = 2)
but no physical actuation (X5 = 0), such as the Lille case, obtains a low total X = 4, which
is methodologically consistent with IRL: technology without system integration does not
constitute a mature solution.
Digital Twin Maturity Models: Zhuang et al. [26] define five levels of DT maturity:
(1) geometric, (2) informational, (3) integrated sensor-data, (4) predictive, and (5) au-
tonomous closed-loop. Criteria X2 (building model) and Y3 (feedback loop) directly
correspond to this progression: X2 = 0 represents Zhuang’s Levels 1–2; X2 = 2 combined
with Y3 = 2 corresponds to Levels 4–5. Among the six cases analysed, only the study by
Zhang et al. [9] reaches level 5, and only at the demonstration stage of development.
The reliability between raters was assessed by a co-author (W. Bonenberg), who inde-
pendently applied the rubric to all six case studies based on condensed case descriptions,
without prior knowledge of the primary rater’s scores. Cohen’s kappa was calculated in all
60 criterion–case assessments. The unweighted kappa was κ = 0.674 [95% CI: 0.509, 0.839],
indicating substantial agreement. The quadratic weighted kappa, which is appropriate for
ordinal scales, was κw = 0.797, also indicating substantial agreement.

3.3. Methodological Limitations and Source T ransparency
Limitations of the rubric: The DFS maturity assessment rubric (Tables 3 and 4) is an
original instrument proposed in this paper. Its three principal limitations are: (1) inter-rater
validation was subsequently performed by co-author W. Bonenberg, yielding unweighted
Cohen’s kappa = 0.674 [95% CI: 0.509, 0.839] and quadratic weighted kappa = 0.797, both
indicating substantial agreement; the original scores were retained after joint discussion
of all 12 disagreements; (2) subjectivity in the interpretation of technical descriptions in
the cited publications—identical data may be interpreted differently by different raters
given specific threshold criteria; and (3) limited sample size—six cases are insufficient to
assess the statistical distribution of scores or to test the internal consistency of the scale.
These limitations do not invalidate the rubric’s utility as a comparative tool; however, they
necessitate caution in extrapolating the findings.
Source transparency and data status differentiation. In this paper, three cate-
gories of claims are used, each carrying a distinct epistemological status that must be
clearly distinguished:

Absence of original calculations and simulations: This paper is a structured narra-
tive review with case-based analysis—it does not include original fire experiments, FDS
simulations, or evacuation drills. No quantitative meta-analysis was conducted due to the
methodological heterogeneity of the cases analysed (different types of buildings, different
definitions of the success of evacuation, and different test conditions). The contribution
of this paper lies in qualitative synthesis and structured comparison—a recognised and
appropriate approach within the structured narrative review with case-based analysis
methodology, although one that requires explicit acknowledgement of the boundaries of
inference. This approach is consistent with established fire safety risk index methods, in
which hazard and protection-related factors are aggregated into a structured evaluative
framework to support comparative decision-making [ 32]. An additional limitation con-
cerns the heterogeneity of the case studies analysed, including differences in building
types, evacuation conditions, and definitions of performance metrics. This limits the direct
comparability of quantitative results and reinforces the need to interpret results as indica-
tive rather than definitive. Furthermore, the absence of standardised evaluation protocols
for DFSs introduces uncertainty in cross-case comparisons and highlights the need for
future methodological harmonisation. Consequently, the findings should not be read as a
prevalence estimate of DFS adoption in the global literature, but as a structured comparison
of selected cases that illustrate the technological and regulatory conditions under which
DFS-oriented solutions may emerge.

equivalent was 6–7 for the actuation layer. The absence of a building model layer (X2 = 0)
has direct implications for system reliability: the IDES system operates as an open-loop
actuation chain in the modelling dimension, with sign decisions driven exclusively by
threshold exceedance in a fixed sensor-to-sign mapping, without any spatial model of
tunnel geometry, fire propagation, or occupant distribution. This means the same sign
pattern is activated regardless of fire location, scale, or propagation direction, constraining
routing adaptability. Scaling to an operational tunnel would require, at a minimum, a static
geometric model (X2 ≥ 1) to enable location-aware routing decisions. The IDES case thus
confirms the IRL logic of the rubric: high actuation capability (X5 = 2) without modelling
integration does not constitute a mature DFS solution.
The IDES Tunnel case Wong et al. [ 31]) achieved a relatively high actuation score
but a lower technological integration score (X = 6; X2 = 0, X3 = 1), mainly due to the
complete absence of a building model and limited analytics. This highlights an important
architectural insight: while a geometric digital model significantly improves the reliability
and decision accuracy—enabling location-aware routing and feedback-driven adaptation—
a lightweight and actuation-focused system can still deliver strong operational performance
(Y = 7) in terms of sign-change latency (<0.1 s) and automatic reversion logic. The IDES case
thus demonstrates that different DFS maturity profiles may be appropriate for different
operational contexts and resource constraints: a full DFS stack (X ≥ 8, Y ≥ 7) is warranted
for complex multi-floor buildings, whereas a simplified actuation layer may be a viable
first implementation step for linear infrastructure, such as road tunnels.
4.7. Cross-Case Synthesis
The rubric (Tables 3 and 4; Figure 3) confirms a maturity gradient of X = 4–9, Y = 2–8.
Three analytically significant observations emerge:
Physical action (X5) appears to be the strongest predictor of Dimension Y within
the analysed cases: its absence directly reduces Y1 and Y5 (IRL logic—without C2P loop,
the system cannot be operationally functional).
Closed-loop feedback (Y3) is the least implemented layer—scoring 0 or 1 in five of
the six cases—corresponding to Levels 3–4 of Zhuang’s DT Maturity Model.
https://doi.org/10.3390/su18125920
Sustainability 2026, 18, 5920 17 of 29
X–Y discrepancy (Taipei 101, X = 9 with Y = 7): high technological integration does
not guarantee operational maturity in the absence of physical validation (Y4 = 0). This
is a methodologically significant finding: it highlights the risk of overestimating systems
validated exclusively through simulation.
These cross-case findings also clarify the sustainability relevance of DFS-oriented
systems across the six analysed cases. Their value lies not only in improving evacuation
outcomes during an emergency, but also in strengthening the long-term resilience of
building operation. By allowing fire safety logic to be updated through sensor data, digital
models, scenario-based evaluation, and, in more mature cases, physical actuation, DFS can
reduce the mismatch between design stage assumptions and actual operational conditions.
This supports a more adaptive and lifecycle-oriented understanding of building safety,
which is particularly important in facilities where functions, occupancy patterns, and risk
profiles change over time.
5. Discussion
5.1. Responses to Research Questions
RQ1: The sensor decision chain → is present in all six cases; it is fully operationally
closed without human intervention only in the Beijing case [ 29] and demonstrably in
Zhang et al. [9].
RQ2: The minimum functional DFS pattern requires: Sensing (X1 ≥ 1) + Analysis
(X3 ≥ 1) + Decision (X4 ≥ 1) + Actuation (X5 ≥ 1). BIM is present in all six cases as the
geometric foundation; a living DT (X2 = 2) is present in three.
RQ3: The six cases suggest that the transposition of selected DFS principles into
the Polish regulatory context may be technically plausible for selected complex facili-
ties corresponding typologically to ZL I–III occupancy categories. The barrier is reg-
ulatory: there is no equivalence pathway for DFS in Polish law (Gap P1). The rubric
(Table 4) further demonstrates that none of the six cases achieved Y4 = 2 (complete scale
validation in an operational building), which would represent the minimum threshold
for formal regulatory acceptance. However, this conclusion should be understood as
analytical transferability rather than statistical generalisability. The six cases provide
typological analogues for selected ZL I–III occupancy categories and complex infras-
tructure, but they do not represent the full diversity of all ZL I–III buildings in Poland.
Therefore, the proposed regulatory recommendations should be treated as a cautious
framework for further pilot implementation and validation, rather than as evidence
that DFS solutions are universally applicable across all ZL I–III facilities. The main bar-
rier remains regulatory: there is currently no explicit equivalence pathway for DFS in
Polish law.

5.4. EU and Polish Regulatory Framework
European regulations—EU Directive 2004/54/EC (road tunnels), Construction Prod-
ucts Regulation CPR 2024/3110, and EN 50172—remain predominantly prescriptive. No
formal acceptance pathway exists for DFS components (living Digital Twin, AI-driven
dynamic exit signage, and closed-loop systems) as a method of demonstrating equivalence
with regulatory requirements. This problem is consistent with recent research in Germany,
which shows that prescriptive building regulations require interpretation through safety
objectives and residual-risk reasoning whenever building types or deviations are not fully
covered by detailed code provisions [35]. This regulatory gap is particularly problematic
in complex and continuously operating facilities, where performance-based fire strategies
can offer safer and less disruptive alternatives to strictly prescriptive solutions [17]. This
structural discrepancy—most acute in critical infrastructure, such as road tunnels—means
that technically superior evidence-supported solutions remain outside the mainstream of
design approval practice.
5.5. Human Factors and Research Gaps
Wong et al. [ 31] (data verified by the authors) investigated exclusively technical
reliability (Y4 = 1 in the rubric). Empirical data are not available on occupant behaviour
in response to dynamic exit signage under actual fire conditions. This constitutes Gap
P4 in Table 6 and should be prioritised in future research, particularly in buildings with
mobility-impaired occupants (ZL II occupancy).
