ABSTRACT As digital twins are gaining importance in various industries, research on their impact, use
cases, and implementation in the field of construction is accelerating. In this study, we examine the impact
of digital twins on building safety and security throughout their lifecycle, utilizing two state-of-the-art use
cases for fire and anomaly detection. The solution employs advanced sensors, 3D visualizations for detecting
safety and security threats, and data analysis to predict potential future threats. Using the data that was
collected over a seventeen-month period, we present an implementation architecture for safety and security
digital twins in buildings, explore the benefits and shortcomings of such a system, and discuss the learnings
applicable to different lifecycle stages of a building. This paper contributes to the creation and study of
a digital twin for fire safety, analyzing its performance using two real-world use cases and reporting the
implementation results. Additionally, we extrapolate the learnings from the use phase of a facility to other
lifecycle phases of buildings and facilities.
INDEX TERMS Digital twin, building lifecycle management, fire safety, safety and security in buildings.

II. LITERATURE REVIEW
This research combines four essential research streams within
the context of modern building management: the building
lifecycle, smart buildings, digital twin and its applications in
building, as well as safety and security in buildings context.
By examining these interconnected domains, we provide a

A. THE BUILDING LIFECYCLE
A lifecycle, generally, refers to the series of stages or phases
that an object, system, or process undergoes from its inception
to its conclusion or eventual end. The lifecycle of a building
can be divided into four main stages which are design, con-
struction, exploitation, and maintenance stages [22]. Accord-
ing to CEN/TC 350 which is the European Committee for
Standardization’s Technical Committee 350 for construction
works, a building’s lifecycle is divided into four stages [23]:
1) Product phase which refers to producing and supplying
the raw materials to the site.
2) Construction phase that refers to the on-site operations
and processes such as installing materials on-site.
3) Use phase which means maintenance and repairs, oper-
ational use such as heating, cooling, ventilation, light-
ing as well as water use.
4) End-of-life phase, which is deconstruction, transport,
recycling and reuse as well as discarding
By integrating the two building lifecycle breakdowns [22],
[23] and to offer a more comprehensive analysis in this
research, we propose a building’s lifecycle consisting of pre-
design, design and construction planning, site preparation,
construction, use during the occupancy, and maintenance,
renovation, and demolition phases, as illustrated in Fig 1.

1) SOLUTIONS TO MANAGE LIFECYCLE
Building Lifecycle Management (BLM) brings together mul-
tidisciplinary teams involved throughout the building life-
cycle and integrates the various processes associated with
a building’s lifecycle [24]. This holistic approach ensures
that all stages of a building’s life are taken into account
and managed efficiently. One of the solutions widely utilized
in the construction industry for managing the lifecycle of
facilities is Building Information Modelling (BIM). BIM is
a system for storing the building information in an accu-
rate and interoperable way to improve planning, construction
and maintenance of a building over its lifecycle [25], [26].

Although BIM is not a real-time system, it provides a digital
historical representation of the functional and physical char-
acteristics of a facility, serving as a shared information source
that can aid in decision-making processes [27]. BIM has been
developed to embed building-related data, such as schedule,
cost estimation, maintenance, and other specifications, into
a digital 3D representation of the building model [28], [29].
However, BIM was not designed to be real-time and, in most
cases, needs to be updated manually after major renovations
or changes to the building in order to accurately represent the
current state of the facility [30].
BIM is highly beneficial for safety and security [31],
including construction safety planning, maintenance safety
planning, emergency planning and management, occupa-
tional safety planning, safety analysis, and fire engineer-
ing [32], [33], [34]. In terms of security, BIM can aid in
security design, such as in conduit and conductor routing and
sizing, device placement for intrusion detection and access
control, and CCTV , as well as performing automated fire
safety code reviews [31]. By linking RFID tracking data with
BIM, managers can monitor the flow of people in and out of
different zones, however, the system cannot pinpoint the exact
location of a worker [35]. An automated system has been
proposed that reacts to alarms by retrieving alarms reported
by facility management systems for corrective maintenance,
with the data written into Industry Foundation Classes (IFC)
BIM file when needed [36].
However, despite the advantages and possible use cases for
building operations, BIM is still not able to solve all chal-
lenges faced during a building’s lifecycle. For instance, the
digital design models are often not kept up to date through-
out the building lifecycle [30]. Although a BIM model may
contain all the necessary information to construct and operate
a building, it does not have an explicit connection to the
physical entity [37]. Implementing BIM requires a multi-
faceted approach involving multiple stages and is influenced
by project networks and supply chain interdependencies [38].
Furthermore, over 80% of buildings in Europe do not have
BIM models [25].
B. SAFETY AND SECURITY IN BUILDINGS CONTEXT
Safety and security are essential for all buildings, both during
construction and operation, as they help minimize the risk of
injuries, accidents, and crime. Safety refers to the absence
of unacceptable risk of physical injury or harm to people’s
health [39]. As such, building safety and security aim to
protect the inhabitants and the building itself from dangerous
situations and events that may cause harm or damage.
To safeguard the building and its occupants, it is vital to
equip buildings with appropriate security systems and pro-
cesses. This not only helps to maintain operational capacity
but also minimizes the potential for loss of life and financial
damage. Adherence to modern safety and security standards
ensures the protection of both the building and its inhabitants
while preserving its operational capabilities [40].

a tendency for false alarms, and the detection of fire through
this method may not provide accurate location information.

alarm systems that are not connected to a network are used in
residential buildings. In the context of commercial buildings,
each building has its own control system and is connected to
the fire department. Fire alarm systems are considered com-
plicated, costly, and uncompact [52], and are triggered only
after fire incidents, meaning they cannot monitor temperature
fluctuations in real-time [53].
Another essential system for safety and security in build-
ings is the access control system. This system regulates
entry by utilizing components like card readers, sensors,
a central host server, and a controller [44]. The HV AC sys-
tem, which includes boilers, chillers, and air terminal units,
maintains desired indoor environmental conditions. Lighting
control is achieved using sensors, timers, and control units.
Additionally, video surveillance involves installing cameras
that are remotely controlled by a dedicated security control
center [44].
C. SMART BUILDINGS
Smart buildings utilize technological systems to improve
system functionality, achieve better financial results from
integrated systems, and realize energy savings [54]. Smart
buildings monitor the living environment and sense the
actions of their inhabitants, observing occupancy and behav-
ioral patterns while also attempting to predict future states of
the living space [55]. This allows for autonomous operations
through continuous sensing and responding, enabling the
system to integrate dynamic user information and optimize
the building’s operations [56].
The architecture of a smart building consists of sen-
sors, a centralized control system, network connections,
controllers, and an interface to communicate with the net-
work [57]. The distinction between older buildings and smart
buildings is related to the availability of sensor systems [58]
and analytics measures [59]. Smart environments can create
safer living environments and have the potential to impact
inhabitants’ lives in many ways, such as reducing human
encounters in a building during a pandemic [60]. A smart
system can intelligently respond to future events and monitor
various aspects of a building, including energy usage, water
consumption, safety, and security [61], [62]. Smart building
technologies can integrate with various building manage-
ment systems like Building Management System (BMS),
Fire Alarm System (FAS), Building Automation and Con-
trol Systems (BACS) which make them more efficient [63].
Smart buildings also may incorporate elements of sustainable
design, such as solar panels or green roofs, to reduce their
environmental impact.
An area of smart building research is focused on the
use of data and artificial intelligence (AI) for analytics to
improve the performance of buildings [64], [65]. Smart
building research is becoming increasingly interdisciplinary,
with researchers from various fields such as computer sci-
ence, electrical engineering, architecture, and environmen-
tal science, working together to develop new solutions to
address the complex challenges faced by building owners and
operators.
D. DIGITAL TWIN AND ITS APPLICATIONS IN BUILDING
The concept of digital twin has been widely studied in engi-
neering context, particularly for creating real-time digital
models of physical systems such as jet engines. However,
research on the implementation and applications of digital
twins in the construction industry has only recently gained
momentum [15], [66], [67]. By creating a digital model
of a building that receives continuous real-time data from
various sensor networks installed in the building, multiple
aspects of the building can be improved. These improvements
include enhancing the comfort and well-being of its occu-
pants, improving the energy efficiency of subsystems such as
lighting and HV AC, and increasing the overall sustainability
of the building [15].
The novelty of building digital twins lies in their ability
to provide visibility into the system and offer additional
services, such as predictive maintenance, through real-time
data exchange between physical and digital assets [68]. Pre-
vious research has identified three main components of a
building digital twin: sensor networks, data analytics, and 3D
visualization [15]. Another study emphasizes the importance
of data exchange between the physical and digital worlds,
focusing on semantic and real-time data, as well as data
processing that includes communication protocols allowing
the exchange between the physical and digital realms [69].
The integration of digital twins with Building Information
Modeling (BIM) can lead to improvements in asset mainte-
nance, asset tracking, and equipment failure prediction [70].
Furthermore, digital twins can enhance safety management
methods and modes of construction operations. A digital twin
system that combines IoT, BIM, and security risk analysis

can be applied to prefabricated building hoisting safety man-
agement, thereby improving the data-driven mechanism of
hoisting safety risk management frameworks [71]. However,
by dividing the timeline of an incident into three stages: pre-,
during, and post-incident, we can differentiate between BIM
and digital twin in a building context. Unlike BIM, which
mainly contributes to pre-incident preparation, the real-time
availability and functionality of a digital twin make it valu-
able and crucial also during and after incidents [15].
The concept of a digital twin of a building is related to
the smart building, but it is distinctive. A digital twin of a
building is a digital representation of a building [15], while a
smart building is a physical residential or commercial build-
ing that utilizes technologies to automate and improve its
performance [61]. One of the main differences between smart
building systems and building digital twins is the emphasis on
the use of 3D models in digital twins. Data from many differ-
ent sensors can be integrated into a dimensionally accurate 3D
model of the environment in real-time. The model allows for
real-time integration and visualization of building conditions
while also providing simulation possibilities for training, pre-
dictive maintenance, and other advanced analysis purposes
using multi-model optimization and scenario modelling.
In contrast, smart building systems are primarily focused
on optimizing building performance, energy efficiency, and
occupant comfort through the integration of sensors, IoT
devices, and automated control systems. In summary, digital
twin of a building can provide actionable data for smart
building systems. Moreover, digital twin of a building allows
for simulation, testing and validation of changes in the digi-
tal model of the building before real-world implementation,
which can lead to more efficient and cost-effective building
lifecycle management.
E. GAP IN THE LITERATURE
There is a gap in the literature on how digital twin tech-
nology can improve the safety [20] and security [21] of
buildings, as well as the implementation and performance
of a real-world digital twin for safety and security applica-
tion throughout a building’s entire lifecycle [72]. Previously,
an initial BIM-based digital twin framework has been out-
lined [20]. Camera-based fire detection systems have been
proposed [53]. A digital twin security simulation was made
as a process-based security framework [21]. However, there
has not been any research illustrating how the digital twins
can help improve building safety and security with real-
world applications. We contribute to filling of the literature
gap related to digital twin application areas in the building
lifecycle.
III. METHODS AND MATERIALS
Application of digital twins is a novel approach and the
real-world implementations and use cases for safety and
security in the context of buildings are very scarce. We used
Eisenhardt’s [73] case study recommendations since, case
study method is especially suitable for revelatory cases where
an observer have access to a phenomenon which was pre-
viously inaccessible [74]. This research is based on two
real-world case studies of implementing digital twin for
safety in building environment. A digital twin solution for
detecting the fire and other anomalies causing fires and safety
related incident is developed and installed in two different
indoor spaces to explore the architecture, benefits, capabil-
ities, and shortcomings of a building digital twin for safety
and security. The case studies investigated for this research
are:
1) First case study: Fire monitoring inside an office space,
2) Second case study: Anomaly detection in a grain drying
facility.
The data was collected between August 2021 and Decem-
ber 2022 in Finland. The digital twin solution used in both
case studies consisted of the following components which can
also be seen in Fig 2:
• A customizable sensor package consisting of various
sensors.
• The 3D visualization of the environment to integrate and
present the real-time status of the environment.
• The backbone and data analytics component comprises
an application programming interface (API) that gathers
data from the sensors, enabling sharing and analysis of
the collected information for presentation to the client
via the web.

B. REAL-TIME VISUALIZATION AND SENSOR FUSION
For real-time visualization, we manually created a dimen-
sionally accurate 3D model of the buildings and spaces using
LiDAR scanners and point clouds, or by referencing building
design plans. We then implemented sensor data fusion using
Unity software and WebGL. This approach enabled us to

C. DATA ANAL YTICS
In the first case study, the sensor package monitored changes
in UV emissions in the environment. Since fire flames
emit UV , UV detection was chosen for this purpose. The
system was designed to detect flames, rather than smoke
or heat. If the UV sensor detected a flame, the RGB and
thermal cameras would activate, recording a short video clip
and uploading it to the cloud for the building manager or
system operator to verify the presence of a fire. Consequently,
data collection was triggered by the UV sensor, rather than the
RGB or infrared sensors.
In the second case study, we relied on the thermal cam-
era for anomaly detection. The thermal camera continuously
monitored the space, and the code detected the presence of
high or increasing temperatures beyond a specified threshold.
When this occurred, the RGB camera would record a short
video and take a photograph, uploading both to the cloud
dashboard. Again, the building manager or system operator
would determine the accuracy of the detection. In the second
case study, which focused on a grain drying facility, we used
two sensors, while in the first case study, which took place in
an office space, we used only one sensor.
In both case studies, we extracted and analyzed data from
the database on an incident-by-incident basis during and after
the testing period. In the first case study, this allowed us to
analyze false detections as well as real fire hazards detected
by the sensors, differentiating them from the existing fire
detection solution, which was a smoke detector in the office.
In the second case study, there wasn’t a fire detection solution,
as regulations do not require farms to have a fire detection
system. Consequently, our system served as the primary fire
detection system.
In the first case study, it was possible to compare the two
fire detection systems. The comparison logic was straightfor-
ward; by conducting tests with multiple fire sizes, we could
examine the sensitivity of both the conventional and the new
digital twin-based system. In both case studies, data was col-
lected during the building use phase. However, the learnings
will be discussed in terms of extrapolation throughout the
building’s lifecycle.
IV. FINDINGS
In this section, we present the key findings of the paper to

A. SAFETY DIGITAL TWIN IMPLEMENTATION
ARCHITECTURE FOR BUILDINGS
In this section, we study the implementation of a digital twin
for fire safety in a building during its use phase.
Based on sensor observations (e.g., infrared and visible
light) of the monitored environment or system over a prede-
termined time period, a normal operational signature (NOS)

status is created and saved for the system. The infrared NOS,
while both sensors and the system operate in a stationary
mode, is determined by establishing lower and upper temper-
ature boundaries for each pixel in the infrared sensor image,
as well as the rate of temperature change for each pixel at any
given time.
The NOS is then used to detect anomalies in the machinery
or environment. The analysis system continuously and in
real-time compares the sensor input results with the NOS,
searching for pixels with significant deviations. When the
out-of-NOS situation persists for a predetermined period of
time, secondary notifications and system status updates are
performed on the real-time 3D environmental model. For
instance, an out-of-NOS condition below the established sig-
nature can be interpreted by the system as a machine shut-
down, while an out-of-NOS condition above the established
signature can be interpreted as a machine anomaly caused by
increased friction or other internal combustion issues.
The architecture of the digital twin for predictive anomaly
detection, early fire hazard detection, and fire propaga-
tion simulation is depicted in Fig 4. The data collected
from the sensors is locally analyzed, and the fire alarms
are then uploaded to the backbone to be shared with the
cloud-based analytics dashboard and the 3D model of the
environment.
FIGURE 4. The architecture of the digital twin solution for safety and fire
detection.
A dimensionally accurate 3D model of the environment is
created for data fusion, with material types assigned to objects
to assist with simulating fire propagation. The dimensionally
accurate 3D model allows for the integration of data from
multiple sensors and sources, improving the efficiency of
monitoring. For instance, while the IR sensor and visible
light camera are both direct line-of-sight sensors and cannot
observe behind obstacles and walls, using multiple sensors
and fusing the data in a single 3D visualized model helps
resolve blind spot issues.
The use of a depth map derived from the dimensionally
accurate 3D model of the environment enables the normal-
ization of the IR sensor temperature measurements, thus
alleviating inaccuracies caused by the impact of distance on
the temperature values recorded by an IR sensor. To create
a stereoscopic image of the scene, we can reposition the
camera by an exact distance at the beginning of the sensor
package installation, which allows us to create a depth map
and subsequently assign depth as one of the criteria for each
pixel. Later, when the system is in use, every temperature
measured for a pixel can be normalized. The normaliza-
tion process involves transferring pixels from different dis-
tance planes to a pre-specified plane using predetermined
distance-dependent thermal value multipliers. This allows for
accurate and distance-independent comparison of pixel tem-
peratures, and temperature variation. In other words, while
two objects with the same known temperature at different dis-
tances present different temperatures and temperature vari-
ations in raw IR sensor readings, normalization enables the
temperature and its variation for both objects recorded by
the IR sensor to be comparable. We propose two methods
for depth detection: static and dynamic. The static method
derives the depth map from the dimensionally accurate man-
ually made 3D visualization model or from the stereoscopic
image of the scene created by repositioning of the sensor
package by an exact amount vertically or horizontally during
installation and calibration, and using image analysis to cal-
culate the depth map. Alternatively, a dynamic real-time mea-
surement using LiDAR can be employed for more accurate
normalization, as the movement of objects in the environment
is not considered in a static depth map calculation based on
images or a 3D model, unless the depth map creation process
is repeated frequently.
1) FIRE LOCALIZATION WITHOUT TRIANGULATION
Using a single sensor package consisting of one infrared and
visible light optical sensor, the location of any pixel can be
synchronized, mapped, and displayed in the 3D environment
model. This enables fire localization by accurately overlap-
ping the two images taken from the same scene by the two
aligned IR and visible light cameras and then finding the exact
same pixels in the dimensionally accurate 3D model. In the
first step, the fire detected in the IR image is mapped onto
the visible light image, and the pixels corresponding to the
fire are identified. In the second step, considering the exact
location of the sensor package in the real world, the precise
point of view of the visible light camera is determined in the
dimensionally accurate 3D model. In the third step, the pixels
are mapped and named based on the visible light camera’s
image. In the fourth step, the pixels identified in the first step
are used to place the fire on the 2D image from the camera’s
point of view in the 3D model. Finally, using the assumption
that fire always forms on floors and surfaces and not in mid-
air, lines are drawn from the location of the camera lens’s
center in the 3D model to the direction of the fire pixels in
the mapped image from step four. The points created from
the first spots where the lines strike the 3D model surfaces in
the direction of the fire pixels are marked as the fire location
in the 3D model.

2) FIRE INTENSITY EVALUATION AND VISUALIZATION
By measuring and assigning depth values to the fire pixels,
it is possible to estimate the actual size of the fire. During the
fire localization process, determining the fire boundary pixels
enables fire size estimation in the dimensionally accurate 3D
model. Furthermore, in a detailed 3D model where material
types are assigned to the scene’s components, a real-time
feed of the event in 3D can be provided to the fire brigade.
Based on the size, shape, and location of furniture or equip-
ment and their materials, a simple fire propagation simulation
can be carried out in real-time, assisting the fire brigade in
developing more accurate and effective fire extinguishment
strategies.
3) ANOMALY DETECTION
The use of a digital twin for safety and fire detection also
enables anomaly detection. While creating the 3D model
for the digital twin, a heat signature for the system can be
incorporated into the model, where the upper and lower tem-
perature boundaries of a system can be determined. Different
temperature ranges can even be assigned to various parts of
a single machine when establishing the NOS. During the
environment monitoring by a thermal camera, the system in
real-time compares the current temperature of all system parts
with the NOS from the digital twin. If temperatures outside
of the NOS are detected, the digital twin can estimate the
location of the anomaly in the 3D model, visualize it, and
inform the operator. This can prevent fire events and mitigate
damages that can cause long equipment downtime.

F. ADDITIONAL FINDINGS FROM THE CASE STUDIES
The digital twin systems were consistently tested with fire
events, demonstrating high accuracy and reliability in detect-
ing small flames. Beyond fire detection, the system also
proved effective at identifying other events and anomalies.
In this section, we present examples of the anomaly detection
capabilities of the digital twin system.
It was observed that the digital twin can accurately monitor
the uptime and downtime of the grain drying facility. For
example, the operation of the hot air blower was clearly
visible in the IR spectrum view. In one instance, we were
able to detect the operation time span of the grain drying
facility by monitoring the operational temperature of the hot
air blower using data collected by the digital twin. As a
result, the heat signature serves as a highly reliable indicator
of machine operation, which in turn allows for conducting
indirect facility/machinery utilization analysis.
In an instance demonstrating the sensitivity of the digital
twin, an area of fire hazard created by significant heat from a
leaf blower used to clean the area after the grain drying season
was detected and registered on the cloud-based dashboard
(Fig 12 (a), (b)). Such incidents, in addition to providing an
opportunity to confirm the alertness of the system, can be
used to improve the anomaly detection capabilities of the
digital twin. This can be achieved by marking the event with
computer vision and adding the machinery (in this case, the
leaf blower) to the items with NOS. In the future, the alarm
will only be sent to the owner/operator when the leaf blower
or other machinery is operating outside its NOS temperature
range.
During our second case study, we encountered an inter-
esting event where the Senpack 2 detected the movement of
birds as areas of temperature variations (Fig. 12 (c)). This
is because birds and other mammals present a temperature

One of the challenges faced by every fire detection system
is false positive alarms. Conventional approaches to address
this issue while using a thermal camera include the use of
two dimensional (2D) exclusion zones in the thermal image.
Exclusion zones refer to areas considered as low fire hazard
areas but can still create a significant number of false alarms
due to processes or events involving high-temperature equip-
ment and systems. However, the use of 2D exclusion zones
can create inaccuracies that result in the inclusion of hazard
areas in the fire detection exclusion zones.
A feature of a digital twin-enabled fire detection system is
the possibility of establishing 3D exclusion zones to mitigate
false alarms. Access to pixel distance data allows the system
to evaluate whether the collected data belongs to the 3D fire
detection exclusion zone or not. For example, in Fig 13 from
the first case study, the cube on the table near the laptop
is a 3D exclusion zone assigned to a low-risk area of the
environment. During the monitoring of the environment, the
excluded spaces are exempted from monitoring and therefore
cannot trigger false fire alarms.

While the case studies in this research primarily focused on
the use phase of buildings, the potential implications of digital
twins for safety and security extend across all phases of a

detection, access control, surveillance, starting from the site
preparation phase and continuing until the demolition phase.
