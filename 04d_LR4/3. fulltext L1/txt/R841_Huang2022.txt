ABSTRACT 
Challenges in the extraction and use of earth resources and spaces are encountered given a growing worldwide population, 
rising infrastructures development, and widespread climate change. In Australia, mining and construction are two major 
bases for economic growth while both being traditional hazardous and heavy industries. A nation -wide infrastructure 
upgrade featuring large -scale underground development is underway, the geological uncertainties and localisation 
difficulties of already laid infrastructure are associated with challenges not seen in building construction. A safer and 
competent subterranean transport solution is yet proposed in the context of sustainable developments. In light of this, 
geotechnical analysis as a fundamental subject for develo ping and maintaining safe and sustainable use of underground 
space has huge potential to be undertaken more intuitively considering the advancements in information management and 
visualisation. The PhD work examines the state -of-the-art applications, limitations and future opportunities of Building 
Information Modelling (BIM) and other computational techniques in the digitisation of tunnelling and underground 
construction. The visualisation and interoperability facilitated by data-driven processes are espec ially important to 
underground construction that engages interdisciplinary and multi-environment interaction. 
1 INTRODUCTION 
After decades of development, digital engineering tools, particularly BIM and associated processes and standards have 
been regularly used in the architecture, engineering and building (AEC) industries (DAUB, 2019; Eastman et al., 2011). 
A key enabler of this transition is how information is handled, which includes both geometries and properties. Correct 
use of information and exchange mechanism yields efficiency that eventually converts to smooth project delivery and 
cost savings. In Australia, a nation -wide infrastructure upgrade featuring large -scale underground development is 
underway, transport sector is especially vulnerable to risks due to 25% increase of tunnelling projects in the next few 
years, which leads to critical issues, majorly on tunnelling and ground movement (Infrastructure Australia, 2021). The 
research focuses on improving efficiency and interoperability of cross -disciplinary and cross -phase applications for 
station infrastructure, characterised by wide -span, complex structural form, and settlement -sensitive properties. We 
propose a multi -module system that coordinates geographic information system (GIS), geological interpretation, 
excavation modelling and BIM for a variety of engineering applications in underground infrastructure projects. With the 
use case of geotechnical analysis, the multiple Level-Of-Details (LOD) BIM models feature visual programming-enabled 
object-oriented modelling (ground, excavation, station parts and support systems, e.g. rock bolt, lining, column) that are 
instantiated parametrically, both in geometrical alterations, and semantic properties (e.g. ground properties), according to 
specific st ation designs. An interactive platform that bridges information modelling and numerical modelling via 
automation to reduce manual efforts and cost -bearing errors is developed. The platform prototype is essentially 
underpinned by the digital twin concept that describe the situation where a physical entity is connected with a virtual 
counterpart via data communications. The research is focusing on underground stations as they are important 
infrastructures while possessing geometrical complexity and geotechnical challenges during construction. 
Underground stations take a variety of forms corresponding to the characteristics of different construction methods.

foundation pit and the side wall of the station structure are combined into one structure.

2 METHODOLOGY AND WORKFLOW 
We propose a multi -module system, as shown in Figure 2, that coordinates GIS, geological interpretation, excavation 
modelling and BIM for a variety of engineering applications in underground infrastructure projects. Geological modelling 
and interpretation for numerical analysis of geotechnical works have been a routine practice. While BIM has been 
increasingly employed for project design authoring, construction monitoring and operation management, the exchange 
channel between BIM and numerical modelling has yet been established. Various information sources and associated 
techniques are engaged in underground construction lifecycle. The differences of scale, information category, and 
processing algorithms featured by these systems need to be addressed for better coordination among cross -disciplinary 
applications. Moreover, since there are various ways of establishing interoperability between heterogeneous applications, 
exchange mechanisms should be established in a way that allows for certain level of extensibility and adaptability. The 
methodologies undertaken expects to perform BIM -driven life-cycle analysis, consisting of (1) object -based modelling, 
(2) model-based collaboration, and (3) asset-based decision making.

2.1 GIS AND GEOLOGICAL MODELLING 
Digital transformation in urban underground development is facing unique challenges caused by the intrinsic complexity 
associated with spatial opaqueness, geological uncertainties, high -risk workin g environment and ground -machine-
structure interactions. Excavation carried out without a reasonable understanding of the ground features can be especially 
problematic. A project -scale geological understanding established incorporating the GIS databases, g eological 
observation and geophysical investigation is essential to mitigate the uncertainties inherited from the ground. Therefore, 
modelling with close reference to geographical and geological information is an important strategy for BIM in 
underground construction. The accuracy of the federated BIM model is to be continuously enhanced with domain-specific 
knowledge reconciled with as-built and as-damaged information (Huang et al. 2021). 
A number of online GIS -based application platforms are maintained at different administrative levels. For example, 
AURIN, the Australian Urban Research Infrastructure Network, provides integrated GIS, data release and delivery service 
for researchers, indu stry and all levels of government. While interface such as AURIN provides interactive geospatial 
map of existing open public data, reflecting multi -categorical information (e.g, geology, geophysics, national satellite 
images), it does not encompass all open public data required for modelling such as borehole information, which is 
provided in a different interactive map under G eoVic as shown in Figure 3a . This interactive online facility displays 
location and depth of boreholes, and allows borehole content to be exported in several formats as separate downloads. 
Moreover, the water depth and ground water data of Victoria can be found in another GIS interface Visualising Victoria’s 
Groundwater. Meanwhile, the digital elevation or terrain datasets are facilitated by the Geoscience Australia’s Elevation 
Information System (ELVIS) to help build ground elevation models.

2.2.2 IFC data model extension 
Interoperability can be facilitated by developing standards or by developing bridges between two applications (Wegner, 
1996), with the latter being dominant in the existing researches while the former approach for applications used in 
geotechnical analysis is limited. The standardisation of information model via IFC could be promising as interoperability 
relies on specified data formats (Ide and Pustejovsky, 2010). 
An official IFC data model for underground infrastructure is yet to be developed. To facilitate the representation of the 
exchange requirements (both geometry and semantic requirements), an extension to IFC schema is proposed to assist the 
implementation of MVDs. A taxonomy analysis for underground stations is applied to help scope out the extension, which 
is shown in Table 1. It is worth noting, station is herein classified as a built facility along with other physical infrastructures 
such as bridge, port, rail and tunnel, in accordance with the taxonomy of buildingSMART IFC extensions for 
infrastructure. 
The extension is separate into spatial elements and physical elements. The spatial elements captured the spatial hierarchy 
of a project (Borrmann et al., 2019). Three subclasses of spatial elements are proposed for the station, including IfcStation, 
IfcStationPart, and IfcStationExcavationZone. The first two were inherited from IfcFacility and IfcFacilityPart that were 
added to the released candi date IFC schema to accommodate possible future definitions of built facilities 
(buildingSMART, 2020). The types of station named after construction methods include cut and cover, drill and blast, 
TBM or shield tunnelling, and sequentially excavated. Majority of physical element additions can be defined through the 
existing entities. The physical entities newly defined specifically for the station are IfcLining and IfcForePoling as subtype 
of IfcBuildingElement, and IfcRockBolt as subtype of IfcReinforcingElement. Figure 6 illustrates the physical extensions 
by means of a Unified Markup Language (UML) model with added elements marked in red.

2.3 REVIT GEOLOGICAL MODEL 
An algorithm is developed to automatically retrieve and combine the geological data given in form of the borehole data 
to model and characterise the subsurface conditions. Th e process retrieves borehole data (synthetic) stored in an Excel 
file format and uses topological relations to construct a simple stratified geological model (Figure 7a). Then the model is 
scaled down to the domain of numerical model, by defining a bounding box that creates a section of suitable size. 
The regional model is achieved by creating a cuboid that has its y-direction parallel to the cavern alignment and truncating 
at a length equivalent to the length of the alignment, and then extending bidirectionally in x -direction to a width suitable 
for numerical modelling that ensures no boundary effects. The project-scale geological model is obtained by splitting the 
stratified geological model along the circumferential surface of the cuboid (Figure 7 b). This process preserves the strata 
corresponding properties for later. It is worth mentioning tha t this stratified geological model is created to demonstrate 
the feasibility of the workflow, which focuses on BIM -numerical modelling interoperability, the limitations of this 
modelling approach in regard to treating uncertainties of geomodelling or geological interpretation provided by specific 
discipline modelling formulations are acknowledged. 
Assembly of the information models, i.e. parametric geometries and semantic embedment using computational design 
approach is achieved by three main steps. First, the excavation (represented by the void) is modelled, which involves 
determining whether the modelled geo -bodies have intersected with the TobeExcavated element and splitting the 
intersected geometries along the surfaces of the TobeExcavated element. Second, parametric objects based on extrusion, 
such as outer shell and waterproofing membrane, are instantiated along the alignment. Finally, parametric objects based 
on face division such as columns, bolts and canopy tubes are instantiated on selected faces. A ll the aforementioned 
procedures are automated using dedicated algorithms. Two dedicated algorithms are designed to add shared parameters 
to model components, retrieve material strength data stored in another Excel Spreadsheet and assign the value to 
corresponding parameter category.

3 BIM-DRIVEN DESIGN-TO-DESIGN INTEROPERABILITY 
The interface selected for numerical simulation for geotechnical analysis is FLAC3D, which supports a command-driven 
program execution and allows the use of data files (.dat format) to describe the model. This supposedly outdated program 
design is actually favoured in information exchange scenarios as it makes the complete automation of program execution 
possible. 
An exchange enabler is proposed to facilitate exchange of BIM model and automate the execution of numerical 
simulation. The enabler is realised by a set of Python scripts under a modular structure to allow future extensions and 
convenient modifications. The modules are constructed in accordance with the general solution procedure of the 
numerical modelling program. The framework of the proposed workflow and processes linked by the exchange enabler 
are illustrated in Figure 9. 
There are two approaches for importing the design geometries (when they are composed of polygons forming a closed 
volume) to the numerical modelling environment depending on which format the geometries are saved. The first approach 
imports the geometry in DXF and extrude the geometry before generating the zone from the extruder. The second 
approach imports the TobeExcavated and the ground model of STL files as individual volumes to the interface and 
perform meshing accordingly. Both approaches generate meshed models as shown in Figure 8a. Example uses unstratified 
ground model. 
The initial condition is set in accordance with where excavation is carried out in the real world. The station is relatively 
shallow buried with an overburden of 25m, the vertical in -situ stress is assumed to only come from the overburden; the 
horizontal stresses are calculated based on the in-situ lateral stress coefficient (k 0) in the range of 0.5 -2. The ground is 
mainly consisted of interbedded siltston e and sandstone deposited during the Silurian Period, and termed Melbourne 
Formation (MF) (Neilson, 2018). FLAC3D undertakes a dynamic calculation loop supported by an explicit finite volume

3 BIM-DRIVEN DESIGN-TO-DESIGN INTEROPERABILITY 
The interface selected for numerical simulation for geotechnical analysis is FLAC3D, which supports a command-driven 
program execution and allows the use of data files (.dat format) to describe the model. This supposedly outdated program 
design is actually favoured in information exchange scenarios as it makes the complete automation of program execution 
possible. 
An exchange enabler is proposed to facilitate exchange of BIM model and automate the execution of numerical 
simulation. The enabler is realised by a set of Python scripts under a modular structure to allow future extensions and 
convenient modifications. The modules are constructed in accordance with the general solution procedure of the 
numerical modelling program. The framework of the proposed workflow and processes linked by the exchange enabler 
are illustrated in Figure 9. 
There are two approaches for importing the design geometries (when they are composed of polygons forming a closed 
volume) to the numerical modelling environment depending on which format the geometries are saved. The first approach 
imports the geometry in DXF and extrude the geometry before generating the zone from the extruder. The second 
approach imports the TobeExcavated and the ground model of STL files as individual volumes to the interface and 
perform meshing accordingly. Both approaches generate meshed models as shown in Figure 8a. Example uses unstratified 
ground model. 
The initial condition is set in accordance with where excavation is carried out in the real world. The station is relatively 
shallow buried with an overburden of 25m, the vertical in -situ stress is assumed to only come from the overburden; the 
horizontal stresses are calculated based on the in-situ lateral stress coefficient (k 0) in the range of 0.5 -2. The ground is 
mainly consisted of interbedded siltston e and sandstone deposited during the Silurian Period, and termed Melbourne

A range of numerical investigations and parametric studies can be facilitated by adopting numerical simulation. However, 
the visualisation of the simulation results without a proper context may not be sufficient to assess the potential 
environmental, social and economic impacts of the project. 
A reverse loop, also realised by a dedicated Dynamo algorithm, is therefore designed to help visualise the impacts of 
implementing current design on the surroundings. The intention behind this process is to maximise the benefits of 
geotechnical numerical simulation via effective visualisation. For example, the technical feasibility of underground 
construction is often assessed by taking the potential effects of excavation induced settlement as a criterion. Th erefore, 
an algorithm is developed to reads the simulation results of displacement at nodes on the ground surface and displays 
them in BIM software viewer using colour -filled contour representation, which is mainly achieved via a built -in node 
namely “Face AnalysisDisplay” and augmented surface deformation that is achieved via “Topology.ByCoordinates”. 
Figure 9 shows the effect visualisation using the algorithm based on a parametric study. Three parameters are investigated 
for their impact on ground surface displacements including overburden depth, lining thickness and presence of column. 
In addition, during the construction phase, the same BIM model with the proposed viewer can be used to store and 
visualise monitoring data such as ground settlements. In that case, the proposed visualisation algorithm also allows for 
comparison of predicted settlement against monitored settlement and highlights locations of where the difference exceeds 
acceptable limits that might lead to increased risks during the construction.

Figure 10 illustrates the proposed visualisation algorithm on the design prototype based on the platform cavern of the 
Melbourne Metro State Library Station and the city context of the relevant region. This visualisation node forming part 
of the workflow incorporates both BIM and GIS to demonstrate the benefits of effect quantification and visualisation to 
facilitate informed decision making, this is especially useful when the environment is sensitive to excavation induced 
settlement. For example, settlement accumulation throughout the construction are monitored. Three of the many surface 
monitoring points implemented along the Swanston Street are illustrated in Figure 1 0b. More visualisation features can 
be developed in future versions such as a module that can export analysis results of all components individually, including 
support systems, and visualises the results in a BIM model in an effective and intuitive manner, such as highlighting only 
parts where deformation or strain has exceeded certain acceptable limits.

4 FUTURE PERSPECTIVES 
Sustainable development of underground construction requires adoption of digital engineering and emerging 
technologies. Research interest and engineering practice of modular construction for underground station have increa sed 
over the past few years (Yang and Lin, 2021). The parametric design for prefabricated station was explored, especially 
for project planning and preliminary design stages leveraging the methods developed in this study. Figure 11a 
demonstrates the parameterisation of segmental composition of a prefabricated station taking the Parkville cut-and-cover 
metro station geological and spatial requirements as an example. Figure 11b illustrates the use case of numerical analysis. 
Based on the numerical simulation and in-situ monitoring, design optimization through back-analysis can be carried out 
in BIM environment forming an engineering decision-making loop to further improve efficiency. 
 
Figure 11: (a) Parametric modelling driven generative design for prefabricated metro station ; (b) 3D numerical 
model and simulation result, and digitised in-situ monitoring result 
Further investigations were made to integrate 3D geological modelling and GIS databases with BIM to allow different 
levels of control over design, improve digitisation and specify visualisation requirements of the ground. As shown in 
Figure 12, model of the ground, boreholes and station structure are present in their respective real-world locations, where 
their geographic relationships are built to provide context for further analyses. Geological model with high level of 
confidence help en gineers identify risk zones and aid in decision makings of excavation and support measures; in 
particular, the identification of geological discontinuity near the structure would be imperative to the stability of 
excavation. Figure 12b shows the numerical modelling of two dykes (D1 & D2) one coming near the trinocular arch and 
one intersecting with the arch in order to understand their effects on the construction. Corresponding geological features 
were discovered on site as shown in Figure 12c (Rail Projects Victoria, 2022), suggesting the importance of studies into 
interoperability, data transparency and investigations among the stages of geo-investigation, design and construction.
