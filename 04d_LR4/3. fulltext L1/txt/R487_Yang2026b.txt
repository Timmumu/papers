ABSTRACT
Recent urban practice has highlighted the need for safe and efficient pedestrian movement in 
urban settings. Urban planners and policymakers are tasked with ensuring that individuals can 
navigate cities while avoiding crowding and minimizing safety risks. Among the various 
proposed solutions to achieve these planning objectives, enhancing public space adaptability 
to changing circumstances, such as fluctuations in pedestrian demand and crowding, has 
garnered significant interest. However, constrained municipal budgets limit the feasibility of 
large-scale, capital-intensive upgrades to pedestrian infrastructure. As a result, there is an 
urgent need for a short-term, temporary, and cost-effective strategy to redesign pedestrian 
public space. This paper introduces a novel tactical urban planning approach, combining 
evidence-based urbanism. Using a campus digital twin system as an urban simulation platform, 
this study presents a unique evidence-based planning approach to improve pedestrian spacing 
on sidewalks by dispersing pedestrians without overly interfering with existing infrastructure. 
As interventions within existing pedestrian public spaces, one-way sidewalk systems and 
building entrance control are considered. Through the proposed agent-based simulation of 
the campus digital twin system, the optimization of their application and the demonstration of 
its effectiveness are achieved. The outcome of the simulations verifies that these measures can 
enhance pedestrian spacing by dispersing them without causing substantial adverse effects. 
While these findings stem from experiments conducted on a particular campus, limiting their 
generalizability, they still hold significance as they verify the potential for practical implemen -
tation of tactical urban planning concepts utilizing a digital twin system for urban 
environments.

1. Introduction
Providing citizens with a high-quality walking environ -
ment is essential in contemporary cities (Gehl 2013 ). In 
public spaces that attract large crowds, effective man -
agement of high-density pedestrian flows is critical for 
safety, comfort, and the overall performance of public 
space (Dufour et al. 2025 ; Schneider et al. 2021 ; Zhang 
et al. 2023 ). Although several studies have explored 
different urban planning interventions for crowding 
and pedestrian management, there is a noticeable lack 
of attempts to combine tactical urbanism with evi -
dence-based planning approaches. In this paper, we 
aim to address this gap by proposing a tactical urban 
planning approach to improve pedestrian movement in 
high-density urban settings, utilizing evidence-based 
planning principles and implementing a simulation in 
a digital twin of a university campus as a case study.
Urban planning and design, which involves techni -
cal and political processes aimed at shaping the 
physical layout, land use, and built environment of 
human settlements (Taylor 1998 ), has evolved over 
time, giving rise to various innovative approaches. 
One such trend is tactical urban planning, which is 
an agile, low-cost, and scalable method that facilitates 
rapid implementation and iterative adjustments to 
urban design. This tactical approach addresses the 
limitations and challenges of traditional urban plan -
ning that may be hindered by institutional capacity 
and complex procedures (Alonso et al. 2018 ). 
Evidence-based planning, on the other hand, is an 
essential aspect of urban planning that ensures inter -
ventions are grounded in robust data and analysis for 
evaluating alternative and optimizing planning sce -
narios, forecasting the effects of diverse interventions 
on urban spaces (Alonso et al. 2018 ; Cheshmehzangi 
2016 ; Chung and Choo 2011 ; Davis et al. 2006 ; 
Moonen et al. 2012 ; Mueller, Klein, and Hof 2018 ). 
Recently, digital twin technology, a powerful tool

In urban planning and design, sidewalks are the 
primary infrastructure enabling pedestrian mobility. 
In commercial districts or public parks, walking on 
sidewalks can itself be recreational; however, in most 
settings sidewalks primarily serve a utilitarian mobility 
function (Kang et al. 2017 ; Southworth 2005 ; Yin et al. 
2023 ). From this functional perspective, the effective 
management of high-density pedestrian flows on side -
walks is essential. Such management ensures comfor -
table walking conditions in everyday circumstances 
and can prevent crowd-related safety accidents during 
temporary events (Asadi-Shekari, Moeinaddini, and 
Shah 2014 ; Still et al. 2020 ). Moreover, as widely 
demonstrated during the COVID-19 pandemic, 
when many cities emphasized physical distancing, 
high-density pedestrian management also plays 
a critical role in reducing transmission risk during 
infectious-disease outbreaks (Chu et al. 2020 ; Li, Yin, 
and Chen 2023 ; Psyllidis et al. 2023 ; Xiao et al. 2022 ).
In this study, among various approaches to high- 
density pedestrian management, we focus on pedes -
trian spacing. As seen in critical cases such as the strict 
lockdown policies during the COVID-19 period, redu -
cing the number of pedestrians through demand con -
trol can be the most effective measure in emergency 
situations. However, this is a very exceptional case. In 
this study, we discuss ways to control pedestrian den -
sity while accepting the demand for sidewalk use as it 
is in everyday life. We define this practice as pedes -
trian spacing, which refers to reducing close contact 
among pedestrians to lower the pedestrian density 
experienced by individuals, leading to comfortable 
and safe walking.
Despite numerous studies examining urban plan -
ning interventions for crowding and pedestrian man -
agement, research that combines tactical urbanism 
with evidence-based planning remains limited. This 
paper proposes a tactical urban planning method for 
improving pedestrian spacing by dispersing pedes -
trians without major disruptions to existing infra -
structure. We employ evidence-based planning 
principles and implement a novel simulation at digital 
twin of a university campus as a case study to assess 
the effectiveness of these measures.
Considering this context, our study introduces 
a predictive approach that incorporates evidence- 
based planning and tactical urbanism principles 
using simulations. We demonstrate this method by 
identifying temporary interventions in urban spaces 
to reduce close encounters and alleviate crowding. By 
applying candidate interventions in a virtual urban 
environment, we evaluate their impact through simu -
lation. This enables us to assess the efficacy of these 
interventions in minimizing close encounters among 
pedestrians on sidewalks and underscores the value of 
digital-twin – based simulation for urban planning.
This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 pro -
vides a review of relevant literature, discussing the 
principles of tactical urban planning, evidence-based 
planning, interventions for pedestrian spacing, and 
digital twin technology. Section 3 outlines the metho -
dology employed, detailing the steps taken to develop 
and implement the digital twin creation and pedes -
trian simulation. Section 4 presents experimental 
designs for simulating the intervention of pedestrian 
movements. Section 5 discusses the results and analy -
sis of the simulation, highlighting the advantages and 
limitations of the proposed urban planning approach. 
Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper, summarizing 
the key findings and offering suggestions for future 
research and implementation.
2. Background
2.1. Urban planning and design with tactical 
urbanism for pedestrian management
As many cities transition from car-centric to pedes -
trian-oriented planning, they are working to secure 
more space for walking. A principal approach is to 
enlarge the existing pedestrian realm – widening side -
walks or implementing road diets that reallocate 
motor-vehicle lanes to pedestrian space. While such 
physical retrofits are demonstrably effective at 
improving pedestrian spacing (Asadi-Shekari, 
Moeinaddini, and Shah 2014 ; Noland et al. 2015 ), 
they are constrained by limited right-of-way and sub -
stantial capital costs. Moreover, they are ill-suited to 
rapid, temporary responses for short-duration events 
or future pandemics such as COVID-19. For these 
reasons, alongside long-term upgrades to pedestrian 
infrastructure, cities should also consider operational 
strategies that improve the performance of existing 
facilities – an area in which the principles of tactical 
urbanism can be productively applied.
Tactical urbanism, an urban planning approach 
that prioritizes adaptability amid rapid social changes, 
is another crucial trend. In contrast to traditional 
urban planning that emphasizes long-term changes 
(Law, Azzali, and Conejos 2021 ), tactical urbanism 
focuses on short-term, temporary, small-scale, low- 
budget projects with active community involvement 
(Graziano 2021 ; Pradifta et al. 2021 ). Due to its grass -
roots origins, tactical urbanism has been referred to by 
various names, including guerrilla urbanism, pop-up 
urbanism, Do It Yourself (DIY) urbanism, and tem -
porary urbanism (Andres 2013 ; Ferreri 2015 ; Graziano 
2021 ; Lydon and Garcia 2015 ). It is often used to assess 
the effects of urban space modifications through tem -
porary transformations employing strategic painting, 
paving, and even potted plants. Few studies have

the capacity to adapt to unforeseen circumstances

2.2. Countermeasures for pedestrian spacing in 
high-density pedestrian environment
From this point forward, we discuss the countermea -
sures to enhance pedestrian spacing in high-density 
pedestrian environments without constraining pedes -
trian demand. In doing so, we draw on a range of 
approaches employed during the COVID-19 period. 
At that time, various measures were implemented or 
considered to minimize close contact and promote 
physical distancing. From this broader set, we identify 
those expected to be applicable in high-density pedes -
trian settings with minimal public resistance. This 
subsection will discuss two such strategies: one-way 
sidewalks and building entrance control.
The concept of one-way sidewalks was introduced 
to reduce close encounters between pedestrians on 
narrow pathways, with several cities and large retailers 
either implementing or contemplating this strategy 
(Cardiff News Room 2020 ; City of Westmount 2020 ; 
Oxford City Council 2020 ; Pearson 2020 ; Romero, 
Stone, and Ford 2020 ; Storeys 2020 ). It is important 
to recognize, however, that close contact between 
pedestrians may still transpire even under one-way 
conditions, casting doubt on the measure’s effective -
ness. Nevertheless, if less congested alternative routes 
are accessible near crowded sidewalks, one-way streets 
can facilitate pedestrian dispersion and enhance phy -
sical distancing. This principle has been explored in 
traffic engineering, where one-way traffic systems 
have been shown to alleviate congestion by dispersing 
vehicles (Karimi et al. 2022 ; Zhang et al. 2020 ). We 
propose that this principle could also apply to pedes -
trian traffic.
Amidst the COVID-19 pandemic, many buildings 
have adopted entrance screening to regulate the num -
ber of entrants and measure their body temperatures 
(Ruki and Wulandari 2021 ). In certain instances, only 
a limited number of entrances are operational, while 
the others remain closed to streamline management. 
Although this technique primarily aims to prevent 
disease transmission within buildings, it can also be 
employed to promote physical distancing on side -
walks. By shutting down entrances near busy side -
walks and only opening those adjacent to less 
frequented walkways, this approach can effectively 
distribute pedestrians. In a similar vein, previous 
research has demonstrated that theme parks can miti -
gate visitor congestion by modifying the placement of 
attractions (Ohtani, Sawada, and Nogami 2010 ). We 
suggest that controlling building entrances can simi -
larly disperse pedestrians by adjusting the location of 
available entry points.
These countermeasures, along with others tailored 
to specific contexts, demonstrate the potential for 
innovative strategies to minimize close contact 
between individuals and promote pedestrian spacing 
in high-density pedestrian environments. However, 
the effectiveness of these measures may vary depend -
ing on the specific circumstances, and further research 
is needed to determine the best practices for maintain -
ing comfort and safe environments in various settings. 
In this study, we have employed both one-way side -
walks and building entrance control countermeasures 
to assess their impact on minimizing close contact and 
enhancing pedestrian spacing in urban environments.
2.3. Evidence-based planning, simulation, and 
digital twin
In urban planning, predicting the future is crucial. 
Simulation is a key approach used to forecast future 
scenarios and is essential for evidence-based planning. 
To achieve this, various simulation models or tools 
have been developed for different urban aspects such 
as land use, transportation, demographics, microcli -
mate, disaster, and landscape (Champion 2001 ; Davis 
et al. 2006 ; Mueller, Klein, and Hof 2018 ; Tyrna and 
Hochschild 2010 ; Urech, Mughal, and Bartesaghi-Koc 
2022 ; Walliss 2018 ).
A digital twin is a virtual world that is based on 
a 3D model connected to the real world. It is also 
considered a simulation process because real data is 
applied to the virtual world to provide feedback to the 
real world (Deren, Wenbo, and Zhenfeng 2021 ). The 
urban digital twin, a concept that applies digital twin 
technology to the urban space (Yang and Kim 2021 ), 
can replicate and predict changes in the urban space 
and the behaviors of people in the urban environment. 
The urban digital twin has a differentiation from other 
similar simulation platforms in that it provides simu -
lations using real-time Internet of Things (IoT) sensor 
data, Closed-Circuit Television (CCTV) data, and 
other various big data, and actively utilizes 3D visua -
lization as needed for this purpose (Jeddoub et al. 
2023 ; Lehtola et al. 2022 ; Nochta et al. 2021 ). 
Ultimately, as an urban-related simulation platform,

3.1. Possible interventions for pedestrian spacing 
on sidewalk
NACTO ( 2020 ) introduced a variety of strategies 
aimed at maintaining physical distancing in public 
spaces, such as sidewalks. These strategies differ in 
their underlying principles and intensity levels. 
Essentially, the degree of physical distancing depends 
on the number of people occupying a given space. For 
instance, when pedestrian traffic on sidewalks 
becomes denser, maintaining adequate distance 
between pedestrians becomes increasingly difficult. 
As a result, temporary measures for promoting pedes -
trian spacing generally focus on reducing the density 
of users in a particular space.
In this study, we explored methods to enhance 
pedestrian spacing without physically modifying exist -
ing urban infrastructure or reducing the number of 
people using urban spaces. To decrease localized den -
sity while maintaining the same overall space size and 
user count, it is necessary to distribute pedestrians 
more evenly throughout the area. Upon reviewing 
various interventions, we discovered that specific stra -
tegies, such as one-way sidewalks and building 
entrance controls, can effectively disperse pedestrians 
and improve pedestrian spacing. These interventions 
impose minimal restrictions on pedestrians, requiring 
only slight inconveniences, such as taking a short 
detour. Additionally, they can be implemented tem -
porarily, tactically, and at a low cost without necessi -
tating significant construction or changes to the built 
environment.
First, one-way sidewalks were proposed as a means 
to minimize close encounters between pedestrians on 
narrow walkways, and several cities and large retailers 
have either implemented or considered this approach 
(Cardiff News Room 2020 ; City of Westmount 2020 ;

3.2. Pedestrian spacing simulation using digital 
twin campus
To demonstrate the effects of the interventions 
described earlier and suggest their suitable applica -
tions, this study utilized agent-based modeling. 
Pedestrian simulation was conducted on the digital 
twin of York University Keele Campus, located in 
Toronto, Canada. This campus spans over 450 acres, 
has over 90 buildings, and hosts over 60,000 members, 
including faculty, staff, and students. With two subway 
stations located inside the campus and many bus lines 
passing through it, the campus has excellent public 
transit conditions, leading to high pedestrian activity. 
In this context, we have chosen the York University 
Keele Campus for our study because it allows us to 
easily apply our a priori knowledge of the campus 
layout and pedestrian transportation dynamics. 
Additionally, the selected campus features a large 
and dynamic urban environment, which makes it an 
ideal setting to assess the impact of our pedestrian 
movement intervention model. By studying 
a complex environment like this, we can demonstrate 
the applicability and effectiveness of our approach in 
real-world situations. Thus, York University Keele 
Campus is a suitable place for pedestrian crowd simu -
lation. The pedestrian movements of university mem -
bers were simulated on the 3D campus digital twin 
according to the experimental design.
For the simulations, the study extracted nine main 
buildings located in the center of the campus and the 
sidewalks connected to them, as shown in Figure 2 . 
These include a subway station, a student center, class -
room buildings, and department buildings. The need 
for physical distancing interventions is minimal for 
sidewalks located on the outskirts of the campus 
(outside the focus area of this paper) because of rela -
tively low pedestrian volume. Therefore, the nine 
selected buildings were deemed sufficient to demon -
strate the effect of interventions by simulation.
3.3. Pedestrian simulation
Given the importance of pedestrian spacing in high- 
density pedestrian environment, numerous studies 
have investigated the issue, with most proposing com -
puter vision-based detection technology to monitor 
physical distance among people (Madane and Chitre 
2021 ; Pari, Vasu, and Geetha 2020 ; Shao et al. 2021 ; Su 
et al. 2021 ; Yang et al. 2021 ). These studies used video 
data to detect individuals who did not adhere to the 
ordinary distance standards (e.g. 1 m or 2 m). 
Furthermore, some studies employed agent-based 
models to analyze pedestrian spacing. Harweg, 
Bachmann, and Weichert ( 2021 ) utilized an agent- 
based model to measure close contact exposure time 
between agents and suggest a suitable user density for 
safe pedestrian environment, while Romero, Stone, 
and Ford ( 2020 ) used exposure time to predict the 
efficacy of a one-way sidewalk intervention in an 
indoor space via mathematical modeling. As our 
study also simulated pedestrian movement to demon -
strate the effectiveness of proposed interventions in 
enhancing pedestrian spacing, the duration of close 
contact between agents (within 2 m) was calculated for 
each agent. While 2 m served as the baseline proximity 
threshold, the simulation allows users to specify alter -
native thresholds to reflect different risk levels and 
design standards.

Pedestrian simulations aim to represent human- 
building and human–human interactions in virtual 
environments over time. To achieve this, researchers 
have explored various pedestrian navigation and col -
lision avoidance techniques, each mimicking differ -
ent aspects of human-like movements (Kapadia et al. 
2015 ; Pelechano et al. 2016 ). This study uses the 
Predictive Avoidance Model (PAM) (Karamouzas 
et al. 2009 ), which is a physical force-based approach 
that uses attraction and repulsion forces to model 
individual pedestrian movement and interactions. 
PAM applies forces that push pedestrians toward 
their destination and away from obstacles or other 
pedestrians. It is important to emphasize that PAM 
operates at a microscopic level, meaning that it simu -
lates the behavior of individual pedestrians rather 
than treating the crowd as a homogeneous entity. 
In PAM, each pedestrian autonomously makes deci -
sions based on its immediate surroundings and 
goals. At its core, PAM considers a range of factors 
for each individual pedestrian when determining its 
next position in space. These factors include the 
pedestrian’s current position, the location of its tar -
get goal, and the presence of obstacles or other 
pedestrians in its vicinity. By continuously assessing 
its surroundings, each pedestrian dynamically adjusts 
its movement to navigate toward its goal while 
avoiding collisions with obstacles and other agents.

This microscopic approach allows us to capture the 
complexity of pedestrian behavior in crowded envir -
onments. Rather than relying on predefined rules or 
trajectories for the entire crowd, PAM enables each 
pedestrian to make independent decisions based on 
real-time information, resulting in emergent crowd 
dynamics that closely resemble real-world scenarios. 
By incorporating the microscopic nature of PAM 
into our simulations, we ensure that every individual 
pedestrian behaves autonomously, responding to its 
environment in a realistic manner. In a related 
example, PAM has been utilized in a study that 
simulated potential collisions between pedestrians 
and sidewalk delivery robots during their navigation 
(Hassan et al. 2023 ). Similar force-based techniques 
have been used previously in architectural applica -
tions to evaluate human-building interactions 
(Schaumann et al. 2020 ; Usman et al. 2019a , 2019b , 
2020 , 2021 ).
To introduce unidirectional and bi-directional 
walking in the simulations, constrained Navigation 
Mesh (NavMesh) links are used. NavMesh links create 
navigable connections between two walkway segments 
on a NavMesh and can include a permissible walking 
direction constraint (e.g. Left-to-Right or Right-to- 
Left). By default, the walkway segments are set to bi- 
directional walking. To enable alternate path routing, 
navigation area costs are used to define the relative 
difficulty of walking across a specific walkway seg -
ment. A lower cost indicates a preferred path during 
heuristic pathfinding. Initially, pedestrians are 
assigned the shortest path to their destinations. 
However, as the shortest path becomes more crowded, 
alternate routes become cheaper and thus more 
appealing to pedestrians, even if they are longer than 
the shortest path.
While Unity is primarily known for its capabilities in 
gaming controls, logic, and 3D graphical rendering, it 
offers flexibility through its scripting capabilities. 
Leveraging Unity’s scripting languages (e.g. C#), we 
integrated the PAM simulations seamlessly into the 
Unity environment. This integration enables us to har -
ness the visualization and interaction capabilities of 
Unity while leveraging the realistic pedestrian move -
ments and interactions provided by PAM. To summar -
ize, while Unity serves as the platform for visualization 
and user interaction, the core pedestrian simulation is 
driven by the PAM, with additional functionality facili -
tated through constrained navigation meshes within 
Unity. This approach allows us to create realistic pedes -
trian simulations that consider real-world origin- 
destination and pedestrian flow rates.
We conducted a series of experiments using the 
pedestrian simulation platform discussed earlier to 
explore the effect of interventions on improving 
pedestrian spacing between pedestrians in public 
spaces. A sample snapshot of the pedestrian simula -
tion is depicted in Figure 6 .

The experiment begins by choosing a region of interest 
within a campus setting, with the goal of enhancing 
both the effectiveness and safety of pedestrian move -
ment. This research considers two countermeasure 
design factors: managing building entrances (open or 
closed) and altering the flow of pedestrian movement 
(unidirectional or bi-directional movement). The 
region of interest chosen is segmented into three dis -
tinct sub-regions: an area with one-sided sidewalks, an 
area with controlled building entrances, and the con -
necting regions between the two. These countermea -
sures and spatial design factors serve as mobility 
intervention components in Figure 7 . Additionally, the 
number of pedestrian agents is generated at two levels 
(basic and doubled volume). Considering different 
movement patterns based on the time of day (morning 
and daytime), the simulation can produce multiple 
alternate scenarios for pedestrian movement by com -

5. Experimental results and discussion
5.1. Experimental setting
By default, the simulator establishes whether to imple -
ment interventions, simulation duration, agent 
spawning rate per OD, and agent spawning interval. 
Interventions are applied based on the predefined 
scenarios in the experimental design. All scenarios 
have a simulation time of 15 min. Agents are spawned 
at random intervals according to the specified flow 
rate, with the choice between random and uniform 
intervals; random is more suitable as it reflects real- 
world irregular movement patterns.
Agent spawning flow rates per OD vary depending 
on the scenario. For morning scenarios, agents are 
assumed to move from the subway station to each 
building, forming an OD matrix with 8 ODs. 
Pedestrian volume is based on actual campus pedes -
trian traffic, obtained from CCTV footage at York 
University. By gradually increasing the agent spawn -
ing rate for each simulated OD, we matched the agent 
volume in the digital twin pathways with the actual 
video data, ultimately setting a default flow rate of 30 
agents per 15 min for each of the 8 ODs. For daytime 
scenarios, the flow rate is similarly determined, assum -
ing movement between all buildings and creating an 
OD matrix with 72 ODs. For each of the 72 ODs, the 
basic flow rate is set to spawn 6 agents per 15 min. In 
congested scenarios (5 through 8), the agent spawning 
flow rate is doubled.
We conducted simulations with these settings, run -
ning each of the 8 scenarios three times. Due to the 
simulation’s nature, agents begin spawning immedi -
ately upon starting, resulting in fewer agents in the 
digital twin space initially. After 15 min, the number of 
agents decreases as no new agents’ spawn. To obtain 
accurate results for close contacts between agents, we 
analyzed records between 5 min and 15 min after the 
simulation’s start.
On average, in the morning scenarios S1 and S2, 67 
and 71 agents simultaneously walked through the 
digital twin campus, respectively. In congested scenar -
ios S5 and S6, the average number of agents walking 
simultaneously was 129 and 140, respectively. In day -
time scenarios S3 and S4, the average number of 
simultaneous agents was 65 and 67, respectively. In 
congested scenarios S7 and S8, the average number of 
simultaneous agents was 145 and 144, respectively. We 
used the position data extracted from these simula -
tions to analyze pedestrian spacing between agents.
5.2. Results
In all scenarios, we observed that the intervention led 
to an increase in the agents’ travel distance while 
simultaneously reducing their close contact time with 
other agents. This pattern held true for both morning 
and daytime scenarios. The intervention’s effective -
ness remained consistent even as the increase in 
pedestrian volume inherently led to more close con -
tact time between agents.
Before implementing the intervention in S1, the 
agents had an average travel distance of 406 m used 
46 segments, had a standard deviation of pedestrian 
volume per segment of 74.6, and experienced 74.1 s of 
close contact time per agent. After the intervention 
was introduced, resulting in S2, these metrics shifted 
to 421 m, 62 segments, a 66.3 standard deviation, and 
62.2 s of close contact time per agent. The intervention 
led to a minor increase in travel distance while 
improving the dispersion within the campus area, 
ultimately reducing close contact time.
In S3, prior to the intervention, the agents’ average 
travel distance was 233 m, with 81 segments used, 
a 54.1 standard deviation of pedestrian volume, and 
26.5 s of close contact time per agent. When the 
intervention was applied, creating S4, these values 
changed to 250 m, 80 segments, a 51.4 standard devia -
tion, and 21.3 s of close contact time per agent. Similar 
to the morning scenario, the daytime intervention led 
to a slight increase in travel distance and improved 
dispersion in the campus space, resulting in reduced 
close contact time.
Before the intervention in S5, with twice the basic 
morning pedestrian volume, the agents’ average travel 
distance was 407 m, with 55 segments used, a 146.6 
standard deviation of pedestrian volume per segment, 
and 115.0 s of close contact time per agent. After 
implementing the intervention, forming S6, these 
values shifted to 421 m, 62 segments, a 130.0 standard 
deviation, and 101.1 s of close contact time per agent. 
Once again, the intervention led to a minor increase in 
travel distance and improved dispersion in the campus 
area, resulting in reduced close contact time.
In S7, with double the daytime basic pedestrian 
volume, the agents’ average travel distance was 232 
m, 82 segments were used, the standard deviation of 
pedestrian volume per segment was 114.5, and the 
close contact time per agent was 56.1 s. After applying 
the intervention, creating S8, these metrics changed to 
253 m, 81 segments, a 107.2 standard deviation, and 
48.1 s of close contact time per agent. Similar to the 
other scenarios, even with doubled pedestrian volume 
during daytime, the intervention slightly increased 
agents’ travel distance and improved the dispersion 
within the campus space, leading to a reduction in 
close contact time.

6. Conclusions
In this study, we present a novel simulation in digital 
twin by combining evidence-based planning and tacti -
cal urbanism, with temporary mobility interventions 
proposed as a specific approach to enhancing pedes -
trian spacing in a university campus under high-density 
pedestrian conditions. The effectiveness of these inter -
ventions has been demonstrated through agent-based 
simulations, yielding the following conclusions based 
on the experimental results.
First, this study developed a digital twin system for 
a real university campus space to simulate pedestrian 
movement in urban settings. Rather than relying solely 
on GIS data for 3D modeling, the digital twin concept 
was implemented by incorporating agent-based simu -
lation functionality into the campus model. Although 
real-time pedestrian data was not used in the simula -
tion, the urban digital twin’s utility was demonstrated 
by generating meaningful results through various sce -
nario experiments.
Second, this study proposed building entrance con -
trol and one-way sidewalks as mobility interventions 
for promoting pedestrian spacing on sidewalk. These 
relatively simple, low-cost measures can be tempora -
rily implemented with ease. The simulation results 
indicate that they can effectively improve pedestrian 
spacing by dispersing pedestrians from congested to 
under-utilized sidewalks.
Third, the refinement of these mobility interven -
tions requires simulation feedback. Experiments on 
multiple scenarios confirmed that the impact of such 
interventions can vary depending on factors like

pedestrian flow patterns and volumes. Applying 
a specific intervention without careful consideration 
may produce undesirable side effects. The study found 
that effective interventions differ based on pedestrian 
flow patterns (one-way in the morning, random in the 
daytime), and the spatial pattern revealing their 
impact was more complex than initially assumed. 
The interventions’ effectiveness also depended on 
pedestrian volume, with higher volumes reducing 
their efficacy. In high-traffic areas, more potent alter -
natives such as spatial expansion or demand restric -
tion may be necessary. Consequently, planners must 
thoroughly analyze the pedestrian spacing conditions 
in the target area and respond cautiously. Evidence- 
based, flexible decision-making is required to deter -
mine the applicability, type, method, and intensity of 
interventions. Effective cross-sectoral cooperation 
demands scientific evidence to support decision- 
making. Our developed system can swiftly provide 
such evidence in an interdisciplinary context.
Fourth, while the study’s findings cannot be directly 
generalized to real-world settings due to its limitations, 
they nonetheless hold significance by demonstrating 
potential applicability in analogous contexts. The 
research employed a simulation-based experiment on 
a university campus with relatively straightforward 
pedestrian patterns, whereas actual urban spaces and 
pedestrian movements are more complex. However, 
this study does confirm the potential for proposed 
mobility interventions to be flexibly utilized for improv -
ing pedestrian spacing in various situations. For 
instance, during severe pandemic periods, mobility 
interventions proposed in this study could be applied 
to outdoor public facilities such as parks. Unlike the 
destination-oriented pedestrian flows commonly 
observed in campus environments, park visitors tend 
to move more freely, rendering one-way sidewalk sys -
tems suboptimal. However, by analyzing park entry and 
exit patterns, temporary measures such as establishing 
additional access points could be implemented to 
anticipate and mitigate crowd density. Similarly, in 
high-density residential complexes comprising multiple 
buildings, controlling primary building entrances could 
potentially prevent overconcentration at specific 
locations.
Future research should focus on providing more 
robust scientific evidence to support planners’ deci -
sion-making. In particular, sensitivity analyses should 
examine proximity thresholds other than the 2 m base -
line so that different risk levels and design standards 
can be evaluated. Data-driven urban planning theories, 
such as space syntax (Hillier and Hanson 1989 ), can be 
employed to connect sidewalk network characteristics 
with intervention applications. In terms of improving 
the quality of the simulation, it is expected that the 
realism of pedestrian simulations can be enhanced 
through the analysis of CCTV footage of pedestrian 
behaviors under high-density pedestrian environments 
or via VR co-simulation techniques, and by incorpor -
ating large-scale mobility datasets – such as anon -
ymized mobile-phone location data and Wi-Fi 
/Bluetooth probe data – into the digital-twin setup for 
calibration and validation. Additionally, artificial intel -
ligence technology can be used to derive optimal appli -
cation methods based on specific circumstances.
