ABSTRACT
Emergency evacuation in complex buildings benefits from tight coupling between digital building representa -
tions and real-time operational data. While Building Information Modeling (BIM) provides semantically rich 
indoor descriptions, many evacuation systems still treat sensing technologies and BIM as isolated components, 
resulting in static or weakly adaptive routing behavior. This paper proposes an IFC-based integration framework 
that establishes semantic interoperability between BIM entities and mobile crowd sensing (MCS) data to enable 
real-time, context-aware evacuation routing. IFC models are transformed into a dynamic decision graph in which 
spaces, exits, and sensors are continuously annotated with live crowd-sensed information. Occupant positions are 
estimated using Wi-Fi – based indoor localization supported by a two-stage crowdsensing strategy that accounts 
for both active and silent users. Evacuation routing is formulated as a multi-criteria shortest-path problem using 
an extended Dijkstra algorithm with dynamically updated weights reflecting distance to hazards, travel time, exit 
availability, and a route safety index derived from environmental conditions and crowd density. Weight prior -
itization is formalized using the Analytic Hierarchy Process and examined through sensitivity analysis. The 
proposed framework is implemented and evaluated in a four-story academic building under three emergency 
scenarios. Experimental results demonstrate improved evacuation effectiveness, reflected by higher in-time 
evacuation rates and reduced evacuation times under scenario-specific time constraints. In addition, evacua -
tion times exhibit lower variability under constrained conditions, indicating stable routing behavior when egress 
options are limited. Overall, the findings confirm that semantic coupling of BIM and mobile crowd sensing 
enables digital-twin – like behavior for real-time emergency management and illustrates how engineering infor -
matics can transform static building models into operational decision-support systems.
1. Introduction
Real-time decision-making in safety – critical buildings increasingly 
relies on the integration of semantically rich digital building models 
with volatile, heterogeneous sensing data. From an engineering infor -
matics perspective, a core challenge is achieving semantic interopera -
bility between Building Information Modeling (BIM) representations 
and real-time sensing streams so that computational models and routing 
algorithms can adapt continuously to evolving indoor conditions. 
Emergency evacuation provides a particularly demanding context: route 
feasibility depends jointly on building topology, hazard dynamics, and 
crowd behavior, and must be recomputed under strict temporal 
constraints.
Mobile Crowd Sensing (MCS) has emerged as a scalable paradigm for 
collecting occupant-level information during emergencies. By exploiting 
the sensing and communication capabilities of smartphones and wear -
able devices, MCS enables decentralized acquisition of location and 
contextual data and supports dynamic situational awareness [1 – 4] . 
Existing work has shown that MCS can reveal population flows, identify 
bottlenecks, and improve responsiveness in evacuation scenarios. 
Nevertheless, MCS data streams are heterogeneous, noisy, and largely 
decoupled from the architectural context in which occupants move, 
limiting their utility for enforcing building-aware route feasibility. In 
parallel, BIM has become a dominant paradigm for representing the 
physical and functional characteristics of buildings [5] . It enables 
information-rich modeling of indoor spaces and supports advanced 
analysis for design, construction, and facility operations. In emergency 
contexts, BIM can represent critical components such as rooms,

BIM semantics, providing object-level access to entities such as IfcSpace,

Recent research has addressed aspects of BIM–sensor integration 
from multiple perspectives. Sensor-enabled BIM frameworks using 
CCTV, BLE beacons, and environmental monitoring devices have been 
proposed, but are typically evaluated in controlled or simulation-based

BIM–GIS integration has been explored mainly within facility manage -

ning capabilities [17,18,24] , but most rely on pre-modeled conditions

Overall, the literature reveals three persistent gaps. First, there is a 
semantic gap between IFC-based building representations and hetero -
geneous crowd-sensed data streams. Second, many BIM-based evacua -
tion systems exhibit a temporal gap, remaining static or simulation- 
driven without real-time adaptation. Third, there is an operational 
gap, as empirical validations in functioning buildings remain limited. 
Consequently, there is a need for engineering informatics methodologies 
that explicitly address semantic interoperability, integrate live sensing 
into routing logic, and demonstrate performance in operational 
environments.
This study addresses these gaps by proposing an integrated BIM–MCS 
framework for real-time adaptive evacuation routing. The framework 
resolves interoperability between IFC-based semantics and live 
occupant-level sensing and exploits this integration to support multi- 
criteria, context-aware route computation. Unlike existing BIM-based 
evacuation models that remain largely simulation-driven, and crowd -
sensing systems that operate independently of architectural semantics, 
this study establishes a semantic-level operational integration between 
IFC-based BIM and live mobile crowd sensing (MCS). The proposed 
framework transforms BIM entities into a continuously updated decision 
graph whose geometry, semantics and weights are governed by real- 
time occupant behavior and environmental sensing. This results in a 
digital-twin-like system that performs real-time computational 
reasoning over buildings, rather than static analysis or offline simula -
tion. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a 
field-validated evacuation system in which IFC semantics, mobile 
sensing, and multi-criteria routing are unified within a single opera -
tional framework.
Building on these contributions, this study further advances the field 
of Engineering Informatics by showing how IFC-based BIM models can 
be transformed from static digital representations into operational 
computational systems for real-time decision support. The proposed 
framework converts IFC entities into a dynamic decision graph in which 
building semantics, spatial structure, and functional constraints are 
continuously updated using live mobile crowd sensing data.
Rather than treating BIM and sensing as separate components, this 
work establishes semantic interoperability at the data-model level, 
allowing occupant behavior, hazard indicators, and exit availability to 
directly influence routing logic. This enables real-time, multi-criteria 
computational reasoning within the building model rather than offline 
simulation.
From a digital twin perspective, the framework demonstrates how 
BIM can be operationalized through continuous state synchronization 
between physical spaces and their digital counterparts. This integration 
advances BIM-centered digital twins from passive representations to -
ward active decision-support systems.

2. Background
Emergency evacuation systems are essential for safeguarding human 
life during crises such as fires, structural failures, and natural disasters. 
However, conventional evacuation strategies are predominantly static 
and exhibit limited responsiveness to real-time conditions. They often 
fail to accommodate evolving hazards or human behavior, which can 
result in congestion, confusion, and delayed evacuations [25] . Inade -
quate situational awareness, stemming from insufficient real-time data 
further, hampers the ability of emergency managers to make informed 
decisions [26,27] . Although numerous technological interventions have 
been explored, a significant disconnect remains between research efforts 
and their application in operational building environments [28,29] . This 
section reviews the current body of literature in three main areas: BIM- 
enhanced emergency evacuation systems, mobile crowd sensing and 
crowdsourced systems, and integrated BIM–MCS approaches.
2.1. Bim-enhanced emergency evacuation systems
BIM has emerged as a vital tool in emergency management due to its 
ability to provide semantically rich and geometrically accurate digital 
models of the built environment [30] . Numerous studies have applied 
BIM for evacuation route simulation, victim localization, and perfor -
mance visualization. For instance, Li et al. [31] proposed a BIM-centered 
indoor localization algorithm to support building fire emergency 
response operations, while Yoon et al. [32] developed a victim locali -
zation system to aid first responders. Chen and Chu [33] presented an 
API-based system combining BIM and in-building rescue routing. Simi -
larly, a study on BIM-enabled Facility Management in nursing homes 
highlights how BIM models integrate with digital twins and building 
sensors to support systemized operations and emergency preparedness 
in real-world settings [34] .
Recent work also explores BIM integration with emerging technol -
ogies such as Bluetooth [12] , VR/AR [35–39] , and digital twins [6,7] , 
demonstrating its potential for improving evacuation awareness and 
interactivity. Kanangkaew et al. [40] developed a real-time BIM–AR fire 
evacuation system, while Ahn et al. [38] demonstrated AR navigation 
for indoor emergencies. However, BIM models are often static and lack 
real-time adaptability unless enhanced by external sensing mechanisms. 
Kazado et al. [21] highlighted this limitation and advocated for the 
integration of sensor technologies with BIM to support facility opera -
tions during emergencies.
Research on extending the Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) data 
model has also gained momentum. Studies have focused on extracting 
geospatial and evacuation-related features [41,42] , enabling path 
planning in 3D indoor environments [43,44] , incorporating sensor and 
occupant data [19,22] , and supporting pedestrian simulations [45] . 
Despite these efforts, the integration of BIM with live sensor data re -
mains a technical and practical challenge in real-world emergency 
settings.

2.2. Mobile crowd sensing and crowdsourced systems
Mobile Crowd Sensing (MCS) utilizes the sensing capabilities of 
smartphones and other personal devices to collect real-time data on user 
movement, environment conditions, and disaster events. This approach 
is highly scalable and supports decentralized data collection, enabling 
dynamic and distributed situational awareness during emergencies. 
Tripathi and Singh [46] proposed a heterogeneous data fusion model 
using participatory sensing, while Nguyen et al. [47] designed a mobile 
app for crowdsourced disaster risk data validation. Cicek and Kantarci 
[27] categorized MCS applications into eight functional areas, including 
evacuation mapping, danger detection, and situational awareness. 
Several systems employ location data from smartphones to support post- 
disaster navigation. Bhattacharjee et al. [48] developed a crowdsensed 
digital pedestrian mapping tool, and Fajardo and Oppus [49] proposed a 
mobile logistics app for disaster relief routing. Wan et al. [50] utilized 
crowdsourced Wi-Fi signal data to enable multi-floor indoor positioning, 
while Kitazato et al. [51] estimated pedestrian flow using Bluetooth 
signals. Asiminidis et al. [52] applied BLE sniffing in tunnel evacuations, 
and Pascacio et al. [53] provided a BLE dataset to support indoor range 
estimation. Advanced sensing methods such as gyroscope and camera- 
based input have also been used for 3D mapping. Sadhu et al. [37]
introduced Argus, which uses smartphone sensors to generate a 3D 
reconstruction of disaster zones, and Piscitello et al. [54] presented a 
Danger System integrating microphone and pedometer data to detect 
hazards. Leitch et al. [55] developed a crowd sensing system that sys -
tematically examines multi-sensor fusion strategies using Wi–Fi, Blue -
tooth, ultra-wideband, and inertial measurement units on smartphones. 
Nonetheless, several limitations persist, including short-range sensor 
constraints, dependency on battery life, app pre-installation assump -
tions, and privacy concerns in location tracking [56,57] . While these 
tools offer high responsiveness, they typically lack integration with 
architectural context or spatial intelligence provided by BIM.
2.3. Combined BIM–MCS approaches
Despite the complementary strengths of Building Information 
Modeling (BIM) and Mobile Crowd Sensing (MCS), their full integration 
in evacuation planning systems remains limited. Early efforts explored 
partial coupling between building models and mobile technologies. For 
example, Chen and Chu [33] proposed a BIM–smartphone API frame -
work, while Pishdad-Bozorgi et al. [23] examined GIS–IoT–BIM inte -
gration for facility-level coordination. More recent studies have 
incorporated Bluetooth Low Energy data into BIM-based evacuation 
models, often combined with crowd simulation techniques [12] .
Beyond data-level integration, several ontology- and semantics- 
driven approaches have been proposed to improve interoperability. 
Deng et al. [18] introduced a BIM-integrated ontology for fire evacua -
tion, while Pang et al. [17] developed a semantic IoT–BIM framework 
for dynamic path planning. Related works have also explored BIM–GIS 
and BIM–IoT combinations for hazard-aware evacuation [15,16] . 
However, these approaches are typically evaluated in experimental or 
simulation-based settings and lack validation in operational building 
environments. Similarly, studies incorporating crowd behavior and 
congestion modeling, such as those by Ji et al. [11] and Deng et al. [10] , 
primarily rely on offline or simulated scenarios. Other frameworks 
demonstrate BIM-based automation or agent-based evacuation planning 
[14,58,59] , but do not integrate real-time user-level sensing data. 
Likewise, BIM–IoT solutions focusing on facility management [20] are 
not designed for time-critical evacuation contexts. Recent semantic IoT- 
based routing models [17,18,24,60] offer indoor and underground 
navigation capabilities, yet often depend on pre-modeled conditions and 
lack real-time feedback from occupants. Blockchain-based crowd- 
sensing systems have also been proposed for trust management and post- 
disaster mapping [61] , but remain largely conceptual. Overall, existing 
research tends to fall into two broad categories: BIM-based evacuation 
models that remain largely static or simulation-driven, and MCS-based 
systems that provide responsiveness but lack architectural and seman -
tic contextualization. Full semantic interoperability between IFC-based 
BIM models and live crowd-sensed data streams remains rare, and 
empirical validation in functioning buildings is still limited. These gaps 
highlight the need for integrated BIM–MCS frameworks that support 
real-time, context-aware evacuation routing under operational condi -
tions. In response, this study proposes an IFC-based BIM–MCS frame -
work that tightly couples semantic building representations with live 
occupant sensing to enable adaptive, multi-criteria evacuation routing 
in real-world environments.
3. Methods
This section describes the methodological workflow developed for 
integrating BIM semantics with real-time crowd-sensed information. 
The overall process follows common structures used in BIM–IoT systems 
but extends them to support continuous data fusion and adaptive 
evacuation routing. The method comprises five components: system 
architecture, BIM–IFC processing, mobile crowd sensing, indoor locali -
zation, and multi-weighted route computation.
3.1. System architecture
The system architecture defines how sensing data, building infor -
mation, and routing computations interact within the proposed 
BIM–MCS evacuation framework. It is organized into five functional 
domains that together support real-time data acquisition, semantic 
processing, and adaptive route generation as shown in Fig. 1 . The 
sensing domain collects raw measurements from occupants’ smart -
phones, including Wi-Fi and BLE signal strengths as well as contextual 
indicators such as barometric pressure and ambient light levels. Baro -
metric pressure measurements are incorporated as an auxiliary sensing 
input to support floor-level differentiation and vertical movement 
detection in multi-story buildings, thereby improving the robustness of 
Wi-Fi–based indoor localization during evacuation. These data are 
transmitted to the collection domain through the network domain, 
which ensures secure and continuous communication between mobile 
devices and the server.
Within the collection domain, incoming measurements are stored in 
a unified repository where they are associated with corresponding IFC 
entities extracted from the BIM model. The processing domain acts as 
the core computational layer, performing data filtering, feature extrac -
tion, semantic alignment, and dynamic updating of the evacuation 
graph. Continuous updates from MCS and IFC-linked sensors allow the 
routing engine to adjust edge weights based on changing conditions such 
as congestion, hazard proximity, and exit accessibility. The application 
domain provides the interface through which evacuation instructions 
and updated paths are visualized for occupants or emergency managers.

connecting BIM semantics, mobile sensing, and the routing engine 
within the proposed architecture.
3.2. BIM model
The BIM model provides the spatial and semantic foundation of the 
proposed evacuation framework. To ensure interoperability and 
robustness across heterogeneous modeling practices, the building model 
is exported to the Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) format, which en -
ables standardized access to architectural and spatial entities while 
remaining independent of authoring software.
Relevant IFC entities are extracted to construct a semantically 
enriched evacuation graph. Spatial hierarchy is defined using IfcBuild -
ing, IfcBuildingStorey, and IfcSpace, while circulation-related elements 
such as corridors, staircases, and vertical connections are identified 
through their spatial containment and adjacency relationships. Rather

To address heterogeneity in IFC representations, an explicit 
normalization step is applied during IFC parsing. In IFC-based models, 
passable connections between spaces may be represented either 
explicitly by IfcDoor elements or implicitly by wall openings modeled as 
IfcOpeningElement, depending on authoring conventions and export 
configurations. In the proposed framework, both representations are 
abstracted into a unified Transition entity in the evacuation graph. The 
IFC schema elements used in this semantic extraction process are sum -
marized in Fig. 4 .
Transitions are identified primarily through IFC relational semantics 
rather than object labels. Specifically, IfcRelVoidsElement and IfcRel -
FillsElement relationships are used to associate openings and doors with 
their host elements, while space-boundary and spatial-containment re -
lations are employed to determine adjacency between IfcSpace entities. 
When an IfcDoor object is present, the corresponding transition inherits 
door-specific attributes such as accessibility status. When only an 
opening is available, a passable transition is still created using default 
attributes. This normalization strategy ensures equivalent graph con -
nectivity across different IFC representations and prevents loss of navi -
gable links due to modeling variations.
While IfcSpace entities provide the basis for indoor spatial repre -
sentation, the term “ space ” is used differently in the IFC schema and in 
the proposed graph abstraction. In the proposed abstraction, decision- 
oriented spaces such as rooms and junctions are represented as nodes, 
whereas traversal-oriented spaces, including corridors, stairwells, and 
elevator shafts, are abstracted as transition elements (edges) connecting 
decision nodes. Environmental conditions such as hazards, congestion, 
or accessibility constraints primarily affect movement feasibility and are 
therefore applied to edges rather than nodes.
Based on the extracted and normalized entities, the IFC model is 
transformed into a graph structure in which nodes represent indoor 
spaces and edges represent navigable transitions. The resulting graph 
encodes both geometric connectivity and semantic constraints, 
including vertical circulation, region-specific accessibility, and exit re -
lationships. This semantically grounded graph serves as the basis for 
dynamic evacuation routing and enables subsequent integration of real- 
time crowd-sensed and hazard-related information. Although geometric 
discretization of walkable spaces is avoided, geometric attributes such as 
distance and traversal cost are explicitly computed on the derived graph 
edges (Transitions) after graph abstraction.
3.3. Mobile crowd sensing
MCS provides the dynamic component of the proposed framework by 
supplying real-time information on occupant locations and environ -
mental conditions. Smartphones carried by building occupants contin -
uously collect Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) signal strength 
measurements for indoor localization, together with contextual in -
dicators such as barometric pressure, ambient light levels, and device- 
level status information. These measurements capture both spatial 
movement and environmental variations that are relevant to evacuation 
behavior.

3.3.1. Evacuation Plan
The Evacuation Plan defines a set of high-level decision rules that 
govern how evacuation routing is adapted under different emergency 
conditions. Rather than introducing additional building-specific con -
figurations, the Evacuation Plan operates as a policy layer that translates 
hazard states and contextual constraints into routing decisions within 
the evacuation graph.
The questions specified in the Evacuation Plan are designed to cap -
ture essential operational considerations relevant to emergency evacu -
ation, such as exit availability, route accessibility, and the permissibility 
of specific building components (e.g., elevators or restricted zones) 
under a given hazard scenario. Each question functions as a decision 
trigger that activates or deactivates corresponding routing constraints, 
ensuring that evacuation strategies remain consistent with safety regu -
lations and situational requirements.
Hazard information is not directly extracted from BIM geometry. 
Instead, BIM provides the spatial and semantic context such as space 
topology, connectivity, and the locations of relevant building elements 
within which hazard data are interpreted. Actual hazard states are 
derived from environmental sensors, mobile crowd sensing inputs, and 
system-level observations (e.g., congestion or route obstruction). These 
hazard inputs are then mapped onto the evacuation graph by modifying 
edge weights, accessibility flags, or availability conditions associated 
with affected transitions.
The Evacuation Plan thus serves as an intermediary decision layer 
between sensed hazard conditions and graph-based routing computa -
tion. By separating policy-level decision logic from building geometry 
and sensing mechanisms, the framework maintains generality across 
different building types while enabling scenario-specific evacuation 
behavior. This design allows the same Evacuation Plan structure to be 
reused across buildings, with instantiation determined by available BIM 
semantics and real-time operational data rather than hard-coded 
configurations.

3.4. Evacuation component
The evacuation component constitutes the core analytical unit of the 
proposed framework, integrating semantic building information from 
the BIM model with real-time MCS data to generate adaptive evacuation 
routes. The IFC-derived spatial graph is continuously updated with dy -
namic edge weights that represent changing indoor conditions, 
including congestion levels, hazard proximity, accessibility, and exit 
status. Through these updates, the routing engine maintains an accurate 
and real-time representation of the building environment during 
emergencies.
3.4.1. Positioning
Accurate occupant localization is essential for computing real-time 
evacuation routes. The proposed system employs Wi-Fi – based indoor 
positioning, in which received signal strength indicators (RSSI) are used 
to estimate the distance between a mobile device and multiple access 
points. Equation (1) expresses the standard RSSI-to-distance conversion 
model, where A represents the reference signal strength and n is the 
environment-specific path-loss exponent. These estimated distances 
form the input to trilateration or fingerprinting methods, refined 
through an optimization procedure defined in Equation (2) , which 
minimizes the difference between measured and predicted distances. 
This approach compensates for multipath interference, device vari -
ability, and signal fluctuations common in indoor environments.
A two-stage positioning strategy is used to address cases where some 
users are unable to transmit sensing data. In the first stage, locations of 
active users are estimated directly from RSSI-based trilateration. These 
positions then serve as reference points for the second stage, in which 
locations of silent users are inferred through proximity relationships 
derived from the signals of nearby active users. As illustrated in Fig. 5 , 
this strategy ensures a complete spatial distribution of occupants even 
under inconsistent data availability, enabling the evacuation engine to 
maintain a coherent occupancy map under real-world conditions.

location rather than global graph reconstruction.
3.5. Implementation Setup
The proposed framework was implemented and evaluated in a four- 
story academic building belonging to the Faculty of Surveying and 
Geomatics. The building includes two staircases located on the eastern 
and western sides and an elevator adjacent to the main corridor. A 
detailed BIM model was created in Autodesk Revit and exported to IFC 
to support semantic processing. Fig. 6 shows the reconstructed BIM 
model, floor layouts, and the placement of smoke sensors installed along 
the main corridors of each floor. The sensor network provides real-time 
hazard indicators that are integrated directly into the evacuation graph.
The building ’ s spatial configuration was converted into a graph 
representation ( Fig. 7 ), where indoor spaces and circulation segments 
were transformed into nodes and edges, respectively. Each edge was 
assigned attributes related to distance, capacity, and travel type (e.g., 
corridor, stair, elevator). Hazard-related properties derived from the 
sensor network were also embedded into the graph to allow real-time 
updates of edge weights.
Mobile sensing data were collected using an application developed in 
Android Studio based on the AWARE framework. The interface ( Fig. 8 ) 
enables participants to activate sensing modules and transmit data when 
connectivity is available. Thirteen physical sensors including Wi-Fi, 
Bluetooth, accelerometer, barometer, ambient light, magnetic field, 
and proximity and three virtual sensors (battery, network, and pro -
cessing status) were utilized. Sensed data were stored locally and 
uploaded to the server during connection intervals, where they were 
processed and mapped to the IFC-based spatial graph.
User positions were estimated using Wi-Fi fingerprinting as 
described in Section 3.4 . A two-stage crowdsensing approach was used 
to infer the locations of hidden users who were unable to upload their 
data. Coordinators or informers were selected according to evacuation-

Route performance was assessed using four indicators: average 
positioning error, average egress time, average route length, and the 
proportion of in-time versus out-of-time evacuations. Table 5 summa -

Scenario A achieved an average positioning error of 3.02 m, while

localization complexity due to the building-wide distribution of occu -
pants. Despite this, Scenario C produced the shortest average egress time 
(103 s) and the shortest route length (188 m). These findings indicate 
that the adaptive routing engine effectively redirected occupants around 
blocked exits and prioritized feasible short paths even under restrictive 
conditions. Scenario B yielded the most reliable route completion rates, 
with 48 in-time evacuations and only 2 out-of-time cases, largely due to 
the model ’ s successful avoidance of the structurally compromised west 
exit.
Overall, the results demonstrate that the routing engine adapts 
effectively to spatial and hazard constraints. In scenarios with limited 
egress options (Scenario B) or building-wide dispersion (Scenario C), the

4.2. Usability evaluation
A usability assessment was carried out with all 50 participants to 
evaluate the Android-based evacuation interface. The interface was 
rated on navigation clarity, guidance clarity, and responsiveness using a 
5-point Likert scale. Average scores were 4.6, 4.2, and 4.4, respectively. 
High ratings for navigation and responsiveness indicate that the system 
effectively communicated route updates and that users could follow 
instructions without confusion. Although more formal usability in -
struments (e.g., SUS or UEQ) will be considered in future studies, the 
current results show that the interface provides intuitive and reliable 
support during evacuation tasks.

moves evacuation planning beyond static path computation toward a 
continuously updated computational process that reflects changing 
building conditions and occupant states.

5.1. Semantic coupling of BIM and MCS
A key contribution of this work is the operational alignment between 
IFC-based building semantics and real-time crowd-sensed data. Con -
ventional BIM-based evacuation models typically rely on precomputed 
routes or static hazard assumptions, whereas the proposed framework 
converts the IFC building model into a dynamic graph structure whose 
nodes and edges are continuously updated using live MCS inputs. This 
transformation enables indoor spaces, circulation elements, and exits to 
function as computational entities rather than passive geometric 
representations.
Through semantic mapping, occupant locations and environmental 
conditions are bound directly to IFC entities, allowing spatial reasoning 
and route computation to operate within a building-aware context. This 
mechanism establishes a persistent link between the physical environ -
ment and its digital representation and conforms to the core digital twin 
requirement of state synchronization between physical and virtual 
systems.
5.2. Routing behavior under multi-criteria optimization
The evacuation routing formulation extends conventional shortest- 
path methods by introducing a multi-criteria cost function that ac -
counts for proximity to hazard, travel time, exit availability, and route 
safety. Unlike distance-only routing strategies, this design treats evac -
uation as a dynamic optimization problem governed by both spatial and 
environmental constraints.
The incorporation of a route safety index allows environmental data 
to influence routing decisions directly. Smoke intensity and crowd 
density are not handled as secondary indicators but are embedded as 
control variables within the routing computation. The Analytical Hier -
archy Process (AHP) provides a structured approach to weighting 
parameter selection and reduces subjectivity in cost formulation.
Observed improvements in routing stability indicate that perfor -
mance gains result from structured multi-criterion integration rather 
than from dominance of any single factor. Sensitivity analysis confirms 
that the algorithm adapts to varying hazard profiles without requiring 
manual retuning, which supports its applicability across multiple 
emergency contexts.
5.3. Implications for digital twin systems in emergency management
From a digital twin perspective, the proposed system demonstrates 
how BIM models can evolve from static design artifacts into real-time 
operational platforms. The integration of live occupancy and environ -
mental sensing enables continuous state updating and route re- 
computation, forming a closed-loop system between sensing, 
modeling, and decision execution.
This approach supports the development of smart building systems 
capable of active intervention rather than passive monitoring. The 
framework establishes an architectural pattern for BIM-centered digital 
twins consisting of semantic modeling, sensor integration, dynamic 
computation, and decision output. This pattern is transferable to other 
application domains, including energy control, facility automation, and 
resilience analysis.
For emergency management, the system delivers prescriptive guid -
ance rather than descriptive status updates. Instead of visualizing haz -
ards alone, the system computes and distributes optimized evacuation 
routes in real time, contributing directly to operational decision-making.
5.4. Reliability and system behavior under constraints
Evaluation results show that evacuation times exhibit lower variance 
under restrictive conditions. In scenarios with limited exits, routing 
convergence was more stable due to reduced solution paths. This 
behavior suggests that constraint-limited environments may exhibit 
higher algorithmic determinism, leading to predictable outcomes during 
navigation.
The two-stage crowdsensing localization strategy contributed to 
maintaining situational awareness even when part of the user popula -
tion was unable to transmit data. From a system reliability standpoint, 
this indicates resilience against partial data loss and supports continued 
operation under degraded communication conditions.
While Wi-Fi–based localization accuracy remains bounded by envi -
ronmental factors, the confinement of user positions to IFC-defined 
spaces mitigates uncertainty by constraining feasible locations to 
architecturally valid zones.
5.5. Limitations and future implications
Several limitations warrant attention. The case study involved a 
single mid-sized academic building, and further validation across 
diverse building types is required. Although the experimental validation 
was conducted in a single academic building, the architecture is inde -
pendent of building type and relies exclusively on IFC and standard 
wireless protocols, enabling direct transferability. Wi-Fi localization 
error remains non-negligible and should be addressed through hybrid 
positioning strategies incorporating BLE, inertial sensors, or Ultra- 
Wideband (UWB).
Ethical and privacy challenges associated with mobile crowd sensing 
must also be acknowledged. Real-time occupant monitoring requires 
strict data governance mechanisms including anonymization, encryp -
tion, and transparency of data usage policies. These considerations are 
central to sustainable deployment of digital twin technologies in occu -
pied environments. In buildings with highly articulated or irregular 
circulation geometries, increased spatial complexity primarily results in 
a larger number of spatial partitions and transitions, yielding a denser 
evacuation graph rather than requiring methodological changes.
Future work will focus on extending the framework through pre -
dictive hazard modeling, sensor fusion strategies, and privacy- 
preserving data architectures. These developments are necessary to 
support scalable digital twin deployment in highly occupied and 
infrastructure-intensive facilities.
6. Conclusion
This paper presented an IFC-based integration framework that 
tightly couples Building Information Modeling (BIM) with mobile crowd 
sensing (MCS) to enable dynamic, context-aware evacuation routing in 
complex buildings. By transforming IFC models into a semantically 
enriched decision graph continuously updated with crowd-sensed in -
formation, the proposed approach moves beyond static BIM-based 
evacuation models toward operational, real-time decision support.
The main contributions of this work can be summarized as follows. 
First, a semantic interoperability mechanism was developed to system -
atically link IFC building entities with live sensing data, enabling dy -
namic annotation of spaces, exits, and sensors. Second, a graph-based 
evacuation model was formulated in which routing decisions adapt to 
evolving hazards, crowd conditions, and exit availability using a multi- 
criteria shortest-path strategy. Third, a two-stage crowdsensing 
approach was incorporated to estimate occupant positions by account -
ing for both active and silent users. Finally, the framework was imple -
mented and evaluated in a real multi-story academic building under 
multiple emergency scenarios, demonstrating improved evacuation 
effectiveness under scenario-specific temporal constraints.
Compared to existing BIM-based evacuation approaches, which often 
rely on static assumptions or precomputed routes, the proposed frame -
work emphasizes continuous data integration and adaptive routing 
behavior. This positions the framework closer to digital-twin–like 
operation, where building models are not only descriptive but also 
actively support real-time operational decision-making during 
emergencies.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The 
experimental evaluation was conducted in a single building, and the 
number of emergency scenarios was limited. In addition, while routing 
adaptability was analyzed through time-based outcomes, detailed agent- 
level behavioral modeling and large-scale multi-building validation 
were beyond the scope of this work.
Future research will focus on extending the framework to larger and 
more diverse building typologies, incorporating richer behavioral and 
compliance models for evacuees, and integrating additional real-time 
data sources such as IoT-based environmental sensors. These di -
rections aim to further strengthen the role of BIM-based digital twins as 
practical decision-support systems for emergency management.
