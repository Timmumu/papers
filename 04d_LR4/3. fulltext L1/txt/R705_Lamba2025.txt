Introduction
The seismic vulnerability of the Indian continent is very 
high as the Indian plate is driving into the Asian plate at an 
approximated rate of 47 mm per year Singh and Pandey [1]. 
As per the Geographical statistics, almost 54% of the land 
in India is vulnerable to earthquakes. United Nations report 
and a world bank estimate that by 2050, around 200 million 
city occupants in India will be exposed to earthquakes and 
storms?. Owing to high seismic activities in India, the new 
IS 1893:2022 has revised the micro-zoning and, introduced 
Seismic Zone VI, and eliminated Seismic Zone I.
Henceforth, all the buildings and civil infrastructures

seismic resilience of existing infrastructure must be done 
with the aim of strengthening these infrastructures. Indi -

Abstract
Integrating key factors such as soil amplification, non-linear seismic response, and mass evacuation simulation is an 
effective way to comprehend a more holistic view of earthquake-induced damage at the urban level. Integrated Earth -
quake Simulation (IES) provides hazard and disaster predictions for various earthquake scenarios, using Geographical 
Information System data to generate digital city models. IES performs soil amplification, nonlinear seismic response 
analysis, and agent-based mass evacuation simulations to model earthquake events comprehensively. This study evaluates 
the seismic risk of the BHU Campus and Crossing Republik, Ghaziabad, under three earthquakes: the 1995 Kobe, 2001 
Bhuj, and 2015 Nepal events. Digital twins of these areas were developed using shapefiles, incorporating attributes such 
as building height, construction material, structural system, and occupancy type. In the BHU campus, dominated by low-
rise masonry structures, five-story or taller buildings exhibited inter-story drift ratios of 5 and 3.5% under the Kobe and 
Bhuj earthquakes, respectively, exceeding the allowable limit of 0.01h (h = building height), indicating significant seismic 
vulnerability. In the case of the 2015 Nepal Earthquake, the seismic responses increased by approximately 250% during 
the second wave, followed by a decrease in the third consecutive wave. The seismic response in Crossing Republik, Gha -
ziabad, shows that rooftop displacement exceeds the permissible limit of h/500, where h is the height of the structures), 
indicating a significant seismic risk in these areas.

In view of this, it is essential to perform the seismic resil-
ience assessment of clusters of structures or of entire cities, 
including the amplification of ground motion while travel -
ing through the soil. This study is often known as urban 
simulation or Integrated Earthquake Simulation. Some 
of the significant aspects within the scope of the study of 
Integrated Earthquake Simulation are soil amplification 
analysis, structure response analysis, and mass evacuation 
simulations. These simulations also include the interaction 
between soil and structures. Miyamura et al. [ 2] performed 
a Large-scale seismic response analysis of a super-high-rise 
steel building incorporating Soil-structure Interaction using 
a supercomputer named K-computer. Ichimura et. al. [ 3] 
performed hybrid finite element modeling for seismic struc-
tural response analysis of the reinforced concrete structure 
using a non-linear elastoplastic concrete model. Chen et. al. 
[4] presented how to apply High-Performance Computing 
(HPC) for a Soil Dynamic Analysis (SDA) based liquefac -
tion assessment for urban disaster mitigation and preven -
tion. Vukobratovic et al. [5] studied the amplification effects 
due to the seismic response in alluvial soil. The authors per-
formed equivalent linear one-dimensional ground response 
analysis on the dynamic model of soil in the frequency 
domain to investigate amplification effects at a site in the 
center of Novi Sad.
The above-mentioned literature on structure response 
analysis and soil amplification analysis incorporated high-
fidelity numerical models but did not consider the impact 
of surrounding structures. To address this, Hori et. al. [ 6, 
7]; Wijerathne, Hori, Ichimura, and Tanaka [ 8] developed 
salable parallel computing extensions for seismic response 
analysis and evacuation simulation modules for an Integrated 
Earthquake Simulator (IES) to simulate earthquake disasters 
in large urban areas. To further enhance the IES, Fujita et. 
al. [9] developed a rapid earthquake tragedy estimation sys-
tem with fast urban earthquake simulation and interactive 
visualization. Furthermore, Sahin et. al. [ 10] extended the 
application of IES to study the seismic response of Istanbul 
city and developed a various module of IES on MATLAB. 
Taking a step further, Burton et. al. [ 11] have developed a 
tool integrating Performance-Based Engineering and Urban 
Simulation to model post-earthquake Housing Recovery. 
The efficacy of various intervention measures intended to 
facilitate post-earthquake housing recovery can be evalu -
ated using simulation models to quantify their benefits and 
trade-offs. In the Indian context, seismic risk assessment of 
Indian cities have three levels of procedures, namely (1). 
Rapid Visual Screening (RVS) Jain, Mitra, Kumar, and Shah 
[12]; (US) [13] using visual inspection, (2). Simplified Vul-
nerability Assessment (SV A) using simplified engineering 
analysis based upon information collected through visual 
inspection R. Bhasker [ 14]; Sinha, Goyal, Shinde, Meena, 
and Sapre [ 15] and (3). Detailed Vulnerability Assessment 
(DV A) through detailed numerical analysis incorporating 
complex material behavior Baruah et al. [16]. While DV A is 
recommended for all prominent and lifeline structures, RVS 
and SV A are commonly used procedures. Moreover, seismic 
response analysis of Indian cities using high-fidelity mod -
els is not adopted because of computational cost involved. 
Although developing an integrated earthquake simulation 
tool, combining ground motion simulations and seismic 
response analysis, is definitely challenging but possible with 
the given state-of-the-art computational infrastructure in the 
country. It is, therefore, essential to perform the seismic per-
formance analysis of Indian cities using existing tools and 
upgrade them if necessary.
In this pilot study, authors have adopted IES Hori et 
al. [ 6] for Indian cities and investigated seismic response 
analysis of the BHU campus and Crossing Republik Town-
ship, an integrated city project, for three earthquakes viz 
1995 Kobe Earthquake, 2001 Bhuj Earthquake, and 2015 
Nepal Earthquake. Digital 3-D models of these sites have 
been developed using shape-files and selected attribute data. 
Results show that in a hypothetical situation, had the 1995 
Kobe earthquake occurred in Varanasi or Ghaziabad, both 
cities would have suffered significant damage. Had these 
sites been hit with an earthquake of the magnitude of the 
2015 Nepal earthquake, most of the buildings, which are 
G+4 or higher, would have collapsed partially or totally.
Integrated Earthquake Simulator (IES): An 
Overview
IES stands for Integrated Earthquake Simulation. IES real -
izes the estimation of earthquake hazards and disasters 
by analyzing all the involved processes. Various pairs of 
advanced numerical analysis methods and analysis mod -
els are used in IES. Each pair performs numerical analysis 
simulating a specific earthquake hazard and disaster process 
in a city and an urban area (see Fig. 1a). IES is a simulation 
system for the following three phases of an earthquake: 
1. Generation and propagation of an earthquake wave.
2. Response of structures subjected to strong ground 
motion and damage to them.
3. Human or social actions against earthquake disasters.

Automated Construction of Digital Model
Current version of automated construction of digital model 
utilizes the building outline (edge information) with height 
of each structure, or 3D maps, and data on construction 
material data and structure system (architectural plan). 
There is a challenge in integrating these data because these 
data are sourced from numerous autonomous agencies and 
are stored at different spatial resolution. In nutshell, data 
are scatters and therefore cannot be used in constructing the 
analysis model. Moreover, 3D maps of Indian cities are not 
accurate.
Authors adopted open street maps to generate shape files 
for extracting building outlines. Once the outlines are gen -
erated, attribute table is populated with required addition 
information including age of structure, nature of buildings 
(office, residential or industry), building structure (RCC, 
masonry, steel, etc), building floor (height of building), etc. 
To generate the digital model of selected site, a shapefile has 
been created which contains the outlines of all structures 
(see Fig. 1) and attribute table (see Table 1) using preexist-
ing open street maps on QGIS software.
Results and Discussion
In this study, three catastrophic earthquakes- (a) 1995 Kobe 
Earthquake, (b) 2001 Bhuj Earthquake and (c) 2015 Gorkha 
(Nepal) Earthquake have been considered for risk assess -
ments due to their widespread destruction of buildings and 
lifeline infrastructures.
Seismic Risk of BHU Campus
A seismic response analysis of the BHU digital twin was 
conducted based on the 1995 Kobe earthquake and the 
2001 Bhuj earthquake. In the case of the 1995 Kobe earth -
quake, the faculty building (G+6) on campus exhibited the 
maximum displacement of 0.55 m and a story drift angle 
of 5.5%. The contour plots of story drift (see Fig. 2a) indi-
cate that story drift values are higher for all buildings with 
a height of G+4 or greater. Similarly, during the 2001 Bhuj

sists in the third wave.
In a hypothetical scenario where the BHU campus or 
Crossing Republik township experienced an earthquake 
comparable to the 1995 Kobe earthquake, structures would 
likely have suffered significant damage. Although structures 
on the BHU campus might have demonstrated better resil -
ience during the 2001 Bhuj earthquake, they would still have 
incurred considerable non-structural damages. It’s important 
to consider that the ground motion data from the 2001 Bhuj 
earthquake, used in this study, was recorded 400 km away 
from the epicenter. As a result, the recorded responses may 
be on the lower side. Additionally, had the BHU campus 
or Crossing Republik township faced an earthquake simi -
lar to the magnitude of the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, many 
buildings taller than 15 m would have sustained damage or 
experienced partial collapse.
Summary and Future Work

Earthquake, 2001 Bhuj Earthquake and 2015 Nepal Earth -

ing Open Street Maps and attribute table feature of QGIS, 
which enlist various attributes such as building height, type 
of building, and nature of buildings. preliminary results sug-
gest that story drift of mid-rise structures, height exceeding 
15 m, is beyond limiting safe values of 0.01h; h being build-
