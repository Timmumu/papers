ABSTRACT
Construction projects and their dynamic and yet hazardous work environments face significant challenges. 
Despite advancements, many proposed solutions for information extraction and utilization remain impractical 
due to complexity and lack of interoperability. Information is often siloed in proprietary formats, making it 
difficult to integrate. This issue is evident in the construction safety domain, where advanced risk analysis tools 
provide detailed insights to hazards but can be overwhelming. Similar challenges exist in cost estimation, 
schedule evaluation, progress monitoring, and quality compliance checking. Decision-making in construction 
scheduling struggles to assess how changes impact site safety due to insufficient information and knowledge 
extraction capabilities, especially when it comes to cross-domain knowledge extraction. This study aims to make 
safety information accessible to safety and planning professionals. By leveraging Digital Twins, automated safety 
analysis, and knowledge representation, we enable decision-makers to gain deeper insights into their domain and 
understand the interplay between project planning and safety. We propose a framework for knowledge extrac -
tion, an ontology for capturing knowledge, and query building blocks to transform natural language questions 
into actionable queries. These methods are tested in a case study, revealing valuable insights into the cross- 
domain impact of decisions.
1. Introduction
The construction industry is experiencing thought-provoking hazard 
statistics compared to other industries [ 1 ]. The high number of hazards 
highlighted by these statistics is due to the production environment 
constantly changing, which makes safety planning labor-intensive. 
Automated safety analysis and prevention strategies such as [ 2 – 6 ] can 
identify and plan mitigation to prevent hazards, with greatly increased 
spatial and temporal resolution beyond what is achieved through the 
current manual practices of safety analysis. Despite promising results of 
automated safety analysis being demonstrated in research settings, 
construction practitioners have not yet been convinced to adopt the 
automated approaches in the industry. The reasons for this include the 
lack of integration of automated methods into current workflows, the 
complexity of correctness assessment, and the placement of re -
sponsibility [ 7 , 8 ]. Thus, the exploitation of automatically created safety 
plans must be investigated further to convince the practitioners about 
their relevance, applicability, and additional insight.
4D CAD was introduced more than two decades ago [ 9 ] and has been 
advancing towards 4D BIM (3D BIM and schedules) and later into Digital 
Twins, which are foreseen to capture planned and real information to 
improve construction planning, execution, and maintenance [ 10 ]. 
Automated safety planning, inspection, tracking, and training are also 
part of the Digital Twins for Construction Safety (DTCS) [ 11 ]. However, 
the exploitation of the information and knowledge captured in the 
comprehensive analysis results is not yet clear. When safety analysis is 
performed in these automated approaches, it considers every planned 
change (i.e., part of the schedule) and analyses the impact of these to 
extract potential hazards and their prevention. The increased temporal 
and spatial resolution is necessary for identifying complex hazards but, 
at the same time, results in a large amount of complex information that 
is difficult to digest and assess for humans.
Over the last decade there has been a major and intensive effort in 
the research domain on developing smart and AI-enabled construction 
safety-enhancing solutions [ 5 , 12 – 14 ]. The application of these AI-based 
tools is based on the extraordinary amounts of real-time information 
that are now being extracted from the construction site. However, cur -
rent workflows are not geared towards handling and disseminating

information is exploited. On the contrary, practitioners are over -
whelmed with the vast amount of data and information that is now 
available to them [ 12 , 15 ]. Examples of the information that is becoming 
available are temporary mitigation equipment [ 16 ], personal protection 
equipment recommendations [ 17 ], safety incidents from video and 
location processing [ 18 ], noise level analysis [ 19 ], etc. When all this 
information is shared with the users, it is done through individual ap -
plications and interfaces, which is not viable when additional pieces of 
information start to add up. Therefore, the research presented in this 
paper aims to capture safety insights that are generated from AI solu -
tions and then present this information to stakeholders in a less 
comprehensive way, thereby reducing the cognitive load experienced by 
stakeholders in processing complex information [ 20 ] and elevating the 
captured data and information into knowledge [ 21 ].
Besides the complexity of safety-related information and its inte -
gration with the safety of responsible persons on the construction site, 
this work is motivated by considering safety from a planning point of 
view. Current practices of construction planning do not allow overall 
planning and safety planning to be performed simultaneously [ 22 ], but 
that will have to change in the future DT-enabled construction industry 
[ 10 , 23 ]. Therefore, design, scheduling, and safety information need to 
be compiled into a single, unified knowledge representation to extract 
the combined knowledge from different points of view, and to achieve 
next-level cost, duration, and safety-optimized construction.
In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been employed 
for effective BIM model querying and analysis [ 24 – 26 ] and construction 
site analysis including safety engineering [ 27 – 29 ]. A major advantage of 
LLMs is that they provide a natural query interface for users (i.e. natural 
language) and can exploit data in the form of BIM models, natural lan -
guage documentation (e.g. safety regulations [ 30 ]), and with appro -
priate prompt engineering and user training offer a distinct approach to 
automated safety engineering compared with existing methods. In the 
context of safety analysis, LLMs can be used to identify hazards and 
propose mitigation measures in the form of “ answers ” to a query. A 
primary concern, especially in the context of safety, is establishing the 
veracity of LLM query results: are they sound (i.e. all identified hazards 
and mitigation measures correct) and complete (i.e. no important haz -
ards and mitigation measures are omitted).
The research presented in this paper aims to complement existing 
efforts in the application of LLM by providing a data model (in the form 
of a knowledge graph ) for which mathematical properties can be formally 
proven and precise queries can be specified ( Fig. 1 ). LLM-based in -
terfaces can subsequently used to rapidly translate from natural lan -
guage into the target formal model in which properties can be rigorously 
proven. This approach is adapted from the decades of cyber-physical 
systems research on model-based safety engineering in other sectors 
such as automotive, avionics, manufacturing and others [ 31 – 33 ]. As 
such, an investigation into the efficacy of LLMs in safety engineering is 
not within the scope of the present paper, and rather this paper focused 
on the development of the knowledge graph data representation and 
corresponding query features.
The work investigates how to request such knowledge through 
queries that are sent to a specialized safety analysis reasoning engine 
(through the proposed knowledge base) to improve the exploitation and 
utilization of safety analysis results in the planning efforts and vice 
versa. This research relies on previously studied automated safety 
analysis tools [ 34 , 35 ] and captures their output in the proposed 
knowledge graph that stakeholders can query to extract knowledge 
within and outside the safety domain. The study examines the following 
research questions: 
RQ 1: How regular BIM and safety analysis results be captured to 
facilitate cross-domain/cross-responsibility collaboration and con -
siderations in decision-making?
RQ 2: How can such knowledge representation give safety engineers 
increased insight into site safety level, crew exposures, and required 
personal protective equipment?
RQ 3: How can such knowledge representation be used to reveal the 
impact of delivery delays and crew reassignment on safety resource 
allocation and future tasks?
The investigation of RQ1-RQ3 leads to the following contributions 
C1-C3 in construction research and the current construction industry 
practices. 
C 1: A series of query “ building blocks ” are identified and categorized 
to facilitate generalized natural language question-to-query trans -
formation, utilized in our example (Q1-Q7).
C 2: A set of nodes and relations are identified and described to 
capture safety analysis components ’ and building components ’ 
connections to schedule information such as tasks and crews.
C 3: Showcasing the knowledge representation capabilities to obtain 
novel cross-domain knowledge that facilitates improved insight 
while decisions are made during the planning and execution of a 
construction project.
Many critical factors and aspects of construction sites are required to 
be addressed to develop a complete end-to-end digital pipeline for safety 
analysis. This research focuses on developing and investigating a formal 
data model of construction safety (i.e. ontology, knowledge graphs and 
structured query services). Other fundamental construction site factors 
that are beyond the scope of the present study include: how construction 
site sensor data is collected, transmitted, processed, stored and managed 
(IoT and related aspects); optimizing user interfaces (UI), and refining 
the UI to be fit for on-site application (i.e. as investigated through us -
ability studies); handling uncertain and dynamic aspects of real con -
struction sites, including misapplication of technology on-site; and 
incorporating image and other on-site sensor data e.g. via drones 
equipped with sensors, scanners, cameras. Computer vision-based 
methods and data-driven AI-based safety analysis [ 18 , 36 – 39 ] is com -
plementary to our approach where the output of such AI-based methods 
can be used to populate a safety knowledge graph, and as such are not 
within the scope of the present study.

2. Related work
2.1. Knowledge representation in construction
Different knowledge representations have been proposed with the 
increasing interest and research of Digital Twins for construction. 
Several large research projects in Europe are ongoing (e.g., COGITO 
[ 40 ] and BIM2TWIN [ 41 ]), each developing an ontology to represent 
their captured knowledge and needs. While the different representations 
share the same objective (i.e., to capture the historical, planned, and 
future state of the construction situation to facilitate an improved con -
struction industry), the approaches still vary slightly. Some strategies 
invite unprocessed data, and some prefer information and knowledge (i. 
e., processing data elevates it to information and combinations if in -
formation creates knowledge [ 23 ]). Ontologies capture the relevant 
classes and relations that are specific enough to represent the data, in -
formation, and knowledge but general enough for similar use cases. 
Many ontologies exist for different purposes, and reuse of both ontol -
ogies and schemas should be considered when creating new ones, as is 
the case with the Digital Twin Construction Ontology (DTCO) [ 42 ], 
which relies on, e.g., Building Topology Ontology [ 43 ] and Building 
Element Ontology [ 44 ]. However, DTCO does not represent safety- 
related classes and relationships.
Within the domain of safety in construction, previous work has been 
investigating ontologies to capture and represent safety-related classes 
and their relationships to other classes within and outside of the safety 
domain. Among others, [ 4 ] presented an ontology that connected the 
potential hazards (i.e., the new class of the safety ontology) to building 
elements through job steps, activities, and tasks (i.e., classes of existing 
ontologies). The safety ontology also captured the potential hazards and 
their relation to mitigation and safety regulation. In [ 45 ], the safety 
ontology presented in [ 4 ] was extended with the capabilities to repre -
sent potential hazards to define hazard spaces, represented as spatial 
artifacts. [ 34 ] extended and detailed the spatial artifacts to cover falls- 
from-heights and struck-by-falling objects hazards in 4D.
While ontologies are available for capturing construction project 
information and others for safety information, they must be merged to 
exploit interdisciplinary knowledge. This also means that additional 
classes and relations are needed to connect the domains. Thus, this study 
will reuse and extend the existing ontologies to incorporate these con -
nections and additional safety-related perspectives.
There is a growing body of research that investigates the integration 
of knowledge graphs, ontologies and LLMs [ 46 , 47 ]. Key areas of inte -
gration include using knowledge graphs as a form of structure training 
data [ 48 ], and using LLMs for constructing knowledge graphs [ 49 ]. 
Towards exploiting such an integration in the construction safety 
domain, the research presented in this paper focuses on establishing an 
conceptually-rich ontology and data model of construction safety that 
can complement the growing role of LLMs in construction site analysis.
2.2. Digital Twins and related technologies
Digital twins represent a significant advancement in the construction 
industry, offering real-time monitoring and integration capabilities that 
surpass traditional methods such as 4D BIM and SPARQL-based query 
engines. While 4D BIM integrates time-related information with 3D 
models to enhance project scheduling and visualization, digital twins 
extend this by creating dynamic, real-time representations of physical 
assets. This allows for continuous monitoring and optimization 
throughout the project lifecycle [ 10 ].
4D BIM, which combines 3D models with the time dimension, has 
been instrumental in improving project planning and visualization. 
However, it lacks the real-time data integration and dynamic updating 
capabilities of digital twins [ 9 ]. SPARQL-based query engines are 
effective for querying structured data but do not support the dynamic, 
real-time updates required for construction safety management [ 50 ].
The proposed framework leverages the strengths of digital twins to 
enhance safety planning by integrating real-time data and enabling 
more effective cross-domain knowledge exploitation. This integration 
allows for the continuous updating of safety information, providing 
decision-makers with timely and actionable insights. Recent studies 
have highlighted the transformative potential of digital twins in con -
struction management, emphasizing their ability to enhance real-time 
monitoring, improve stakeholder collaboration, and optimise project 
planning and execution [ 51 , 52 ].
By combining digital twins with knowledge graphs, our approach 
uniquely enables the seamless integration of safety and planning data, 
facilitating more informed decision-making and improving overall 
project safety and efficiency.
2.3. Technologies for improved safety in construction
As it is described in DTCS [ 53 ], safety should be considered con -
cerning four main responsibilities: 1) planning (Prevention through 
Design and Planning), 2)inspection (conformance checking), 3) right- 
time identification and mitigation of arising hazards in the production 
(proactively), and 4) improving work crews’ safety performance 
(training), which all require data and creates information that can 
improve the safety situation in construction.
Different strategies have been proposed in the planning domain to 
automate identifying and mitigating construction hazards in 3D BIM 
models, e.g., [ 2 , 5 , 54 ]. Some studies extend the functionality to consider 
the schedule in combination with the 3D BIM model (i.e., 4D BIM or 
Digital Twins, where geometry and schedule are also represented) 
[ 4 , 34 , 55 ] or semantic analysis, e.g., [ 7 , 56 , 57 ]. Recent research high -
lights the transformative potential of Digital Twins in construction 
management, emphasizing their ability to enhance real-time moni -
toring, improve stakeholder collaboration, and optimize project plan -
ning and execution [ 51 , 52 ]. For instance, Digital Twins have been 
shown to increase the transparency of information and provide real-time 
feedback, which is critical for effective safety management [ 51 ]. How -
ever, the connection between safety, construction elements, and 
schedules still must be captured.
Spreadsheet-based checklists (e.g. via Excel) are an effective tool in 
construction safety engineering owing to their simplicity and familiarity 
by many users [ 58–60 ]. The main advantage of knowledge graphs over 
only generating checklists is enhanced comprehensibility through vi -
sualizations, semantic richness, verifiability and automation: such 
checklists can be automatically generated if one has access to a knowl -
edge graph; 3D visualizations and 2D diagrams can be automatically 
generated from knowledge graph linked to a BIM model; knowledge 
graphs can be formally verified (i.e. assessed in terms of geometry, 
soundness and completeness, and so on).
3D models can be complex and challenging to assess upfront. 
Therefore, manual procedures often only perform short-term look -
aheads, which means information about the total amount of safety 
material and where to apply it is rarely available [ 22 ]. Furthermore, 4D 
models can be represented as a sequence of evolving 3D models, which 
increases the complexity and, therefore, the time consumption for 
manual approaches. The complexity of the problem is also rising for 
automated systems, but the impact of necessary manual labor input and 
costs related to safety planning is constant. However, the complexity of 
interacting with the results of automated safety planning is increasing 
when the planning is performed for longer durations and for additional 
hazard types..
Even though efforts in safety planning are becoming increasingly 
accurate, the reality might deviate from the plan. There are several 
reasons for temporarily removing the mitigation measures to access 
workspaces, and sometimes, these are forgotten to be replaced. Equip -
ment can also be worn out due to the rough environment or even 
incompliant installations from the beginning. The safety-responsible 
person(s) must traverse the job site frequently to ensure continued

compliance, which is labor-intensive, especially when large sites have 
high activity in several locations and issues gradually evolve. Re -
searchers investigated different strategies to support compliance 
checking (a.k.a safety inspection), including computer vision from 
different perspectives [ 3 ] and point clouds in combination with deep 
learning [ 61 ]. However, these methods all produce information about 
compliance but cannot fix the issues themselves. Therefore, humans 
must be brought into the loop, and the identified compliance issues must 
be presented clearly and concisely to the practitioners to avoid over -
whelming them.
While the details of BIM models and schedules are improving, it is 
still impossible to foresee all hazard incidents solely based on these. 
Therefore, identification and mitigation of safety incidents, besides 
compliance issues, during the execution of the construction project are 
necessary. Those cases are referred to as dynamic incidents, e.g., over -
head crane swings or pedestrian workers not using the designated paths, 
machine-to-machine- and pedestrian-to-machine-incidents. Identifying 
these kinds of incidents requires additional sensor techniques to record 
where the entities are and how they interact. [ 62–67 ] are all examples 
where location tracking technologies are used to identify dynamic 
hazards, and some, in combination with the cloud, enable them to 
operate in Digital Twin settings. Similarly, these incidents have to be 
presented to the stakeholders to increase awareness and improve safety 
behavior. However, presenting long lists of dynamic safety incidents to 
the practitioners is overwhelming and does not allow them to act easily 
to improve the situation.
Besides acting directly on the construction site, the safety insights 
about hazards and increased model details facilitate the creation of 
realistic training scenarios for the workers in Virtual Reality (VR). The 
virtual training allows the workers to be trained in environments rep -
resenting the concrete construction site at the concrete state where the 
current progress is considered (i.e., as built) [ 68–70 ]. The training im -
pacts the workers’ behavior and awareness, and the training perfor -
mance can be used in the planning when the crew allocation is decided.
The many technology-driven approaches to improve the construc -
tion safety situation all produce information that needs to be assessed by 
human stakeholders. However, if this information is not provided in 
format that is easy to understand and exploitable then it ends up over -
whelming practitioners, resulting in critical safety information being 
lost. Additionally, there are cases where information produced from 
individual tools can be even more useful if it is combined with other 
pieces of information and delivered to stakeholders as knowledge. As the 
safety planning domain is the most developed and streamlined 
compared to other domains, such as safety inspection and safety 
behavior monitoring, this work focuses on capturing and representing 
that information.
2.4. Construction scheduling
Construction scheduling plays a vital role in successfully executing 
construction projects. The main objective of scheduling is to estimate the 
cost and time of completion and organize the workflow to fulfill de -
pendencies [ 71 ]. Planning a complete construction project is a highly 
time-consuming task, which motivates the efforts of automated con -
struction scheduling and planning. These automated approaches include 
case-based reasoning [ 72 ], knowledge-based [ 73 ], model-based [ 74 ], 
constraint-based [ 75 ], genetic algorithms [ 76 ], expert systems [ 77 ], 
neural networks [ 78 ], and other methods [ 79 ]. The decision-makers 
must select the most desirable one from a selection of automatically 
generated schedules based on some Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), 
such as duration, cost, and planning risk assessment of critical path [ 80 ]. 
However, automated construction scheduling that considers Lean prin -
ciples and incorporates automated safety planning is still unavailable for 
decision-makers when selecting the desired construction schedule. 
Therefore, it has yet to be possible to identify the impact that a changed 
construction task sequence will have on the safety situation on site. 
While the construction planning domain has been investigated to assist 
practitioners in planning procedures, there is still not a lot of focus on 
integrating safety planning into the broader construction planning 
domain. One reason is the lack of maturity of automated safety analysis 
and the lack of support for stakeholders in their interaction with safety 
analysis results. The lack of maturity is evident as the tools are not yet 
widely implemented in current practices due to a lack of trust, lack of fit 
to current workflows[ 81 ], lack of support by regulation, and lack of 
organizational commitment [ 82 ]. Additionally, there is a lack of stan -
dardization in the automated approaches, which also covers the stan -
dardization of how to capture the safety analysis results, meaning that it 
is hard for the construction planning community to incorporate the 
safety information in their approaches [ 81 ]. This work also attempts to 
define the integration of safety analysis results in construction plans.
3. Methodology
While the domains of construction safety and construction planning 
are pushed forward individually in academia, they remain disconnected, 
as also pointed out in the previous section. This is also the case for the 
solutions that are already adopted and applied in the industry as pointed 
out in the introduction. Therefore, this work investigates how the 
knowledge within an automatically created safety plan can benefit other 
domains of construction management, such as construction planning. 
Fig. 2 illustrates the proposed framework, capturing the steps that will 
be performed and their outcome. The remainder of this document will 
follow the structure presented in this figure.
The first step is to identify domain-relevant questions to ensure that 
the solution is relevant to the industry and the practitioners. The ques -
tions have been identified through interactions with practitioners on 
construction sites in Germany, Spain, and Finland, which ensures a wide 
perspective within Europe. The practitioners are domain experts in 
construction management with years of experience working on different 
aspects of construction management. Subsequently, these questions are 
sorted based on query category and the current availability of infor -
mation in the safety plan (i.e., the information that is extractable with 
the safety analysis tool used). The query categories (i.e., Entity, Exis -
tential, Relationship, Exploratory, Attribute, Comparative, List, Count 
search (further explained in Table 3 )) are used to determine the nature 
of the query, which can identify the expected result type.
Two things must be in place before the query engine can be used to 
answer the questions. First, the natural-language-formulated questions 
need to be transformed into queries that a query engine can interpret 
using the query building blocks ( Table 3 ). The query building blocks can 
ease the later transformation in this study, but also in the longer term, 
where others have to extend the solution. The other is to define an 
ontology to capture the BIM, schedule, and safety analysis result infor -
mation in a knowledge graph that can be queried for extraction of 
domain knowledge. The two tasks are interdependent as the queries 
must be formed such that they are using the proposed ontology. It has 
been chosen to capture the knowledge graph in the ontology that was 
described [ 42 ], which captures the construction elements, processes, 
and tasks. However, the previous ontology does not describe the safety- 
related components which have been added on top using the safety 
ontology proposed previously by the authors [ 34 ]. This is further 
described in the following section 4.2.
After defining the ontology and queries, a knowledge graph needs to 
be populated for validation. For this study, it has been decided to cap -
ture the graph in a local database of Prolog facts, consisting of mainly 
two types of predicates, called node and edge. The primary reason for 
selecting Prolog [ 83 , 84 ] is that, owing to its foundations in mathemat -
ical logical, it is tailored for domain-specialised knowledge representa -
tion and reasoning, which aligns well with our aim to capture 
construction safety knowledge, and to make safety analysis more readily 
accessible to stakeholders, which we accomplish via Prolog’s native 
querying engine. Moreover, Prolog is a suitably powerful programming

language (i.e. Turing complete) and has been proven to be stable and 
reliable in many application domains. This is also why it is among the 
most popular declarative languages in research and commercial prod -
ucts. Its query engine allows for fast interaction with a domain captured 
as facts, which allows for fast lookups and recursions that allow tracing 
through (logical) relations. Other options for representing knowledge 
graphs exist (e.g., Neo4J [ 85 ], GraphDB [ 86 ], or other declarative op -
tions [ 87 – 89 ]).
Neo4j offers robust performance for large-scale graph data but may 
require more complex query syntax for certain logical operations. 
SPARQL, while powerful for querying RDF data, lacks the native support 
for recursive queries and logical reasoning provided by Prolog. Our 
performance tests indicate that Prolog executes queries in less than one 
second for the case study graph, consisting of approximately 3,800 
nodes and 20,000 edges. This efficiency makes Prolog a suitable choice 
for our framework, balancing performance and ease of use. Therefore, as 
this study does not aim to identify the better options to represent the 
graph, it has been decided to move forward with the Prolog approach, 
which has low overhead, a large and active development community, 
and a knowledge base that is easy to share.
To address concerns about query efficiency and scalability, we con -
ducted additional tests to evaluate the framework ’ s performance with 
larger datasets. Our findings indicate that while the current imple -
mentation handles the case study graph efficiently, performance bot -
tlenecks may arise with significantly larger graphs. To mitigate this, 
several approaches can be employed: 
• Limiting the Query Space: By filtering queries based on relevance, 
such as introducing spatial details like quadrant and level, the query 
space can be significantly reduced, improving efficiency.
• Distributing Workload: Implementing distributed computing solu -
tions can help manage larger datasets by spreading the workload 
across multiple processors, thereby increasing computational power.
• Optimizing the Graph: Further optimization of the graph using 
various algorithms can enhance performance. This approach be -
comes particularly relevant as the graph size increases.
Translating questions into queries might still be too technical and 
challenging for the average construction manager. However, researchers 
have been investigating the topic to facilitate and ease translation (e.g., 
[ 90 – 92 ]). Moreover, the drastic recent improvement of large language 
model capabilities (e.g., [ 93 ]) makes it reasonable to exclude generic 
query translation, besides the examples that are brought forward, from 
the scope of this work. Additionally, some LLMs aim to transform nat -
ural language-formulated questions into SPARQL queries in a given 
context, i.e., the ontology [ 94 ].
4. Safety knowledge exploitation
This section will present a set of domain questions, and their domain 
relevance will be reasoned. Subsequently, the ontology will be formed 
and described, and a knowledge graph will be populated for validation. 
In the end, the section will briefly describe the translation of the natural- 
language question into formal queries that the query engine and the 
query engine can answer using the knowledge graph.
4.1. Identification of domain questions
As mentioned in the research questions, this study investigates both 
questions that relate to the individual domains (i.e., RQ 2) and cross- 
domain (i.e., RQ 3). The first part ensures better exploitation within 
the safety domain, and the second part ensures that the solution can also 
move the boundaries in the more advanced cross-domain impact esti -
mation and optimization situation. Even though the questions in the 
safety domain consider the effect of the schedule (safety analysis has 
been based on 4D BIM), the answers do not consider changes and, thus, 
become specifically safety-related. On the other hand, the cross-domain 
questions consider the impact of safety changes on the construction plan 
and, vice versa, the impact of the construction plan changes on the safety 
situation.
The following questions (Q1 to Q7 in italics ) have been identified in 
situations where the practitioners have been presented with safety 
planning of a scheduled construction situation, captured in 4D BIM. 
Thus, the safety analysis is performed automatically each time the 
construction site was planned to change (i.e., completion of construction 
tasks). Additionally, the questions have been inspired by the reasons that 
have been identified to be the source of real incidents, such as the ac -
cident in Ludwigshafen, Germany [ 95 ], and in Hamburg, Germany [ 96 ], 
which in both cases involved poor planning of hazard mitigation and 
synchronization between stakeholders. The questions will represent 
domain-specific variables with “ # ” followed by a letter (e.g., #X or #Y ), 
and their rationale is presented in the following:
Q1: “ Which hazards are work crew #X exposed to while they complete 
task #Y? ” .
Knowing which kinds of hazards have been identified to expose a 
crew during the task makes them more aware of what to pay extra 
attention to, and in some cases, it is even worthwhile for them to learn or 
refresh their mind on how to act in these situations. Eventually, this 
learning can be performed in a VR environment as proposed in [ 68 – 70 ], 
where the selected training scenarios are based on the specific hazards 
exposing the crew during their task(s).
Q2: “ Are hard hats required while performing task #X? ” .
Similarly, the stakeholders are interested in particular hazard types, 
e.g., if the crew is exposed to struck-by falling object hazards and must 
wear hard hats while performing a certain task. Current practice on the 
construction site is to make a site-level requirement of wearing the hard 
hat, which in some cases is not reasonable. Still, the only option for 
safety planning with the manual approaches does not practically allow 
safety planning in high enough resolution to separate specific areas. This 
leads to safety fatigue, and the workers think there is no reason to 
comply with the requirements, even though the dangers are luring 
around the site. This situation can be avoided if the workers were given a 
concrete reason to wear hard hats, and the requirement can be lifted 
when there was no reason to uphold it. However, it should be mentioned 
that automated safety tools at this stage still need further testing and 
improvements of scheduled activities (i.e., only planned tasks can be 
automatically safely assessed). For this reason, the tools should only be 
considered as assisting measures.

Q3: “ Are any guard rails installed for shorter than #X number of days? ” .
There are situations where the falls-from-height hazards appear for 
only a short duration of time, e.g., when a slab element has been placed 
and the wall elements are placed immediately after. For these cases, it 
does not make sense to install guardrails, especially if there are no other 
tasks planned in the area between the slab installation and wall instal -
lation, i.e., the only crew that would be presently exposed to the fall 
hazards would be the crew removing the guardrails to install the wall. In 
this case, it is beneficial to identify if the fall hazard remains longer than 
a practical duration and if any other crews are planned in that area in the 
meantime. If that is the case, it is more profitable and safe to fence off 
access to the slab and instruct the wall installation crew to use harnesses 
instead of installing and removing guardrails. This is also part of com -
mon practices nowadays, but this work enables the construction man -
ager to list all the situations and retrieve formal arguments for the 
verdict. Additionally, it allows the construction manager to gain further 
insight into the impact of different durations.
Q4: “ How is the overall safety situation be affected if crew #X is swapped 
for crew #Y? ” .
If historical information about the construction crews ’ safety per -
formance is available, it can be used to improve the crew assignment and 
obtain improved safety. The information can be collected based on a 
combination of safety training performance and records of loss of con -
trol events [ 55 ]. With this information available, it is possible to identify 
cases where swapping two crews could lead to a lower overall hazard 
exposure for the construction workers. Additionally, it will have a more 
significant impact if the scheduled tasks can be swapped, but that would 
require capturing scheduling dependencies in the knowledge graph to 
know the available options and that the planning is performed itera -
tively between general planning and safety analysis. This way, sched -
uling constraints can remain fulfilled while the wiggle room is exploited 
to improve the safety of the construction site.
Q5: “ How much guardrail must be installed and removed tomorrow? ” .
Foreseeing how much hazard mitigation equipment to install and 
remove the following day, in three days, or during the next week enables 
the safety management to plan the workforce needed to install it. 
Additionally, this knowledge can be used by the planning team to 
consider the local transportation of the equipment. Moving the equip -
ment closer to the application point must be planned in the crane 
schedule to ensure optimal utilization and avoid disturbance of the other 
planned crane activities. Therefore, it is useful from a site logistic 
perspective and safety perspective to ensure that the required amount of 
guardrailing is available on the site and in the right area of the con -
struction site or building story in the building when needed.
Q6: “ How much guardrail is needed to keep the site safe? ” .
In the initial planning stage, the safety management must ensure that 
the necessary mitigation equipment is available to keep a site compliant 
with the regulation. Extracting the amount directly from the safety plan 
eliminates the need to estimate and guess the amount, affecting the site ’ s 
storage and continued availability. Capturing the number is, however, 
more complex as there might be varying reuse policies based on the 
equipment location and temporal overlap (i.e., different combinations of 
the installed and removed amount).
Q7: “ How is the project be affected if delivery truck #X is postponed by 
one day? ” .
In a situation where the construction management is informed about 
a delivery truck being delayed for an unforeseen reason and needs to 
quickly know how this affects the construction project regarding plan -
ned safety. It depends on the truckload, but based on a list of delayed 
building elements, it can be identified if and which hazard spaces and 
mitigation equipment the elements would replace and the crews to 
inform about the delay. The crews must be informed that those pieces of 
safety mitigation equipment are to be kept even though they may appear 
in their plan to be removed. Consequently, it improves the ability to plan 
day-to-day activities more agile and adapt to unforeseeable situations 
that are part of the nature of the construction industry.

To initiate the translation efforts, a set of query “ building blocks ” has

more sophisticated queries, as shown in the following examples. Table 3

Q1: “ What hazards are work crew #X exposed to while they complete 
task #Y? ” .
Fig. 5 illustrates how to retrieve the exposure of a crew while they 
perform a task. Firstly, we must decide if we want to identify a crew 
automatically, i.e., allowing the engine to replace the crew variable. 
Alternatively, we can bind the crew variable, forcing the query engine to 
search for solutions involving that crew. This is general for the 
remaining nodes in this and other queries, but this example is generally 
about Crews, Tasks, BIM objects, etc Fig. 5 .
The crew is identified with an entity search, which is only limited to 
the type of crew. Having a crew assignment allows the engine to identify 
one of the tasks they are performing (#1), and having the task allows the 
engine, through a relation search, to find the state where the task is 
performed (#2). As Q1 is generally talking about hazards, we are 
interested in struck-by hazards OR fall hazards, and from here, we follow 
two different routes to identify if the hazards are exposing the crew. The 
two routes arise from the different nature of the hazards and their re -
lations to the remaining nodes. If the hazard node is a struck-by hazard 
(i.e., left path #3), then we follow the exposed relation (#3.1) and find 
the BIM object that is worked on (#6). If the BIM object worked on is 
also built by the task (#7) identified in the first step (#1), then the query 
has identified an exposure of a struck-by hazard to the crew.
Similarly, if the entity search identifies a fall hazard, we perform a 
relationship search to find the exposed movement space (#4.1). Based 
on the movement space ’ s relations, we need to find the existing BIM 
object (#4.2) from which the work is performed (#4.3). As the routes 
merge from step #4.3 and on the queries, they use the same technique to 
identify the exposure of a crew. Having the exposures of a crew 
enumerated allows one to do an entity search to find out whether hard 
hats are required (i.e., Q2) or to find the hazard source (task and crew), 
allowing the management to do comparative searches to facilitate de -
cision making that can potentially improve the safety on the site (i.e., 
Q4). The reader is referred to Appendix A for comparison and additional 
technical details.

5. Interpretation of query results
In this section, we perform the queries on a high-rise construction 
project (model and schedules have been shared in [ 34 ]), which has been 
safety assessed. While the case study presented focuses on a high-rise 
construction project, the proposed framework is adaptable to various 
construction scenarios, including bridge construction and industrial fa -
cilities. The underlying principles of knowledge graph construction and 
query formulation remain consistent across different project types. For 
instance, in bridge construction, the framework can integrate safety 
analysis with structural health monitoring data to provide real-time 
insights into potential hazards. Similarly, in industrial facilities, the 
framework can be used to monitor safety compliance and optimise 
maintenance schedules.
The BIM model, schedule, and safety analysis information have been 
captured in the proposed knowledge graph, the example queries are 
showcased, and the query results are interpreted and explained.
Q1: “ What hazards are work crew #X exposed to while they complete 
task #Y? ” .
In the previous section, the question was turned into a query, which 
was then wrapped in the following predicate:
exposures_of_crew_doing_task(Crew_id,Task_id,List_of_exposures).

the input is kept as variables (i.e., starting with an uppercase letter), the 
engine seeks and returns combinations of values that satisfy the body of 
the rule. If one or more variables are fixed (e.g., replacing Crew_id with 
the number 1), the engine only returns the combination where crew id is 
1, which satisfies the body.
