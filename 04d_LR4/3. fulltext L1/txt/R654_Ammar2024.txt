Abstract
Digital T wins have emerged as a transformative technology with immense potential for enhancing asset data management in
various industries. The transportation industry relies heavily on properly functioning and maintaining safety hardware assets,
such as crash barriers, guiderails, and road signs, among others, to ensure the safety of road users. However , traditional asset
data management practices for ancillary assets often fall short of providing real-time, comprehensive insights into the condi-
tion and performance of these critical assets. Conversely, Digital T wins can analyze data for predictive and preventive mainte-
nance using cutting-edge technologies. This will enable asset managers to proactively address potential safety concerns, plan
maintenance schedules efficiently, and optimize resource allocation. This can be achieved by leveraging historical asset data
and real-time condition updates and providing the Digital T win with an information-rich analytical model. The key steps to
creating an information-rich analytical model are identifying the asset data that should be collected, understanding data archi-
tecture throughout the asset lifecycle, and generating asset data governance and stewardship frameworks. As such, this paper
investigates the current state of practice for ancillary asset data collection and management and investigates the generation of
information-rich analytical models for guiderails, a critical roadway safety hardware. Moreover , this study demonstrates how
Digital T wins can be integrated and affect the development of transportation asset management systems. The findings of this
article emphasize the significance of adopting Digital T wins in transportation asset data management. The case example show-
cases a compelling roadmap for other transportation agencies to consider integrating digital twins into their asset manage-
ment strategies.

Background
Roadway Safety Hardware: Overview and Challenges
Roadway safety hardware is considered the last line of
defense against roadway incidents and fatalities ( 13).
Roadway safety hardware includes longitudinal barriers
(such as guiderails), barrier terminals (such as guiderail
end treatments), crash cushions, and breakaway hard-
ware (such as signs, signals, and light posts) ( 13).
The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA), in
collaboration with the American Association of State
Highway Transportation Officials (AASHTO), is con-
tinuously working to improve roadside safety hardware.
They are considering a three-fold approach: (a) establish-
ing rigorous and standardized crash testing standards,
such as the AASHTO’s Manual for Assessing Safety
Hardware (MASH) to guide manufacturers in develop-
ing safety hardware and take into account the wide range
of vehicles and consider potential crashes with a diverse
range of conditions ( 14); (b) collaborate with local agen-
cies and state DOTs to investigate the proper device
installment and maintenance practices of roadside safety
hardware and offer support through specialized training
programs ( 13); and (c) develop in-service performance
evaluation (ISPE) programs to monitor how these
devices are performing continuously, collect safety
hardware-related crash data and communicate with state
agencies with respect to crash reporting, and manage
data to optimize the effectiveness of hardware inventory
and maintenance practices ( 13).
Although these practices and safety approaches are
continuously updated, the unsettling aspect of safety
hardware fatalities lies in the heightened crash severity
they entail. The main objective of roadway safety hard-
ware is to reduce severe crashes by judiciously imple-
menting barriers and ensuring their effectiveness when

shielding roadside obstacles or terrain features; however,
in 2021, approximately 20% of motor vehicle crash fatal-
ities occurred when a vehicle veered off the road and col-
lided with a stationary object alongside the roadway
(15). During such incidents, the most frequently struck
objects are trees, utility poles, and traffic barriers, where
18% of the fatalities happened on interstate and free-
ways, 38% on other major roads, and 42% on minor
roads (15).
A report by the U.S. Government Accountability
Office (GAO) reviewed the FHWA’s framework for road-
side safety hardware. It investigated the extent to which
information is available on roadside safety hardware’s per-
formance once installed and found that ISPE information
is limited. On the other hand, having strong and complete
data sets is crucial for ISPE and asset management analy-
sis. This allows researchers to thoroughly examine the
safety equipment and evaluate its performance in different
roadside and crash scenarios ( 16). With this valuable
information, researchers can expedite the ISPE process
and gain more accurate insights into safety equipment
installation and maintenance needs (16). As such, FHWA
advises state DOTs to improve their asset management
practices and consistently track the safety hardware on
roadways by gathering and maintaining high-quality
information for data-driven decision-making. In addition,
FHWA encourages the inclusion of safety assets in trans-
portation asset management plans (TAMPs) to acquire
quality information and provide data analysis for an opti-
mized ISPE program (16).
State DOTs are facing several challenges in managing
safety hardware effectively, where the current practices
for roadway asset management primarily focus on identi-
fying asset locations through periodic inventory sche-
dules (e.g., annual or every five years) ( 10, 16). Collecting
this data is challenging, leading state DOTs to seek help
from service providers equipped with mobile technolo-
gies such as LiDAR and navigational sensors; however,
these practices often overlook roadside hardware assets
(16). Roadside safety hardware data, such as installation
dates, installers, and repair history, is not effectively col-
lected or recorded by state DOTs and service providers
(10, 16). Having this information could enhance integra-
tion into safety asset management programs. However,
maintaining an up-to-date database poses difficulty since
the accuracy and availability of such data sets are highly
dependent on scattered hardcopy documentation across
various departments and may require additional infor-
mation requests from manufacturers and installers ( 16).
Also, the lack of data sharing hinders the comprehensive
management of such assets ( 16). Implementing technolo-
gies for transportation asset management and improving
state DOTs’ digital capabilities have proven beneficial
(17–19). As such, it is critical to investigate the
implementation of emerging technologies, namely Digital
Twins, that would support collecting, processing, and
analyzing the asset data and provide data accessibility
and integration with management systems.
Digital T wins for T ransportation Asset Data
Management
State DOTs adopt data-intensive asset lifecycle manage-
ment systems to support decisions that seek to (a) effec-
tively operate and maintain physical assets, (b) enhance
and expand their service life, and (c) optimize their per-
formance (11). Thus, with the Digital Twins’ capabilities
of connecting the enterprise asset data and extracting
actionable insights ( 10, 20), implementing Digital Twins
for transportation asset management will support state
DOTs in leveraging their transportation asset manage-
ment practices and improving their asset management
maturity.
Ammar et al. ( 9) defined Digital Twins for infrastruc-
ture assets as the digital representation of a physical asset
or systems of assets connected bi-directionally with their
physical counterparts. In their definition, they empha-
sized that it is crucial to note that data in the infrastruc-
ture sector is incredibly diverse and can be sourced from
various locations. These sources include semantic asset
data from physical assets and their digital representa-
tions, spatial data from the surrounding environment,
and data collected by intelligent transportation technolo-
gies such as fixed sensors and mobile or remote transpor-
tation data collection technologies ( 9). The authors
further emphasized that Digital Twins can integrate his-
torical asset data and updated asset condition informa-
tion to create data-rich analytical models, which can
then be used with technologies such as artificial intelli-
gence and machine learning to facilitate predictive and
preventive maintenance, thus supporting the operation
and management of infrastructure assets ( 9).
Implementing Digital Twins for transportation assets
has mainly focused on managing pavement performance
and predicting the performance of highway materials
(21–24), supporting a lifecycle management analysis for
tunnels ( 25, 26), developing a Digital Twin-driven man-
agement system to support bridge maintenance ( 27, 28),
and deploying Digital Twins as a traffic management
and control system for railways ( 29, 30). More recently,
the authors of Ammar et al. ( 9, 10) investigated the cur-
rent state of practices of state DOTs toward digitizing
ancillary transportation asset data to implement the
vision of Digital Twins to support transportation asset
management. Also, they contextualized the state DOTs’
maturity in the advancement of digital processes.
Integrating Digital Twins in the transportation sector
can enhance the effectiveness of managing ITS

Research Gaps, Objectives, and Significance
Previous research investigated the benefits of implement-
ing Digital Twins for transportation asset management.
Still, the impact of its implementation on the development
of transportation asset management systems (AMSs) has
not been investigated. On the other hand, state DOTs are
facing challenges in managing the data of their safety
hardware assets, and the data necessary to conduct ISPE
is limited. To contribute to filling this gap, this study is
part of ongoing research to investigate and document the
state of practice in collecting and managing ancillary
transportation asset data to investigate the readiness of
state DOTs to adopt information and communication
technologies, namely Digital Twins, and to support and
guide state DOTs in navigating this journey. Since the
ancillary asset systems include various asset classes that
cannot be comprehensively investigated within one study,
this paper aims to highlight a roadway hardware asset
where the guiderail was selected as the asset of interest. As
such, this study aims to achieve the following objectives:
(1) assess the current practices of state DOTs in collecting
and managing the data for guiderails, a roadway safety
hardware asset; (2) understand and document where state
DOTs stand in collecting and digitizing the data for guide-
rails; and (3) investigate the impact of implementing the
vision of Digital Twins in managing the data of a road-
way hardware asset on the development of AMSs.
It is crucial to comprehend and record how state
DOTs collect and manage data related to roadway safety
hardware assets. This understanding is vital for enhancing
roadway safety, pinpointing data deficiencies essential for
informed decision-making, ensuring compliance with
FHWA guidelines, and establishing a centralized knowl-
edge hub for state DOTs to handle the data for roadway
safety hardware assets effectively. This will enable state
DOTs to proactively approach roadway safety and their
goal of zero fatalities, prioritize their safety investments,
continually improve infrastructure and systems to prevent
accidents, protect road users, and effectively allocate
resources. In addition, investigating the impact of imple-
menting Digital Twins on managing a roadway safety
hardware asset can lead to more advanced asset manage-
ment practices and improve asset management maturity,
optimized maintenance strategies, enhanced safety, and
better-informed decision-making. Implementing Digital
Twins will empower transportation agencies to make
proactive and data-driven decisions, ultimately contribut-
ing to improved roadway safety and infrastructure
efficiency.
Methods
This study is part of an ongoing effort to capture the cur-
rent practices of state DOTs in collecting and managing
their ancillary asset data. As such, a web-based survey was
developed, piloted with subject matter experts from the
Kentucky Transportation Cabinet (KYTC) and Utah
State DOT (UDOT), and approved by the Office of
Research Integrity at the University of Kentucky
(Institutional Review Board [IRB] # 69802) before distri-
bution. The survey was distributed to the AASHTO
Committee on Maintenance members and the FHWA
asset management contact list. The survey captured the
current practices of state D OTs in managing 34 ancillary
asset classes. A total of 45 complete responses were
received from 30 state DOTs, as presented in Figure 1.
Responses were collected from several divisions within the
surveyed state DOT personnel with knowledge of ancillary
asset maintenance and operation (59% of the responses),
ancillary asset data management and analysis (23% of the
responses), and other expertise (18% of the responses).
Examples of respondent positions include bureau chiefs,
managers, directors, and state-wide engineers.
The operation and maintenance of transportation ancil-
lary assets vary by asset class; accordingly, the needed
asset data might vary. For instance, the data needed to
manage culverts might vary from those needed for traffic
signals management. This paper emphasizes the guiderail
ancillary asset class, a roadway safety hardware asset, to
establish a deeper understanding of the current practices
of state DOTs in collecting and managing the data of
roadway safety hardware assets. In addition, this paper
aims to thoroughly examine how implementing Digital
Twins can affect the development of AMSs for roadway
safety hardware. To understand the impact of implement-
ing Digital Twins to manage the data of guiderails, it is
critical to develop a deeper comprehension of the data
requirements and flow and capture the guiderail asset data
lifecycle, data governance, and stewardship frameworks.
As such, a case study with subject matter experts from
Connecticut DOT (CTDOT) was conducted. A summary
of the research methodology is presented in Figure 2.
Web-Based Survey
The conducted web-based survey was distributed over
two sections. The first section covered the current prac-
tices of state DOTs in collecting ancillary asset data, and

the second was related to ancillary asset data manage-
ment. For ancillary asset data collection, six major data
collection practices were categorized (i.e., variables), and
each category was described by several subcategories
(i.e., variable levels). For each ancillary asset, for each
variable, respondents filled in a level defining whether
and to what extent they collected data related to the vari-
able. Respondents were allowed to select more than one
level for some variable levels.
The six major ancillary asset data collection variables
and their associated variable levels are as follows.
1. Data format: the format in which the asset data
is collected (paper-based, portable document for-
mat [PDF], smart PDF [i.e., fillable PDF], two-
dimensional [2D] models, and three-dimensional
[3D] models).
2. Data level of detail: the level of detail in which the
asset data is collected (not available, L4, L3, L2,
and L1).
3. Data collection features: the different asset fea-
tures collected (asset location [i.e., indexed loca-
tion of the asset], asset dimensions, asset material
type and properties, and asset condition).
4. Data collection/construction phase: the construc-
tion phase during which the asset data is collected
(design, construction, project close-out [as-built],
and maintenance).
5. Data collection techniques: the technique used for
the asset data collection (manual collection, auto-
mated collection, and remote collection).
6. Data collection frequency: the frequency with
which the asset data is collected (monthly, quar-
terly, semi-annually, yearly, more than a year).

Results
Current Practices of State DOTs in Collecting and
Digitizing Guiderail Asset Data
Out of the 45 collected responses, 38 respondents (i.e.,
84% of the responses) indicated that they collect data for
the guiderail ancillary asset class. This further emphasizes
the critical role played by guiderails to improve roadway
safety. The collected data were aggregated and analyzed,
and the percentage of responses for each variable level
was calculated. Figure 3 summarizes the current state of
practice of state DOTs in collecting guiderail asset data
for the six ancillary asset data collection variables and
variable levels investigated, and the key findings are dis-
cussed in the following.
Data Collection Format. The current state of practice of
state DOTs on the collected data format for guiderails
indicates that state DOTs mainly adopt 2D models (39%
of responses), followed by paper-based ones (24% of
responses). In comparison, smart PDF is the least
adopted format for guiderail data collection (5% of
responses).
Data Collection Level of Detail. The current state of practice
of state DOTs on the collected level of detail for guide-
rails indicates that state DOTs mainly adopt a level of
detail L3 for guiderail data collection (i.e., sufficient
detail to conduct elementary methods of maintenance)
(36% of responses), followed by a level of detail L4 (i.e.,
basic details with summary statistics of inventory, perfor-
mance, and utilization) (26% of responses). However, the
results indicate that state DOTs do not collect guiderail
asset data with higher comprehensive levels of detail, that
is, L2 and L1, where the percentages for adopting these
two levels pertain to less than 10% of the responses.
Data Collection Features. The current state of practice of
state DOTs on the collected data features for guiderails,

Guiderail Data Architecture, Data Governance, and
Stewardship Frameworks
After capturing and documenting the current practices of
state DOTs in collecting and managing guiderail data, it
is critical to understand the guiderail asset data architec-
ture, asset data governance, and asset data stewardship
frameworks. As such, an interview was conducted with
subject matter experts from CTDOT.
Guiderail Data Architecture. Understanding data architec-
ture is fundamental for an enterprise since it creates and
maintains organizational knowledge about data and its
lifecycle. This understanding supports enterprise data
management and leveraging the asset data’s value to opti-
mize asset performance and decrease the risk of asset fail-
ure ( 35). The first section of the interview focused on
documenting the guiderail asset data lifecycle and achiev-
ing a better understanding of the guiderail asset data
architecture. The deployed asset data lifecycle framework
was adopted from Ammar et al. ( 36) ,w h e r et h el i f eo fa
transportation asset commences with its construction. It
undergoes several rehabilitation and renewal activities,
which would occur as maintenance work orders before
eventually deteriorating ove r time or because of natural
forces. However, data related to the asset extends before
its construction phase, originating from the conceptual
asset design and planning stages and continuing through
to the asset construction phase to reflect its as-built condi-
tion. Moreover, this asset data continues to expand and
support various operations and maintenance activities,
including rehabilitation or replacement, until the asset
becomes obsolete. At this point, the associated data is
archived or disposed of. As a result, most asset data accu-
mulates during the operation and maintenance phase of
the asset’s lifecycle, where it is widely recognized that
operation and maintenance represent the largest expendi-
ture and value within the transportation system (36).
The interviewees were asked to apply the adopted
transportation asset data lifecycle ecosystem (TADLE),
as presented in Figure 5 ( 36), for guiderails. The TADLE
is initiated with the first stage of the guiderail asset data
lifecycle, the asset design data model. During the asset
design data model, the guiderail data will mainly consti-
tute a CAD model that will include design data features
such as the status or work type. For instance, if the asset
is newly constructed or rehabilitated, the pay or the bid
item and data features, such as the item description and
the asset’s exact geo-referenced location, are necessary.
Such data will support the fabrication of guiderails by
using the design data features stored within the CAD
model, such as the asset dimensions, material, descrip-
tion, how the asset will be paid for, and the asset loca-
tion, to enable better asset location in the field. The next
phase of the guiderail asset data lifecycle is the asset pro-
duction data , where the asset data will pass through a
temporary pending inventory status before the asset is
given an identification number within the implemented
GIS inventory databases. After this temporary stage, the

data related to the designed guiderail will be stored and
referred to using a unique identification number, associ-
ated work types, and project information, all saved
within the GIS inventory databases. The guiderail asset
data is stored and can be easily extracted using the
unique identification number or the asset geolocation.
For instance, if this is a new construction, the guiderail
can be installed, and the asset will physically exist.
After the guiderail asset is placed into service, the
guiderail asset data will be referred to as the as-built
guiderail asset data . The as-built asset data should cap-
ture information related to validating the asset’s exact
installation geolocation and any updates that the asset
went through during the asset installation and would
require adjustment of the previously referred to ‘‘design
data model,’’ where any changes in the asset condition
should be documented in the now ‘‘as-built data model.’’
If the change is because of a change in the contract, the
updates are captured using a change order; however, if
the changes were conducted in the field, the updated
information should be captured and documented using a
daily work report (DWR).
Following the asset’s as-built data, the guiderail asset
lifecycle will be initiated, and the guiderail asset will be
operated and maintained through multiple maintenance
and rehabilitation treatments. The guiderail operation
data will include information related to the scheduled
maintenance activities, roadway inspection, and asset
condition data. For instance, it is important to capture if
the change in the guiderail condition is caused by crashes
and the frequency of occurrences, since this might reflect
a highway safety problem that requires design interven-
tion. Moreover, the guiderail asset maintenance and
rehabilitation data will capture work order information
and cost information, such as the cost of the workforce
and tools or equipment used to maintain the guiderail.
This type of information is usually saved in the asset
maintenance management system (MMS).
Conversely, if the installed guiderail becomes obsolete
and requires replacement, the guiderail replacement
could take two routes: if the guiderail asset is replaced
through a capital program (i.e., designed and paid
through a capital program), the asset data lifecycle is re-
initiated with the design data model stage. However, sup-
pose the guiderail asset is replaced through a mainte-
nance work order. In that case, maintenance workers
will replace the obsolete guiderail, and the new guiderail
will be given the same old identification number. Thus,
even if the guiderail asset no longer physically exists, the
corresponding data will be archived, and the data of
the obsolete guiderail will be decommissioned from the
inventory. Asset history information is recorded in
the inventory with information related to the prior asset,
the initial date of installation, service life, removal condi-
tion, asset materials, and cost of preservation throughout
the guiderail lifecycle. The adopted TADLE framework
for guiderails is a comprehensive approach to present
and summarize the information required at each phase
of the guiderail asset data lifecycle, which should be
readily available for the following phase and would allow

Guiderail Data Governance and Data Stewardship
Frameworks
Data governance outlines the responsibilities and
accountability pathways concerning asset data manage-
ment to fulfill its business objectives and ensure adher-
ence to relevant legislation, regulations, and business
standards ( 37). Conversely, data stewardship refers to
the designated and entrusted lines of accountability for
business responsibilities, distinct from IT responsibilities.
The primary goal of data stewardship is to ensure the
effective control and utilization of data and information
assets ( 37). As such, the second section of the interview
focused on comprehending the guiderail asset data gov-
ernance and data stewardship frameworks.
To ensure the fulfillment of the CTDOT mission of
providing a safe, efficient, and cost-effective transporta-
tion system that meets the mobility needs of its users,
CTDOT’s objective is to develop and adopt asset data
governance and data stewardship frameworks that
would enable the complete population of intelligence by
including all the necessary and applicable data that sup-
port informative insights in the asset design model, that
is, at the first stage of the asset data lifecycle and critical
to flow to the following stages, for instance the asset geo-
location. Also, CTDOT is establishing an asset data gov-
ernance framework based on a well-structured
foundation for the ‘‘digital as-built’’ data of the asset. In
the context of the overarching data governance frame-
work, they developed a set of policy instruments, includ-
ing a digital project development manual (DPD), to
support standardizing the asset design model. Within the
developed DPD manual, CTDOT has a dedicated sec-
tion called electronic engineering data (EED). This sec-
tion outlines the specifications for consultant-led and in-
house designed projects, providing guidance on the con-
tent to be included in the design model with respect to
2D and 3D elements. EED will be continuously updated
to align with the latest version of newly adopted soft-
ware, such as OpenRoads, and utilize data dictionaries
developed by other state DOTs, such as those developed
by the Florida DOT and UDOT. The aim is to enhance
the visual presentation by adopting a spreadsheet-like
format and incorporating distinct levels of detail. For
instance, adopting a new software highlighted the impor-
tance of making the data requirements in CAD more
user-friendly and understandable. It is critical to ensure
that users clearly understand how the data should appear
in the design model and the significance of certain data
components. The interviewees also highlighted that even
though relevant data governance guidelines are in place,
continuous improvements are being made to align the
current practices with the desired outcomes.
To implement data stewardship frameworks, CTDOT
has a chief data officer and designated asset stewards
responsible for managing specific assets. The chief data
officer is vital in providing support in various capacities.
However, there is a need to clarify the roles and responsi-
bilities of these asset stewards for future improvement.
Their primary task is to ensure that the data related to
asset conditions is regularly updated to support effective
asset management. This emphasizes the importance of
data management and assigning enough responsibility to
the asset stewards, where maintaining an accurate and
up-to-date inventory is essential. In addition to data stor-
age and acquisition, other important factors must be con-
sidered when managing resources. For instance, since
decision-making is critical, assessing new data sets to cap-
ture asset conditions, such as aerial imagery, might be
needed. This responsibility primarily falls on the data
steward in charge.
Furthermore, CTDOT is currently setting targets for
2024 and improving its MMS to include more asset
information and a wide variety of ancillary assets,
namely guiderails. In addition, CTDOT is piloting a
project to implement BIM with guiderails and exploring
how to manage the underlying data by incorporating
attribution into early design models. This allows inspec-
tors to avoid dealing with attributes unless changes or
specific issues arise. CTDOT is developing processes that
enable them to write back information to their assets by
creating a common data environment (CDE) that inte-
grates project management information, project informa-
tion models, and asset information models. The
developed CDE supports the integration with a project
estimation software (e.g., AASHTOware) and an intern-
ally developed enterprise GIS application (ATLAS 2.0
ESRI WebMap App). This approach enables CTDOT to
continuously update its processes as they advance in
managing different assets. CTDOT is currently investi-
gating the implementation of the developed approach to
managing guiderails and how it affects multiple stake-
holders with different responsibilities.
Discussion
Implementing Digital T wins to Support the
Management of Guiderail Assets
Digital Twins represents an innovative solution to man-
age assets throughout their lifecycle by collecting and
integrating asset data to improve the design, construc-
tion, operation, and maintenance of infrastructure assets,
resulting in the sustainable development of infrastructure
systems. Data is the core of the definition of Digital
Twins for transportation assets, where the asset data
126 T ransportation Research Record 2678(10)
plays a crucial role in connecting the physical asset to its
digital representation. However, emphasizing the impor-
tance of data does not imply that Digital Twins should
be constructed using an overwhelming number of data-
bases that are challenging to understand and process.
Moreover, implementing Digital Twins for asset manage-
ment does not necessarily require that all assets be repre-
sented in 3D models; in contrast, Digital Twins should
be built on databases containing high-quality informa-
tion that is easily accessible, extractable, updatable, and
integrable ( 9). As such, state DOTs need to invest in
improving asset data quality, prioritize assets that should
be represented in 3D models, and identify assets that can
be represented using comprehensive 2D models with the
necessary data attributes to manage assets. Ammar et al.
(38) referred to high-quality data as informative data
that enhances analytical asset models, such as asset
inventory data, asset geolocation, and updated asset con-
dition data, among other data attributes. These data ele-
ments are essential for extracting informative insights for
creating predictive and preventive maintenance strategies
and providing valuable insights for decision-making.
The findings from the conducted survey showed that
50% of the respondents indicated that the desired guide-
rail asset management approach is ‘‘cyclic-driven plus,’’
where it is important to collect condition data and regu-
larly inspect and maintain guiderails. Guiderail condition
data should also be associated with the scheduled main-
tenance activities, roadway inspection data, work order
information, and cost information needed to maintain
the guiderail. State DOTs need to improve the level of
detail of the collected data to include comprehensive
asset data (i.e., level of detail L2). This can be enabled by
collecting guiderail data using automated and remote
technologies such as LiDAR and aerial imagery. It can
also be achieved by adding necessary attributes to the
asset design model and collecting the as-built asset data.
Moreover, findings indicated a risk-based management
approach could be considered to manage guiderails, thus
emphasizing the importance of the asset in enhancing
roadway safety. As such, the guiderail is a suitable candi-
date for implementing Digital Twins. Ammar et al. ( 9)
reported the same findings.
A key step of creating a Digital Twin for managing
guiderail assets is understanding and identifying the data
types and attributes needed. This research indicates that
guiderail data sets should include the asset design model,
which can be a comprehensive 2D model enriched with
data attributes, including design data features, pay or bid
item, asset exact geolocation, asset dimension, material,
and description. Using extraction, transformation, and
loading (ETL) processes, relevant project information
from the asset design model, which could be exported
from BIM, is integrated with GIS inventory databases,
the asset’s unique identification number, and the associ-
ated work types.
Establishing asset data management maturity is criti-
cal to optimize guiderail management and successfully
implement Digital Twins. This will enable the inclusion
of informative data in the guiderail asset design model
and allow for better data architecture and governance
throughout the asset data lifecycle. Asset data maturity
and Digital Twins implementation can be achieved by
creating a CDE that provides a single version of asset
data and enables all parties to communicate and collabo-
rate securely and integrate data from multiple resources,
such as BIM and GISs. ISO19650 3.3.15 defines CDE as
an ‘‘agreed source of information for any given project
or asset, for collecting, managing and disseminating each
information container through a managed process’’ ( 39).
As such, CDE is necessary for creating a Digital Twin
and is an essential outcome for asset data flow and inte-
gration throughout the asset lifecycle, where data
extracted from different management systems, such as
project management, asset information, cost, and project
estimation, must be included. Thus, collecting, storing,
and managing guiderail asset data within an established
CDE would improve the asset lifecycle operation and
maintenance. The established CDE should be able to
integrate data sets that can be easily extracted, trans-
ferred, and loaded back to the developed data environ-
ment. Furthermore, it is essential to have established
asset management information systems (AMISs) where
informative insights can be extracted, thus enabling state
DOTs to reap the benefits of implementing Digital Twins
and conduct predictive and preventive maintenance, in
addition to developing asset deterioration analysis to
enable cross-asset data-driven decisions supporting mul-
tiple objectives.
Conclusions
Digital Twins have emerged as a transformative technol-
ogy with immense potential for enhancing asset data
management across various industries, particularly trans-
portation. The proper functioning and maintenance of
safety hardware assets, such as guiderails, are critical for
ensuring the safety of road users. However, traditional
asset data management practices often lack real-time,
comprehensive insights into these crucial assets. As such,
this study investigated the current practices of state
DOTs to collect and manage ancillary asset data with a
primary focus on guiderails by developing and distribut-
ing a web-based survey. A case study was also conducted
to provide a detailed understanding of the guiderail data
architecture, governance, and stewardship. This under-
standing will enable the development of information-rich
analytical models for guiderails using Digital Twins and
Ammar et al 127
identifying relevant asset data throughout the asset
lifecycle.
The responses from the survey showed that guiderail
data could be presented using 2D models. However,
high-quality asset data, including asset inventory, geolo-
cation, updated condition data, and other attributes, is
crucial in enhancing analytical asset models. It is impor-
tant to include attributes in the asset design model in
addition to collecting the as-built asset data (i.e., during
the asset construction close-out). Also, the survey indi-
cated that guiderails should be managed using an estab-
lished AMS with modeling capabilities that can provide
informative insights. The preferable guiderail asset main-
tenance management approach can be a cyclic-driven
plus, where guiderails should be maintained cyclically,
and the inspection and maintenance activities should be
performed simultaneously. Condition data is collected
and maintained for analysis that could lead to additional
condition-based decisions.
Implementing Digital Twins for managing guiderails
requires condition data and integrating automated and
remote technologies such as LiDAR and aerial imagery
for comprehensive data collection. Also, establishing
asset data management maturity is essential to optimize
guiderail management through Digital Twins. This
involves creating a CDE and integrating data extracted
from BIM and GISs. Data from various management
systems, such as project management, asset information,
cost, and estimation, must be included in the CDE.
Moreover, adhering to ISO 19650 guidelines for manag-
ing information containers in a managed process facili-
tates efficient data collection, storage, and dissemination,
ultimately improving the asset lifecycle’s operation and
maintenance. Successful implementation of Digital
Twins in transportation asset management requires
established asset management information systems capa-
ble of extracting informative insights. This enables the
benefits of predictive and preventive maintenance, asset
deterioration analysis, and data-driven decision-making
with multiple objectives and contributes to the future
vision of ITSs.
Future research will investigate the implementation of
Digital Twins for other critical transportation assets.
Furthermore, it will develop smart asset data manage-
ment plans to guide state DOTs in navigating the jour-
ney of digitizing and digitalizing their asset management
processes to successfully implement Digital Twins for
transportation asset management.
