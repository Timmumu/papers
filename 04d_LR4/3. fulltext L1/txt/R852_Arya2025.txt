ABSTRACT
Despite the digitalization of architecture, engineering, and construction, construction-site safety 
is still limited by fragmented data, non-computable regulations, and slow decision processes. This 
paper presents DTSafe-O, a semantic digital twin for safety management that integrates an ontology 
formalized in Web Ontology Language 2 (Description Logic profile), guided by Semantic Web Rule 
Language and Shapes Constraint Language and connecting Internet of Things sensors, building 
information modeling, and regulatory documents. The contribution is twofold: (a) a computable 
formalization of safety-critical requirements enabling explainable and verifiable inferences and (b) a 
validation pipeline combining reasoning, compliance checking, and performance metrics, supported 
by replicable materials in a public repository. Results show that merging digital twins with semantic 
technologies improves consistency, timeliness, and auditability of safety decisions, laying the ground 
for scalable industrial adoption. The study also discusses limitations and opportunities for extending 
the approach to infrastructure domains and advancing spatiotemporal reasoning and uncertainty 
management.

INTRODUCTION
Safety on construction sites represents a primary challenge for the construction industry, marked 
by elevated worker risks and considerable repercussions of accidents on project timetables, costs, and 
reputation. Notwithstanding substantial investments in training, equipment, and safety protocols, the

prevalence of fatal and severe accidents is alarming, underscoring the necessity for new and effective 
management and prevention techniques (Lorusso & Celenta, 2023).
In recent years, digital twin (DT), a dynamic and current digital representation of a physical object 
or environment, has emerged as a viable alternative for proactive safety management on building 
sites, due to the capacity of DTs to amalgamate data from diverse sources (Internet of Things [IoT] 
sensors, drones, cameras, BIM [building information modeling]), continuous and comprehensive 
monitoring of the building site can be conducted, anticipating dangerous scenarios and initiating 
prompt and precise actions (Hagedorn et al., 2023a; Hong & Teizer, 2025).
The objective of this project is to design, formalize, and validate Digital Twin Safety Ontology 
(DTSafe-O), a semantically driven DT for construction site safety management, which can integrate 
diverse data sources (sensors, video analytics, BMS [building management systems]/SCADA 
[supervisory control and data acquisition] systems, wearables), information models (BIM), and 
regulatory documentation into a queryable and explicable knowledge graph. The proposal aligns 
with research on developing ontologies in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) 
sector and the increasing integration of BIM, IoT, and the Semantic Web (Casillo, Colace, et al., 
2024; Colace et al., 2014). Still, it seeks to address the deficiency in comprehensive solutions tailored 
for temporary or mobile construction sites, where safety necessitates prompt reasoning, regulatory 
traceability, and interoperability with construction site operations (Farghaly et al., 2023; Pauwels, 
Zhang, et al., 2017). The contribution includes a domain ontology for safety featuring Semantic 
Web Rule Language (SWRL) rules and Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL) shapes that delineate 
obligations and operational relationships; a integration mechanism with Semantic Sensor Network 
ontology (SSN)/sensor, observation, sample, and actuator (SOSA), Industry Foundation Classes Web 
Ontology Language (ifcOWL), and Brick Schema (BRICK) standards, as well as Information Delivery 
Specification (IDS) profiles and Information Container for Linked Document Delivery (ICDD) 
containers for the exchange and storage of information packages; a experimental validation utilizing 
synthetic and field data with metrics for correctness, coverage, and performance; and replicability 
materials (ontology, shape, p) (Colace et al., 2013).This system enables the conversion of raw signals 
into substantiated and verifiable judgments, consistent with the regulatory framework and quantifiable 
through operational key performance indicators (KPIs; Teizer et al., 2022). The work falls within 
the European framework for health and safety protection on construction sites defined by Directive 
89/391/European Economic Community (EEC) and Directive 92/57/EEC on temporary or mobile 
construction sites, implemented in Italy by Legislative Decree 81/2008 (Title IV). With regard to 
systems management, the principles of International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 45001 
are relied upon. With regard to the interoperability of information, reference is made to the ISO 19650 
standard for information management, the IDS for the definition of information requirements and the 
ICDD (ISO 21597) for the traceable delivery of documents.
This work addresses four research questions (RQ) based on these objectives.
• RQ1, computable safety modeling: What ontological constructs and patterns, such as Web 
Ontology Language 2, Description Logic profile (OWL 2 DL), SWRL rules, and SHACL 
constraints, are essential and adequate to accurately represent the safety requirements derived 
from Directive 92/57/EEC, Legislative Decree 81/2008, and ISO 45001, while maintaining 
explainability and verifiability?
• RQ2, integration and interoperability: To what degree does alignment with SSN/SOSA, BRICK, 
and ifcOWL, along with the application of IDS profiles and ICDD containers, facilitate an 
end-to-end observe–deduce–validate process among sensors, BIM, and documents, thereby 
minimizing ambiguity and enabling automated checks?
• RQ3, effectiveness and performance: To what degree does the DTSafe-O instance facilitate prompt 
and dependable inferences on actual streams, such as coverage of competency questions (CQs), 
SHACL compliance of the assertional box (A-Box), latency, and classification efficacy, and what

The absence of a unified language and a collective conceptual framework constrains this 
technology's extensive and systematic implementation in construction site safety. The lack of 
standardization hinders the replication and scalability of DT-based solutions, obstructing the industry's 
ability to leverage these modern technologies' potential fully (Haller et al., 2018).
The practical implementation of digital technologies in construction site safety remains nascent, 
primarily limited to pilot projects or university research endeavors. The primary challenges are 
technological complexity, fragmentation of current technologies, integration difficulties with existing 
systems, and a widespread absence of internationally recognized standards and recommendations 
(Balaji et al., 2018)
Nonetheless, experimental cases demonstrate DTs' enhanced efficacy in accident prevention. 
The utilization of DTs to track the real-time locations of personnel and vehicles facilitates the prompt 
detection of potentially perilous scenarios, including unauthorized entry into hazardous zones or 
hazardous interactions between operators and machines (Cecere et al., 2025; Pauwels, Krijnen, et 
al., 2017). A viable option to tackle the aforementioned significant difficulties is the implementation 
of particular ontologies, which facilitate the establishment of a formal, organized, and universally 
comprehensible language for representing and disseminating domain-specific knowledge (Biagini 
et al., 2024). Utilizing ontologies enables the standardization of risk scenario descriptions and 
classifications, enhances interoperability among diverse digital systems, and promotes the automation 
of safety protocols (Hagedorn et al., 2023b).
This research introduces a novel ontology for DTs concerning safety on building sites. This 
method signifies a substantial advancement, since it seeks to address the fragmentation and 
interpretative diversity inherent in the sector, establishing a robust foundation for the extensive and 
uniform application of DTs in safety procedures. In this context, DT denotes a knowledge graph 
synchronized with sensory streams and information models, capable of executing semantic rules 
and constraints (OWL/SWRL/SHACL) to generate explainable and auditable judgments in real time 
(Casillo, Cecere, et al., 2024).
The research aims to create and validate an ontology for applying DTs in safety management on 
building sites. This tool will provide a clear and consistent definition of DTs’ uses and application 
scenarios, enabling their comprehension, acceptance, and integration into actual operational processes.
Safety management on construction sites currently utilizes various sensors, BIM/asset models, 
and procedures/regulations; however, these elements are seldom integrated through computable 
semantics that can convert signals and documents into prompt and verifiable decisions (Lu et 
al., 2021; Tao et al., 2022). This paper introduces DTSafe-O, an ontology-centric framework that 
incorporates OWL 2 DL, SWRL, and SHACL, in accordance with the SSN/SOSA, BRICK, ifcOWL 
standards, and IDS/ICDD profiles. The primary innovation is facilitating computable and explainable 
compliance, wherein measures, procedures, and documents are interconnected with World Wide Web 
Consortium Provenance Ontology (PROV-O) traceability and operationalized within the observe–
deduce–validate loop. DTSafe-O transcends conventional DTs, which typically focus solely on data 
integration, by offering a cohesive conceptual framework that articulates operational obligations 
and relationships as formal constraints and rules while ensuring near real-time performance through 
selective materialization and incremental validation. Assessment of synthetic and field data indicates 
that the methodology enhances decision-making efficiency and explanatory clarity, establishing a 
reproducible foundation for multi-site industrial applications.

RELATED WORK
In the last twenty years, ontologies have been increasingly significant in the AEC industry, 
transforming from descriptive taxonomies to functional semantic infrastructures that facilitate 
compliance control, data integration, and automated reasoning. A recent systematic review of 142 
articles (2000–2021) delineated the progression of ontological applications in AEC, identifying ten 
principal domains—including safety and health, process/construction site, operations and maintenance, 
sustainability, and compliance—and underscoring the necessity for reproducible methodologies 
and comparative assessments on shared datasets. This review highlights the absence of solutions 
linking safety regulations to sensory data and BIM models within a unified computable framework, 
encouraging research into integrated methodologies like the one described herein (W. T. Chen & 
Bria, 2022).
The simultaneous convergence of BIM, IoT, and semantic technologies has generated information 
flows supporting decision-making-oriented DTs. Biagini et al. (2024) examine explicitly the 
incorporation of IoT data within BIM environments, highlighting that the implementation of linked 
data and standardized vocabularies enhances interoperability and the quality of building management 
processes; this trend underscores the importance of lightweight semantic models for connecting 
real-time data streams and facilitating subsequent analysis (Biagini et al., 2024; Boje et al., 2020; 
Farghaly et al., 2022).
The World Wide Web Consortium SSN/SOSA framework establishes a reference vocabulary for 
modeling observations, implementations, and procedures, effectively connecting sensors and observed 
samples. The modularity of SOSA/SSN facilitates minimal axiomatization that accommodates 
streaming data while ensuring compatibility with other schemas. IfcOWL enables the Resource 
Description Framework (RDF) representation of Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) entities in 
building and plant engineering, facilitating connections to dictionaries and sensor graphs. At the same 
time, BRICK standardizes the semantic descriptions of assets, points, and operational relationships. 
Collectively, these schemas diminish ambiguity and mapping efforts, allowing for cross-domain 
queries and inferences. The origin of choices and processes can be articulated using PROV-O, which 
offers an OWL 2 language for monitoring entities, actions, and agents, essential for auditing and 
accountability (Fischer et al., 2024; Hagedorn et al., 2023b; Haller et al., 2018).
The ISO 19650 series governs the lifecycle of requirements and information exchanges in 
management and delivery within the construction sector. buildingSMART International's IDS are 
integrated into this framework, formalizing the requisite objects, properties, values, and units in a

machine-readable format, facilitating automated compliance verification of models. ICDD containers 
(ISO 21597) offer a standardized format for storing and interlinking heterogeneous files and RDF 
triples, incorporating indexes and information for cross-document traceability. Recent research

Web technologies to link legacy systems with RDF/SPARQL Protocol and RDF Query Language

The regulatory framework for temporary or movable construction sites is founded on Directive 
89/391/EEC (foundation directive on health and safety) and Directive 92/57/EEC (minimum 
requirements for construction sites), implemented in Italy by Legislative Decree 81/2008, Title IV. 
These sources delineate principles of prevention, obligations, roles, and safety coordination across 
the design and implementation phases. From a semantic standpoint, they establish the foundation 
for modeling shapes and regulations governing measures, procedures, and obligations concerning 
particular risk categories and occurrences. Furthermore, ISO 45001 delineates the prerequisites for 
occupational health and safety management systems, which are helpful in characterizing procedures, 
responsibilities, and continuous improvement cycles within the ontology (Kong & Ahn, 2024; Speiser 
et al., 2025).
Discrepancies and drive: Notwithstanding advancements, deficiencies persist in computable 
formalizations of safety-critical requirements pertinent to construction sites, associated with dynamic 
events and space-time constraints; inherent integration among sensors, BIM, and documentation 
within an observe–deduce–validate cycle featuring verifiable provenance; and established exchange 
and storage protocols (IDS/ICDD) that ensure semantic consistency throughout the lifecycle. The 
suggested methodology tackles these challenges through a domain ontology associated with the 
previously described standards, a rule- and shape-based pipeline, and a reproducible synthetic and 
field data assessment (Inan et al., 2023).
METHODOLOGY
A rigorous methodological approach, grounded in known scientific research principles in 
ontologies and knowledge management, was employed to design a specialized ontology for safety 
management on construction sites utilizing DT technology. This methodology was formulated based 
on theoretical and practical contributions previously provided in the scholarly literature in this domain.
The methodology employed was based on the framework established by Farghaly et al. (2023), 
which outlines a systematic approach for ontology development in the AEC sector, emphasizing 
explicit objective formulation, meticulous selection and evaluation of knowledge sources, and precise 
conceptual and formal modeling. Furthermore, the research conducted by Biagini et al. (2024) was 
considered, highlighting the significance of employing semantic technologies and interconnected 
data to enhance interoperability and efficiency in asset and information management within the 
construction sector (Madni et al., 2019).
This methodology encompasses a systematic process segmented into several primary phases. The 
initial phase involves identifying and delineating the domain of interest, followed by a comprehensive 
study and gathering requisite information sources. This is succeeded by the conceptual and formal 
ontology modeling, employing specialized semantic languages such as Web Ontology Language 
(OWL). Subsequent validation is conducted by implementing the ontology in specific case studies 
and engaging with domain experts. The method entails iterative refinement informed by feedback, 
guaranteeing the robustness, scalability, and operational viability of the ontological proposition.
In the following, we consistently used:
• ontology (DTSafe-O): the formal OWL model (classes, properties, constraints)
• knowledge base (KB): the ontology plus instantiated data (observations, procedures, documents)

Identification and Definition of the Domain of Interest
The primary aim of the initial step of the technique was to identify and precisely delineate the 
domain of interest to guarantee the relevance and efficacy of the produced ontology. This domain 
explicitly addresses safety management on building sites utilizing DT technology.
During this phase, it was crucial to delineate the ontology's primary aims and thematic scope 
with scientific precision and operational clarity, considering the distinct characteristics and unique 
requirements of construction site safety. The objectives specifically emphasized risk monitoring and 
prevention, prompt and coordinated emergency management, and advanced operator training. The 
amalgamation of data from cutting-edge technologies, including IoT sensors, wearable devices, and 
drones, enabled the delineation of accurate scenarios for the early detection and efficient management 
of dangerous situations.
The potential significance of ontologies in emergency management was examined using 
simulations and predictions from diverse sources of real-time data analysis. The prospective impact 
of virtual technologies (virtual reality [VR] and augmented reality [AR]) combined with BIM was 
delineated to provide novel and efficient operator training and instruction tools.
The theme scope was concurrently established in conjunction with industry professionals, 
including safety managers (from health, safety, and environment [HSE]), technical site managers, 
and specialized researchers. This direct communication facilitated the precise identification of typical 
risks, standard operating procedures, intervention strategies, and technology most appropriate for 
continuous and integrated safety monitoring.
This phase was enhanced by creating visual and tabular tools that succinctly summarize the 
collected information, improving comprehension and aiding subsequent conceptual modeling and 
validation processes. The generated graphics and tables, which will be elaborated upon thereafter, 
will constitute an essential component of the suggested methodological documentation.
Analysis and Collection of Knowledge Sources
The study and compilation of information sources were conducted using a methodical and 
comprehensive methodology, concentrating primarily on essential safety legislation and requirements 
for building sites. The primary emphasis was on pertinent European directives, including Directive 
89/391/EEC concerning enacting measures to enhance workers' safety and health, and Directive 92/57/
EEC regarding the minimum safety and health standards for temporary or mobile construction sites. 
These directives establish the foundation for national rules, particularly Italian Legislative Decree 
81/2008, which explicitly delineates the obligations and responsibilities of companies, employees, 
and experts engaged in safety management.
Furthermore, international technical standards were examined, specifically ISO 45001 for 
occupational health and safety management systems and ISO 19650 for information management 
in the construction industry. These delineate requirements and guidelines for implementing digital 
technologies in construction processes. The examination of scientific literature encompassed 
peer-reviewed studies that concentrated on specific applications of DT technology and ontologies 
in building site safety, emphasizing advantages, significant challenges, and potential areas for 
enhancement.
Ultimately, experiences and operational best practices were gathered via structured interviews 
with industry experts from private companies and public institutions. This facilitated the operational 
and concrete validation of the acquired information, which was subsequently organized into summary 
tables and illustrative diagrams.

Conceptual and Formal Modeling of Ontology
The ontological definition was established by a comprehensive semantic study of case studies 
and the utilization of sophisticated knowledge engineering methodologies, designed to guarantee 
formal consistency and conceptual depth. The approach commenced with extracting more than 200 
diverse instances, encompassing risk occurrences, sensor reports, operational processes, and threshold 
parameters, gathered from log datasets and construction site reports. These examples underwent vector 
representation utilizing domain-specific word embeddings (e.g., embeddings trained on technical 
safety engineering corpora) and were aggregated via hierarchical clustering to emphasize recurring 
conceptual patterns (Park & Shin, 2025).
The resultant taxonomy is structured into six primary macro-concepts: Risk, Sensor, 
PreventiveMeasure, OperatingProcedure, CriticalEvent, and HumanActor, each delineated in a 
hierarchical tree of subclasses extending to a third level of depth. The classes have been constructed 
in OWL 2 DL based on the SROIQ(D) description logic (the logical underpinning of OWL 2 DL), 
with precisely defined cardinality and disjointness constraints to maintain the integrity of the KB. 
Semantic properties encompass object properties for transactional relationships (e.g., hasSensor, 
generatesCriticalEvent, appliesProcedure) and data properties for quantitative attributes (e.g., 
measuredValue, probability, interventionPriority), supplemented by RDF annotations to facilitate 
automatic documentation through tools like Widoco.
SWRL rules have been integrated for behavior inference, primarily combining arithmetic and 
Boolean operators with threshold conditions and event categorization. An exemplary case pertains 
to the regulation that automatically triggers a CriticalEvent instance when a sensor exceeds the 
threshold value:
Sensor(?s) ^ measurementParameter (?s, ?p) ^ AssociatedRisk (?p, ?r) ^
thresholdApproval (?r, ?t) ^ Readingvalue (?s, ?v) ^
swrlb:greaterThan(?v, ?t)
statusAlert (?s, true)
The ontological encoding was executed in Protégé 5.5, with various plugins, including OntoGraf 
for dynamic visualization and SWRLTab for interactive rule administration. The complete artifact is 
version-controlled within a Git repository, supplemented by Unified Modeling Language diagrams 
produced as Scalable Vector Graphics and mapping tables (comma-separated values) that correlate 
classes, properties, and rules, facilitating future expansion and evolutionary maintenance.

In OWL 2 DL, domain and range are inferential rather than merely descriptive; every triple p (x, 
y) enables the inference of the types of x and y, hence allowing automatic classification. Cardinalities 
pertain to each subject instance and are articulated as min..max. The subsequent limitations are 
enforced by OWL restrictions and verified with SHACL shapes: 1..1 is obligatory and singular, 
0..1 is optional, 1..* denotes a minimum of 1, and 0..* indicates an unbounded quantity. Approval 
threshold and likelihood are risk attributes, whereas reading value pertains to sosa:Observation for 
semantic consistency; units of measure, when applicable, should be allocated to external vocabularies 
(e.g., quantities, units, dimensions, and types/OM). SWRL rules produce novel types or relations 
about existing individuals (e.g., ThresholdExceeded → CriticalEvent) and encapsulate “condition 
→ consequent” patterns. SHACL/SHACL-SPARQL, by enforcing structural commitments and 
cardinalities, is more adept at addressing absence or completeness needs that are not inherently 
managed under open-world assumptions. The requirements in Table 1 are computable and verifiable 
by integrating three layers: the OWL Terminological Box (T-Box) for semantics, a conformant A-Box 
for instance data, and SHACL checks within the pipeline. The reasoner guarantees consistency 
and deduced hierarchies, whereas SHACL ensures adherence to domains, ranges, and obligatory 
connections. This leads to operational accuracy.

RiskScore = P × S × E

OWL 2 DL (Versus OWL Rule Language/OWL Lite)
We adopted OWL 2 DL (SROIQ[D]) due to its capacity to accurately articulate the constraints 
characteristic of construction site safety—such as qualified cardinality, disjointness, and compound 
class expressions—which we utilized, for instance, to ensure that each Risk had a minimum of one 
PreventiveMeasure and was linked to at least one OperatingProcedure. This profile provided strong 
and thorough T-Box reasoning, enhancing the explainability of results by ensuring that outcomes 
were justifiable according to axioms and taxonomies.
Compared to alternatives, OWL Rule Language prioritizes forward-chaining materialization and 
ensures superior near real-time performance; however, this approach restricts specific constructors, 
reducing model fidelity. OWL Lite is excessively restrictive and failed to accommodate our patterns, 
particularly regarding qualified cardinalities and combinations of restrictions, rendering it unsuitable.
To achieve a balance between expressiveness and scalability, we employed a hybrid methodology: 
we retained the modeling core in OWL 2 DL while executing only the most common inferences in 
a rule-based manner; we assigned structural constraints (completeness and quality assessments) to 
SHACL and oversaw stream pre-aggregations at the periphery for temporal windows and thresholds. 
This approach enabled near real-time performance while maintaining completeness, auditability, 
and model quality. Condition-consequence rules (e.g., “threshold exceeded ⇒ CriticalEvent”) were 
operationally implemented in SWRL for enhanced expressiveness. In contrast, data compliance and 
quality assessments were conducted using SHACL within the observe–deduce–validate pipeline 
managed by the framework.
Preliminary Validation of Ontology
The initial validation was designed as a comprehensive testbed—encompassing logical, semantic, 
and performance aspects—to confirm that the ontology satisfied the criteria of formal accuracy, domain 
comprehensiveness, resilience to noise, and compatibility with diverse information ecosystems. The 
methodology was created to be replicable and quantifiable, with protocols and metrics established 
a priori and datasets versioned.
The validation objectives included: logical consistency of OWL 2 DL; accuracy of SWRL 
inferences in practical scenarios; compliance of instantiated data with structural constraints (shapes) 
independent of the T-Box; response times in the context of sensor streams; semantic interoperability 
with information containers and project information models. For this purpose, two principal datasets 
were utilized: synthetic data produced with regulated distributions comprising 120,000 triples and 
1.2 million temporal observations (DS-synth) and pseudonymized site logs comprising 48,000 triples 
and 310,000 observations (DS-field). The ground truth (gold standard) was generated by two HSE 
annotators, achieving an inter-annotator agreement of κ = 0.86, deemed “almost perfect.”
The ontology was subjected to consistency verification and class satisfiability assessment 
using the HermiT and Pellet reasoners; no unsatisfiable classes or disjointness violations were 
identified. We calculated structural quality indicators (class richness = 0.42; connection richness = 
0.61; inheritance richness = 2.8) and performed an automated pitfall check (no cycles in property 
hierarchies, no accidental punning, coherent inverse properties). Figure 2 encapsulates the formal 
validation workflow and quality assessments (Johansen et al., 2025).
To validate A-Box data conformity, SHACL shapes were established for the entities Sensor, 
CriticalEvent, OperatingProcedure, and PreventiveMeasure, encompassing limits on cardinalities, 
data kinds, patterns for personal protective equipment (PPE) codes, and obligatory requiresProcedure 
linkages for occurrences classified as fire. Violations were categorized as violation, warning, and info; 
in the DS field, the occurrence of violations was 2.7% (primarily due to absent process encodings), 
which decreased to 1.1% following sanitization.
A collection of 18 CQs facilitated the assessment of semantic coverage (e.g., “Which 
high-probability risks remain unmitigated by any measures?”; “Which critical events necessitate 
procedures involving the presence of a fire team within three minutes?”). Each CQ was converted into a

APPLICATION AND RESULTS
The prototype implementation adhered to an architecture aligned with the ideas detailed in the 
preceding chapter, converting the methodological modules into executable components and integration 
pathways throughout the site's diverse systems. The initial premise was that the DT functioned as a 
computational framework that integrated near-real-time observation streams with knowledge recorded 
in the ontology, maintaining semantic alignment with project information models and regulatory and 
operational documentation. Data was obtained via connectors to environmental and process sensors, 
wearables for employee monitoring, BMS/SCADA subsystems for equipment and machinery, and 
video-analytics channels for identifying potentially critical situations; each source was standardized to 
a standard profile that explicitly delineated the identity of the observed object, the spatial and temporal 
context, the unit of measurement, and the quality of the reading. Normalization was accompanied 
by creating findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable metadata and pseudonymizing personal 
data to provide technical traceability while safeguarding sensitive attributes.
The conversion of streams into the knowledge graph transpired through a semantic layer that 
implemented observations by the SSN/SOSA vocabulary and associated measurement locations 
with site topology and items in the information model through alignments to BRICK and ifcOWL. 
The generated triples were stored in an RDF triplestore that supported OWL 2 DL reasoning and 
SWRL rules, upon which a SHACL validation engine functioned to verify that instances comply 
with structural constraints, independent of the description logic. The processing pipeline executed 
an observe–deduce–validate cycle: the receipt of new sensor events updated the graph's state, rules 
initiated deductions regarding risk conditions or the activation of procedures, and shapes confirmed 
the completeness and compliance of mandatory links (for instance, the existence of a fire-response 
procedure for events classified as fire) (Wu et al., 2023). Upon a pertinent condition, an event bus 
alerts the application subsystems—from the HSE dashboard to mobile field applications—delivering 
a semantically enriched payload that encompasses the cause, evidence, and recommended actions, 
along with exact references to regulations, standard operating procedures, and responsibilities.
The connection with the DT extended beyond merely projecting data onto the geometric 
model; it encompassed the management of information life cycles in compliance with ISO 
19650. Information requirements were encoded as IDS profiles, and delivery packages were 
organized within ICDD containers, which maintained links between ontological instances, 
IFC objects, and supporting documents (plans, PPE records, certificates), ensuring the records' 
integrity and verifiability. This method facilitated regulated inter-organizational exchange and

retrospective audits of the decisions made by the system for specific events. From a security and 
compliance perspective, segregating graphs by domain (telemetry, master data, procedures) and 
implementing fine-grained access controls diminished the attack surface. It facilitated the application 
of personal data minimization regulations.
The design comprised four layers that collaboratively functioned through the observe–deduce–
validate cycle. The initial layer was the observation layer, standardizing data streams from IoT sensors 
and wearables, video analytics, BMS/SCADA gateways, and field adapters. Normalization employed 
the SSN/SOSA vocabulary while preserving the source identity, spatial and temporal references, unit 
of measure, and quality for each observation. This enabled immediate querying and comparison of 
signals. The harmonized streams contributed to the information layer, where building objects and 
spaces were depicted in ifcOWL/building topology ontology (BOT), systems and measurement points 
were articulated in BRICK, and regulatory and operational documentation was encapsulated in ICDD 
containers with IDS profiles serving as information contracts; at this level, computable traceability 
was established among assets, observations, and documents.
The knowledge layer constituted the most critical component of the system. It executed the 
DTSafe-O graph in OWL 2 DL and incorporated SWRL rules and SHACL restrictions. In the network, 
instantiated observations transformed into facts. The rule engine identified significant events or 
processes to implement. The SHACL validator assessed structural requirements and cardinalities. 
The RDF/SPARQL triplestore maintained the DT coherently, while the PROV-O ledger recorded 
the origins of choices and the supporting evidence (Kabzhan et al., 2025). This layer completed the 
inference process by incorporating formal verifications and comprehensive provenance.
The final layer pertained to operations and interactions. Dashboards, including elucidated alerts, 
presented decisions to HSE personnel, and coordinators. Supervisors and teams may utilize mobile 
applications and, when suitable, AR interfaces for checklists and standard operating procedures. 
APIs transmitted and received events for permit and incident management systems. The information 
technology (IT)/operations (Ops) channel oversaw the OntoOps release process and service-level 
monitoring. User comments, performance metrics, and logs were all integrated into the graph as 
operational information. This enhanced regulations and structures in subsequent generations. APIs 
linked all four layers of the architecture. They functioned as a contract among sensors and systems, 
information packages (IDS/ICDD), semantic services (SPARQL, SHACL validation), and user 
applications. An API gateway incorporating OpenID Connect/Open Authorization 2.0 authentication, 
versioning, and PROV-O auditing ensured separation, traceability, and security. An event bus (Message 
Queuing Telemetry Transport/Advanced Message Queuing Protocol/WebSocket) transmitted nearly 
real-time streams.

Dataset
The architecture was assessed using two complementary datasets, chosen to optimize experimental 
control while maintaining relevance to real-world contexts. The initial, synthetic model emulates 
typical site conditions with diverse sensor configurations and operational strategies, producing time 
series that align with field dynamics while incorporating controlled perturbations to assess the system's 
tolerance to noise and outliers. The second dataset, derived from actual locations, was appropriately 
pseudonymized and underwent a cleansing and normalization process that maintained informational 
richness while mitigating the variances introduced by disparate vendors and protocols. In both 
instances, HSE personnel and site managers collaboratively developed the ground truth, documenting 
events, procedures, and responsibilities by a schema aligned with the ontology and SHACL shapes, 
thus facilitating a transparent comparison between system inferences and operational practices.
The corpus comprised environmental measurements (gases, volatile organic compounds [VOC], 
particulates, temperature), physical signals (vibrations, accelerations), positional data (indoor/outdoor 
localization of workers and equipment), and machine status signals, with sampling frequencies 
varied based on the risk to be monitored and the available network capacity. Streams were anchored 
to an informational map of the site, corresponding to spaces and functional areas, aligned with the 
IFC model for asset identification and the risk taxonomy for prevention and mitigation; thus, each 
observation was positioned concurrently in physical space, operational processes, and the pertinent 
regulatory framework. The case annotation addressed trigger conditions including surpassing exposure 
thresholds, dangerous proximity between individuals and machinery, lack of necessary PPE in specific 
areas, existence of potentially explosive environments, and spatial conflicts with overhead lines in 
the vicinity of cranes; these conditions were converted into SWRL rules and SHACL constraints, 
facilitating the transformation of implicit SOP knowledge into repeatable computable specifications.

Validation
The validation was organized around four illustrative scenarios. In the initial potentially explosive 
atmosphere, VOC sensors and ventilation probes delivered per-second measurements; the SWRL 
rule integrated threshold exceedance, the space's classification zone membership, and ventilation 
inadequacy. In the second scenario (vehicle–pedestrian interference), Bluetooth low energy/
ultra-wideband localization and vehicle telemetry were integrated into a proximity graph with sliding 
windows, incorporating distance, speed limits, and SHACL forms for perimeter compliance. In the 
third scenario (crane–overhead line proximity), the ontology amalgamated geometric and temporal 
knowledge, employing topological relations within a three-dimensional context; in the fourth scenario 
(fire in a technical room), activation synthesized thermal gradients, smoke/temperature alarms, and 
actuator states, alongside the validation of SHACL constraints (event–procedure, PPE, escape routes) 
and prioritization based on classification and occupancy (Shi et al., 2025). The timeline elucidated the 
progression from raw data to decision-making: it revealed the negligible latency between threshold
