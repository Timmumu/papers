ABSTRACT
Traditional approaches to architectural heritage conservation often prioritize emergency main -
tenance, which lacks the capability to monitor and adapt to the dynamic, ongoing changes in 
structural integrity, thereby compromising long-term conservation objectives. This study in -
troduces a digital twin five-dimensional model framework specifically tailored for architectural 
heritage conservation, designed to facilitate a shift from reactive to proactive conservation 
strategies. Incorporating dimensions of physical entity, digital twin, twin data, service, and 
connection relationships, this framework enables real-time situational awareness and hyper-real- 
time virtual extrapolation. Through the reconstruction of the historical building information 
model and the development of both a structural linkage rule model and a displacement trend 
model based on mechanical properties, the framework offers a comprehensive digital represen -
tation from geometric, physical, behavioral, and rule-based perspectives. Validated through a 
prototype system at the Yingxian Wooden Pagoda in China, a site susceptible to natural and 
anthropogenic hazards, the findings demonstrate that the digital twin effectively predicts and 
adapts to spatio-temporal dynamic changes in the pagoda ’ s structure, enhancing resilience and 
promoting sustainable heritage conservation. This research supports disaster risk reduction 
strategies, contributing to vulnerability analysis and resilience in heritage conservation amid 
increasing threats from climate change and other emergent risks.

periods, embodying the cultural implications of social structures, religious beliefs, and lifestyles [ 1 ]. Nevertheless, due to variables 
such as material degradation and human intervention, some architectural historic sites are already compromised [ 2 , 3 ]. They urgently 
require appropriate conservation and maintenance strategies. Presently, most conservation strategies rely on emergency repair, which

conservation efforts from passive salvage restoration to proactive preventive maintenance and intervening prior to the onset of threats

Digital twin technology [ 9 ] offers a novel approach to precisely understanding architectural history by creating a virtual model that 
represents an entity ’ s complete life cycle state using data and model-driven simulation, monitoring, prediction, and optimization [ 10 ,
11 ]. It can assess the condition of historical structures in real-time using multi-source heterogeneous data and execute hyper-real-time 
extrapolation, thereby enhancing building health management and minimizing human intervention [ 12 ].
The digital twin concept was initially introduced by Michael Grieves in 2003, characterized as a three-dimensional representation 
of entities, voxels, and their informational interconnections [ 13 ]. Subsequently, Negri et al. expanded this concept, highlighting the 
real-time connection between physical and virtual systems via data synchronization [ 14 ]. As research advances, the three-dimensional 
model is increasingly inadequate to fulfil complicated requirements. Tao Fei and colleagues developed a five-dimensional architecture 
encompassing physical entities, virtual entities, twin services, twin data, and connectivity linkages [ 15 ]. This design has significantly 
advanced the utilization of digital twin technologies across various study domains.
Although digital twin technology has shown unique advantages in many fields, its application in architectural heritage conser -
vation is still in the exploratory stage, especially in preventive conservation, and the theoretical construction and empirical analysis 
have not been systematized yet. Vuoto et al. explored its potential in conserving the structural integrity of architectural heritage but 
lacked the verification of its practical application [ 16 ].Tan et al. combined laser scanning with tilt photogrammetry tools for archi -
tectural archaeology, but this approach is limited to specific heritage types [ 17 ]. There is no universal method for building digital twins 
of architectural heritage.
Digital twins, as virtual entities, are digital representations of physical entities for understanding, predicting, optimizing and 
controlling natural objects. The universality of its construction method directly affects the application effect of the digital twin so -
lution. In the face of complex building structures and variable environments, how to construct pervasive digital twins to realize real- 
time monitoring and early warning is a vital issue in current research [ 18 ]. Bordeleau et al. explored various model-driven engineering 
techniques used in the model building process, identified as potentially overcoming the challenges associated with digital twin 
modeling [ 19 ]. Concurrently, Rasheed et al. provide a comprehensive overview of the methods and techniques currently applied in 
constructing digital twin models [ 20 ]. They discuss the challenges encountered during the construction process in detail, offering 
specific recommendations and insights from the authors. Funari et al. proposed a parametric Scan-to-FEM framework and validated the 
application of this method in historical masonry structures through the S ˜ao Tocato Church in Portugal. However, the applicability to 
complex or irregular structures is limited [ 21 ]. Jouan et al. proposed a digital twin construction method based on the Historical 
Building Information Model (HBIM) to integrate multidimensional data to monitor changes and propose solutions [ 22 ]. The model 
(HBIM) digital twin construction method integrates multidimensional data to monitor changes and propose solutions. HBIM can 
accurately characterize complex geometrical structures and physical attributes of architectural heritage by integrating time di -
mensions and multilevel information [ 23 ]. However, most current HBIM applications only serve as static models, failing to capture the 
dynamic processes of architectural change over time and multifactors and achieving proper preventive conservation. Colucci et al. 
investigated the interoperability of HBIM with 3D GIS, but their aim was limited to providing more comprehensive data documen -
tation for heritage conservation [ 24 ].
This work aims to thoroughly investigate the methods for generating digital twins of architectural heritage utilizing the HBIM 
model, focusing on the four essential characteristics of "geometry-physics-behavior-rule." This will encompass the intricacies of 
architectural heritage in all dimensions, including structural modifications, physical conditions, behavioral responses, and adherence 
to regulations. Consequently, genuine preventive protection can be achieved, offering a novel perspective for architectural asset health 
monitoring and life cycle forecasting.

2.2. Digital twin modeling
Architectural heritage digital twin construction is the digital modeling of physical entities ’ properties, methods, behaviors, and 
other characteristics in a digital space to simulate and predict their state and behavior. In order to ensure that the twin is highly 
consistent with the physical entity, it needs to be modeled in a virtual environment from the fusion of four dimensions: geometry, 
physics, behavior and rules, as shown in Fig. 2 . 
(1) Geometric Modeling
Geometric modeling ( M
G
) is a digital representation of architectural heritage, detailing the geometric attributes of elements such as 
columns, beams, and sapphires. These define the heritage ’ s intricate internal and external structures and constitute the initial phase in 
creating a digital twin. Point cloud modeling and building information modeling are commonly utilized. Point cloud modeling uses 
laser scanning and other techniques to gather dense point cloud data, thereby capturing the intricate aspects of the item. The volume of 
data remains substantial and necessitates high-performance computing resources. This work employs a hybrid point cloud approach 
and building information modeling to ensure data consistency to reconstruct physical things ’ geometric form and non-graphical data, 
thereby establishing a foundation for future model analysis and interactive functions. 
(2) Physical Modeling
Physical modeling ( M
P
) is the addition of physical properties and constraints to a geometric model to simulate the dynamic 
response of built heritage under natural environments (e.g., wind, rain, earthquakes, etc.) or anthropogenic forces, such as stresses and 
deformations. Mathematical approximations are usually made using finite element modeling techniques to construct models reflecting 
the physical phenomena of built heritage at macro and micro scales. Training and optimizing the models with real-time data streams 
can achieve a high-level mapping of the built heritage ’ s physical entities. 
(3) Behavioral model
The behavioral model ( M
B
) mainly describes the real-time behaviors of physical entities under the coupling of the external 
environment and internal causes in different time and space scales, such as evolutionary behaviors, performance degradation be -

3. Related work
3.1. HBIM-based geophysical modeling
Historical Building Information Modeling (HBIM) is a specialized modeling technique designed for architectural heritage, adept at 
accurately representing the intricate geometry of such structures while encompassing multidimensional data, including physical 
properties and material characteristics. This study integrates a conventional parametric model and an irregular triangular mesh model 
to precisely represent the geometric distinctiveness of built heritage in HBIM modeling. This study proposes a three-step digital-model 
decoupling, geometric conversion, and attribute mapping process to enhance the weak data interoperability between HBIM and the 
established digital twin framework integration scheme, ensuring that the HBIM model can be rendered and interacted with within the 
digital twin framework. The procedure is illustrated in Fig. 3 .
Fine modeling is a core aspect of architectural heritage digitization. In order to balance high-quality digital representation and 
efficiency, this study blends two approaches: standard parametric modeling and participatory regular triangular mesh modeling. Fine 
hybrid modeling integrates a specific component ’ s irregular triangular mesh model into the standard parametric model framework. In 
order to avoid the problem of missing data caused by cutting point clouds during irregular triangular mesh modeling, the irregular 
triangular mesh model is finely tuned using the standard model parameters. The irregular triangular mesh model is precisely posi -
tioned and moved to the predetermined position of the standard parametric model to realize the accurate spatial docking of the two. 
The exemplary hybrid model of the Yingxian wooden tower is shown in Fig. 4 . After completing the model fusion, attribute input and 
Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) type categorization are performed, and the model is exported in IFC format.
Due to their intricate architecture and attribute data, IFCs are inappropriate for direct rendering and cannot be used for graphical 
representation in a 3D format. Consequently, IFC files must be transformed into a compatible format. Gl Transmission Format (glTF) 
encompasses all elements necessary to create a 3D scene. glTF serves as a target format for converting IFC model files to finalize the 
building of geometric models within the defined digital twin architecture.Nonetheless, the straight conversion from IFC to glTF format 
is more intricate because of existing technical constraints. The OBJ format is a prevalent standard for 3D model files, encompassing 
vertex coordinates, usual coordinates, and texture coordinates. This study selects OBJ as an intermediary to develop the IFC-OBJ-glTF 
conversion framework to create glTF model files. The essence lies in processing the relative coordinate system of HBIM during the 
conversion of IFC-OBJ to the global coordinate system of WebGL, aiming to accurately map the spatial relationships of geometric 
information. The coordinate conversion procedure encompasses several essential steps: Initially, the local point coordinates of the 
components must be extracted from the IFC file; subsequently, these local coordinates are integrated with the global coordinates of the 
overall model by iteratively applying the matrix transformation layer by layer in the following equation to derive the absolute co -
ordinates of the vertices ultimately. The coordinate transformation is presented in Equation (2) .

3.2. Modeling of structural linkage rules taking into account mechanical properties
In digital twin modeling, a rule model delineates the regulations governing the interaction and data synchronization between 
physical entities and their digital counterparts. It encompasses mapping data structures and properties, as well as regulations for 
behavior, operations, and responses. In structural linkage rule modeling that considers mechanical properties, the goal is to ensure that 
the digital twin system not only reflects the geo metric information of the structure but also accurately simulates the structure ’ s 
response under various mechanical loads and environmental conditions.
Following geometrical-physical modeling to precisely represent the structural components, Finite Element Analysis (FEA) is 
employed to develop a mathematical model of the structure, account for the linkage effects, and examine the forces among the 
components. The geometric model is partitioned into finite element meshes. Finite element simulations are initially conducted to 
examine the mechanical behavior by assessing the stresses, strains, and displacements of the parts under diverse loading conditions. 
Subsequently, structural linkage analysis is conducted to examine the interactions among the different components of the structure and 
to assess the impact of the linkage effect on the overall structural response.
Mechanical properties denote the characteristics and behavior of structural materials and components under external forces. These 
qualities are crucial when evaluating and designing structures, as they directly influence the structure ’ s stability, durability, and 
safety. Specify the material parameters, including the modulus of elasticity and Poisson ’ s ratio, for all structural components. This 
study focuses on a timber tower ’ s single-story, outwardly fluted column structure as the primary subject. The outer slotted column 
network is a type of larch; the wood exhibits orthogonal anisotropy; the columns endure vertical loads, primarily from longitudinal 
grain compression; appendages and other horizontal connecting elements experience mainly transverse grain compression, with their 
material properties referenced in Table 1 . The modulus of elasticity encompasses all material qualities, including the modulus of 
elasticity and Poisson ’ s ratio. The modulus of elasticity is defined within the elastic range as the ratio of tensile or compressive stress to 
strain per unit length of the material. Conversely, Poisson ’ s ratio quantifies the extent to which a material contracts in the perpen -
dicular direction when subjected to tensile stress in a single direction. It is the negative of the ratio of transverse strain to longitudinal 
strain.

3.3. Behavioral model construction based on long and short-term memory networks
The behavioral characteristics of architectural heritage are complex and diverse, particularly in how structural components 
respond to external changes. Given the central role of wooden columns in maintaining the structural stability of such heritage, this 
study focuses on the behavioral analysis of wooden columns, exploring effective methods to capture and simulate the dynamic 
displacement changes of these critical structural elements over time. Displacement serves as the most direct, quantifiable, and easily 
monitorable external indicator of structural performance and potential damage. It intuitively reflects changes in structural loading 
conditions, joint relaxation, component damage, or foundation settlement, making it a crucial early-warning metric for assessing both 
the immediate health status and long-term evolutionary trends of a structure. The rule-based model transforms the complex physical 
mechanisms of force transmission between components into a displacement-driven modeling framework. This transformation ensures 
strong consistency among the model ’ s inputs, internal states, and outputs in terms of physical meaning. Therefore, selecting defor -
mation displacement as the prediction target enhances the model ’ s interpretability and intuitiveness.
When selecting a model foundation, although traditional predictive models such as linear regression, grey prediction, Markov 
prediction, traditional time series analysis, and neural networks are widely applied across multiple fields, not all models are suitable 
for the research subject of this paper. The linear regression model assumes a linear relationship between the dependent variable and 
the independent variables, and that the independent variables are linearly independent, thus unable to handle nonlinear relationships; 
Grey prediction is primarily used for handling incomplete information in sequence data and performs poorly in predicting nonlinear 
data; Markov prediction is suitable for predicting the probability of state transitions but has limited direct predictive capability for non- 
stationary or continuous data; most traditional time series analysis methods, such as ARIMA and VAR, are based on linear assumptions 
and have limited ability to capture nonlinear dynamics. In deep learning models, the Backpropagation (BP) neural network, as a 
representative of traditional neural networks, does not allow neurons within the network layers to exchange information, which limits 
the network ’ s understanding of the semantic relationships between sequential data. In contrast, traditional recurrent neural networks 
(RNNs) add inter-layer node connections to BP neural networks, allowing information to be transmitted within sequences and 
combining historical information with the current moment. However, due to the gradient multiplication in RNNs, they face issues of 
gradient vanishing or explosion when handling long sequence data, limiting their predictive period. To address these issues, Long 
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks introduce an internal state structure within units to preserve the current state and 
propagate it to the next time step, while selectively controlling the flow of information through gating mechanisms. These features 
enable LSTMs to excel in handling complex time-series data requiring the capture of long-term dependencies. Additionally, LSTMs 
support both univariate and multivariate predictions, making them adaptable to any scenario. Therefore, the LSTM model is partic -
ularly suitable for addressing the long-term sequence prediction problem of displacement in the column heads of ancient architectural 
wooden columns in this study. Furthermore, establishing a digital twin of ancient architecture requires a comprehensive under -
standing and prediction of the behavior of wooden columns, including interactions between different directions of load. Thus, this 
study constructs a multivariate-to-multivariate network based on the LSTM model.
3.3.1. Data cleaning
The data must meet the equal interval criterion for time series modeling and analysis. Consequently, it is essential to extract the 
requisite data from the raw data at uniform intervals to create a dataset. The chosen dataset originates from displacement monitoring

data collected between July 1, 2023, and October 12, 2023, at the Yingxian Muta in China. This data encompasses displacements in the 
longitudinal, transverse, and vertical axes (measured in millimeters). The monitoring robot is equipped with high-precision mea -
surement capabilities, offering an accuracy of 0.5". It operates reliably in outdoor environments, ensuring both the accuracy and 
consistency of data collection. Measurements are taken every 4 h, resulting in a time series dataset with 624 equally spaced obser -
vations. The data collection frequency is determined based on multiple factors, including the rate of structural changes and the robot ’ s 
power consumption. Displacement and deformation in wooden structures occur gradually over time and are influenced by environ -
mental conditions. Therefore, the chosen frequency effectively captures structural changes without causing unnecessary resource 
consumption.
The collected data is wirelessly transmitted in real time to the monitoring cloud platform for storage and subsequent processing. 
This wireless transmission approach effectively reduces latency, ensuring the continuity and timeliness of the data stream. However, 
network signals may be affected by environmental interference, transmission distance, or weather conditions, which can lead to packet 
loss or transmission interruptions, resulting in missing values within the time series data. To maintain data quality and accuracy, 
appropriate data imputation methods must be employed to fill these gaps. The prevalent techniques for addressing missing values are 
classified into three categories: direct rounding, imputation with predetermined values (such as sample mean, median, or mode), and

3.3.2. Model construction
Although a single LSTM unit can effectively address the gradient vanishing and explosion problems inherent in traditional RNN 
models, its predictive accuracy remains limited by the complexity of the network architecture. Increasing the stacking depth of LSTM 
layers can significantly enhance the model ’ s capability to process long-sequence data. In this study, a three-layer stacked LSTM ar -
chitecture is employed, designed based on the core principle of progressively increasing feature representation complexity in deep 
neural networks. Following careful evaluation, three LSTM layers were selected, consisting of 100, 200, and 300 neurons, respectively. 
The initial layer captures fundamental temporal patterns from the original input through finite-capacity focusing. Its 100-unit 
configuration ensures the fidelity of local feature extraction while minimizing the risk of overfitting due to redundant parameters 
in the lower layers. The intermediate layer serves as an integration hub for cross-period dependencies, with an expanded capacity of

200 units, which significantly enhances the network ’ s nonlinear representation capabilities. This increment was systematically 
determined through hyperparameter tuning as the optimal balance between gradient stability and model expressiveness. The top layer, 
with 300 units, represents the engineering-optimal configuration, derived from the convergence of three key constraints: task 
complexity, data scale, and computational resources. Specifically, the maximum time dependency length required for the modeling 
task demands a theoretical minimum of 250 units; the upper limit of model capacity, as supported by the training sample size, is 
approximately 346 units; and the maximum feasible dimension under hardware memory constraints is 320 units. The intersection of 
these constraints defines a feasible solution space centered around 300 units. The entire network and internal structure are shown in

To meet the training requirements of stacked LSTM models, this study employs the Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) algorithm 
as the optimization method. Adam integrates the benefits of momentum-based methods and adaptive learning rate adjustment, 
enabling the model to effectively capture the high-dimensional nonlinear characteristics of architectural heritage displacement data. 
This facilitates efficient convergence on complex loss surfaces while helping to avoid entrapment in local minima.
3.3.3. Hyperparameter settings
To optimize the performance of the LSTM deep learning model for displacement prediction tasks, this study carefully controlled and 
selected several key hyperparameters, including the learning rate, look_back (the time step length for sequence prediction), batch_size 
(the batch size), and epoch (the number of training iterations). Among the three displacement directions — longitudinal, lateral, and 
vertical — vertical displacement is generally easier to measure and exhibits higher measurement accuracy. Therefore, the Mean Ab -
solute Error (MAE) of vertical displacement in the prediction results is used as the primary evaluation metric for assessing the 
effectiveness of different hyperparameter configurations. 
(1) Learning rate
Although the Adam adaptive optimization algorithm can automatically adjust the learning rate during training, the choice of the 
initial learning rate still significantly affects the model ’ s training dynamics and final performance. To evaluate this impact, other 
parameters were held constant while different initial learning rates were tested. As shown in Fig. 8 , the model ’ s MAE gradually de -
creases as the initial learning rate increases up to 0.001, indicating an improvement in prediction accuracy. However, once the learning 
rate exceeds 0.001, the MAE begins to rise, suggesting a decline in performance. Based on this observation, the model achieves its 
highest accuracy when the initial learning rate is set to 0.001. Therefore, this value is selected as the optimal initial learning rate for 
subsequent experiments. 
(2) Look back
The choice of time step length (look_back) directly influences the model ’ s ability to capture long-term dependencies and patterns in 
time series data, thereby affecting the accuracy and reliability of predictions. By fixing other parameters, a comparative analysis was 
conducted using different time step lengths. Fig. 9 illustrates how the model ’ s MAE varies with look_back values ranging from 1 to 10. 
As shown in the figure, the MAE fluctuates across different time step lengths. Notably, when the look_back is set to 3 and 5, the model 
achieves lower MAE values, indicating higher prediction accuracy. In contrast, higher MAE values at look_back values of 2 and 9 
suggest reduced model performance. These fluctuations may be attributed to the model ’ s varying ability to capture temporal de -
pendencies at different time window lengths. Based on this trend analysis, a look-back of 5 is identified as the optimal choice for 
predicting the displacement time series at monitoring point No. 223.

(3) Batch size
The batch size determines the amount of data the model processes in each training iteration, influencing both learning efficiency 
and memory usage. With all other parameters held constant, different batch sizes — ranging from 1 to 512 in powers of two — were 
tested to observe their effect on model performance. As shown in Fig. 10 , the model ’ s MAE initially decreases and then increases as the 
batch size grows. Specifically, when the batch size is small (between 1 and 16), the MAE remains low, indicating strong model per -
formance. However, as the batch size increases beyond 32, the MAE begins to rise gradually, suggesting a decline in prediction ac -
curacy. It is worth noting that smaller batch sizes, while improving performance, can significantly increase training time. Therefore, to 
strike a balance between training efficiency and model accuracy, a batch size of 8 or 16 is recommended, with further adjustments 
made based on the specific task requirements.

Each training epoch consists of one or more forward and backward propagation processes, allowing the model to learn from the 
training data and adjust its weights. By fixing other parameters and varying only the epoch size, Fig. 11 shows how the Mean Absolute

number of training epochs increases to 200, the model reaches its lowest MAE, suggesting that it may have approached an optimal 
state. After 200 iterations, the MAE fluctuates slightly but remains stable overall. At specific points, it increases slightly (e.g., the MAE 
at 350 and 500 iterations is higher than at 200 iterations), which may be due to the model beginning to overfit the training data or the 
training process reaching a stable state, where further training does not result in significant performance improvements. Based on the 
above analysis, selecting 200 training iterations is optimal, as the model achieves a relatively low MAE value at this point.

4. Results
4.1. System setup and operation
This research creates a prototype system of digital twins utilizing real-time observation of architectural heritage. The efficacy of the 
suggested five-dimensional architectural heritage digital twins model is validated through the case study of the Yingxian Wooden 
Pagoda in China. The system uses B/S architecture, wherein the front-end and back-end are distinct, and a defined data interface 
protocol facilitates efficient data transmission.
Real-time displacement monitoring data of architectural heritage components, gathered by an automated monitoring robot, is kept 
in a dynamic database to ensure the immediacy and correctness of the information. A displacement prediction model based on LSTM 
regularly assesses these time-series data. When users request monitoring and prediction data for a specific component via a web page, 
the system transmits requests that include the component ’ s unique identifier (ID) to the server. The server addresses these requests by 
retrieving the real-time monitoring data and projected displacement trend data from the database and transmitting them to the front 
end using an API. The front end utilizes ECharts to render a line graph of this data, facilitating immediate and intuitive data visual -
ization, as illustrated in Fig. 12 .

5. Conclusion and discussion
This study focuses on architectural legacy as the subject of research and proposes the building of a five-dimensional digital twin 
model. The objective is to create a highly detailed digital replica that can accurately replicate and analyze the intricate features of 
architectural heritage. Furthermore, the practicality and applicability of the architectural heritage digital twin 5D model framework 
and the architectural heritage digital twin prototype are confirmed through the use of the Yingxian Wooden Pagoda as a case study.

The following findings are derived. 
(1) Constructing geometric and physical models of wooden tower buildings based on historical building information modelling
By integrating standard parametric models with irregular triangular mesh models, a detailed hybrid HBIM model of wooden tower 
buildings has been developed. The proposed three-step strategy — numerical model decoupling, geometric transformation, and attri -
bute mapping — effectively ensures that the HBIM model can be seamlessly rendered and interacted with within a digital twin 
framework. 
(2) Modeling of structural linkage rules taking into account mechanical properties
Finite element analysis was employed to examine and simulate the interactions among the components of the built heritage, 
informed by the mechanical properties of these components. This approach accurately represented its structural dynamics, utilizing 
exponential fitting models with a fit degree of 94 % and polynomial fitting models with a fit degree of 97 %. The findings indicate that 
employing a data-driven model correction methodology rectifies errors stemming from initial model simplification and parameter 
uncertainty. 
(3) Construction of a prediction model for the displacement behavior of wood-frame tower buildings based on long and short-term 
memory networks
A displacement behaviour prediction model for the wooden column head of a timber tower is developed using an extended short-

Based on research into the five-dimensional digital twin model, this study also has certain limitations, primarily manifested in the 
following two aspects: Although the LSTM network model demonstrates excellent predictive accuracy, careful analysis reveals that the 
LSTM model ’ s predictive results exhibit significant lag. Upon further analysis, the fundamental causes of this issue may stem from two 
aspects: first, during training, the LSTM model optimizes by minimizing the error between the actual values and predicted values in the 
objective function. However, the model sometimes tends to select the closest previous observation value as the current prediction 
value, leading to prediction delays. Second, the feature extraction method based on a sliding time window strategy has an inherent lag, 
making it unable to respond promptly to trend changes in the data sequence. To address this issue, future research will focus on 
exploring alternative methods to mitigate prediction lag.

This study begins with the displacement prediction of ancient building structures, achieving an early assessment of structural 
health. However, we also recognize that displacement prediction itself can be achieved without relying on digital twins. The true 
advantage of digital twins lies in their ability to comprehensively and dynamically simulate and evaluate the structural response under 
various scenarios in a virtual environment. In future research, we will build upon the predictive results of this study to expand the 
functionality of the digital twin model, enabling virtual simulation and comparative analysis of repair and reinforcement strategies for 
ancient buildings. By simulating different repair strategies, construction techniques, and material selections within the digital twin 
platform, it will be possible to predict the impact of each approach on structural performance without directly intervening in fragile 
heritage. This will optimize the decision-making process and reduce the risks associated with on-site trials. This extension will fully 
leverage the potential of digital twins in the protection of ancient buildings, providing comprehensive support from monitoring and 
prediction to repair strategy evaluation, further enhancing the scientific and forward-looking nature of heritage preservation.
