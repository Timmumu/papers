ABSTRACT 
Accurate assessments of over-and-under-excavation volumes during tunnel construction are critical for stability 
and structural safety. Over-and-under-excavation assessment is typically time-consuming as it is undertaken 
manually and is prone to inaccuracies that adversely impact quality, safety, and costs. To overcome this problem, 
a status digital twin (to monitor physical conditions) is developed to assess over-and-under excavation during 
tunneling, enabling a robust and automated assessment of volumes of earth to be determined. The development 
of the status digital twin comprises three stages: (1) creating an as-designed building information model and as- 
built point cloud model; (2) producing an automated status update with newly added data; and (3) calculating 
the of over-and-under excavation volume by building a Triangulated Irregular Network (TIN). The status digital 
twin is tested in a large-scale tunnel project to evaluate its feasibility and effectiveness. The average over-and- 
under excavation thickness is 0.05 m, and the mean error of volume calculation is 4.54 %. The results 
demonstrate that the status digital twin can accurately assess over-and-under excavation during the construction 
of large-scale tunnel projects by providing instant feedback concerning over/under excavation, thus enabling 
quality to be effectively managed and costs to be controlled. 
1. Introduction 
Drilling and blasting are popular excavation methods in tunnel 
construction [56] . When drilling and blasting are used, over-and-under 
excavation (also called overcut and undercut) is likely to occur more 
often than using other construction methods, such as Tunnel Boring 
Machines (TBMs) [25] . Over-excavation is defined as an excessively 
blasted rock area beyond the tunnel ’ s designed contour line. Oppositely, 
under-excavation is defined as the remaining rock area within the 
designed contour line in the tunnel. Over-and-under excavation can 
negatively impact the peripheral rock mass around an excavation area, 
affecting a tunnel ’ s stability and structural safety and increasing con -
struction costs [22,57] . Monitoring excavation quality is a manually 
laden process and, as a result, is time-consuming, with estimates of 
excavated material often inaccurate, adversely impacting project costs 
[2,22] . 
While several rule-based or artificial intelligence (AI) methods have 
been developed to assess over-and-under excavation accurately, it re -
mains a pervasive problem during tunnel construction 
[2,12,36,13,35,22,43] . With this in mind, this research addresses the 
following question: How can over-and-under excavation be accurately 
assessed during the construction of large-scale tunnel projects ? This research 
proposes that a status digital twin is used for basic condition monitoring 
applications to assess and manage the excavation process. Creating 
operating parameters with visualization tools is core to developing a 
status digital twin. Thus, this research utilizes a non-invasive three- 
dimensional (3D) point cloud-based method to accurately and auto -
matically assess a tunnel ’ s over-and-under excavation during 
construction. 
Thus, the paper commences with a brief review of the three- 
dimensional (3D) point cloud and semantic segmentation application 
in tunnel construction ( Section 2 ). Details about the proposed status

2. Background to the research 
Digital twins can take various forms depending on their function 
[31,41]. As mentioned above, the development of a status digital twin 
forms the focus of this paper. However, a cursory glance at the literature 
reveals two other types of digital receiving attention: (1) Operation twins, 
which are used to adjust operating parameters based on linked actions 
and/or workflows; and (2) Simulation twins, which are used to predict 
how an objective or device responds to operational conditions in the 
future – this is key for predictive maintenance and product development. 
With advances in AI and sensing technologies coming to the fore, 
digital twinning has become a popular research topic, as evident by the 
sheer number of research papers dealing with this subject matter (e.g., 
[7,24,8,37,9,27,42]. Undertaking a detailed review of digital twin 
literature is outside the scope of this paper as it focuses on developing a 
new approach for assessing over-and-under excavation during tunnel 
construction. However, within the context of tunnel construction, 
readers are advised to refer to the works of Li et al. [28] and Li et al. 
[29]. 
2.1. Three-dimensional (3D) point cloud for tunnel construction 
Due to the accuracy and efficiency of 3D point clouds, they have been 
widely used in construction for numerous applications, such as progress 
monitoring, building performance examination, safety inspection, and 
retrospective building information models (BIM) from ‘as-builts’ docu -
mentation[32,38,49,54]. Several studies have also applied point clouds 
in tunnel construction for cross-section extraction[3,10], deformation 
measurement[18,48], and reconstruction[11,55]. However, using point 
clouds to assess over-and-under excavation in tunnel projects has been 
limited to the work of Huang et al. [22], who employed image-matching 
and cubic parametric interpolation spline methods to visualize and 
calculate the volume of over-and-under excavation. 
While Huang et al.’s [22] work enabled significant headway to be 
made with the calculation of over-and-under excavation, improvements 
are required to their method to further improve its accuracy by: (1) 
automatically segmenting part of a tunnel from a point cloud; and (2) 
the process to determine geometric calculations. Point cloud semantic 
segmentation is the task of clustering parts of points that belong to the 
same object class. Studies examining point cloud semantic segmentation 
can be categorized as: (1) model-driven approaches; and (2) data-driven 
approaches. 
2.2. Point cloud semantic segmentation 
Model-driven approaches classify point clouds into different seg -
ments based on predefined labels (e.g., descriptions of objects of inter -
est). In the context of point cloud semantic segmentation, a plethora of 
model-driven approaches were developed. For example, the model 
fitting-based approaches aim to fit shapes onto point cloud data to detect 
objects’ geometric primitives. Two of the most widely used model 
fitting-based approaches are the Hough Transform (HT)[5]and Random 
Sample Consensus (RCS)[16]. Still, they require prior knowledge of 
predefined shapes or surfaces[14]. However, the RCS has been proven to 
perform better in terms of efficiency and robustness than the HT[1,15]. 
Data-driven methods predict objects using training data[44,51,52]. 
For example, the feature clustering-based method aggregates points 
with similar attributes into clusters[6,58]. Implementing this method 
initially requires extracting predefined features from the points (e.g., 
normal surface vector, height difference, and saliency). Although 
feature clustering-based methods are proven to perform better than 
region-growing methods[1,45], they also suffer from noise or outliers, 
and designing a practical feature is a critical factor for their successful 
implementation[15]. 
With significant advancements in AI, deep learning-based data- 
driven approaches have been widely used for segmenting point clouds 
[17,50]. Compared with the approaches mentioned above, a deep 
learning algorithm automatically learns predictive features from many 
labeled samples, avoiding the need for expert-defined features 
[23,26,40]. However, semantic segmentation of point clouds remains 
challenging due to their irregular format and varying point densities 
[33]. 
2.3. Volume calculation with triangulated irregular network 
The TIN has been widely used as a geometric processing tool in 
various manufacturing and engineering fields. It is a versatile tool rep -
resenting the spatial distribution of physical elements measured in 
discrete locations[46]. The TIN differs from point clouds by offering a 
continuous surface to represent geometric data, which allows different 
interpolation techniques to be applied for finer value extraction or 
computation. For example, the vertices of a TIN can be used to calculate 
the slope and aspect[21], gravity-specific curvature, and other land 
surface parameters[34]. 
The TIN has been combined with various sensing techniques for 
volume measurement. For example, large TINs are constructed to assess 
the volume of earthwork[19]) and lake water from remote-sensing data, 
and a smaller range of TINs can also be used to measure the crown 
projection area and volume of trees[30]. Combined with dedicated al -
gorithms, TINs can be generated from different data sources and serve as 
a basis for more advanced geometric calculation tasks. Using TINs for 
tunnel excavation volume calculation has received limited attention. To 
overcome the challenges as mentioned earlier associated with auto -
mated point cloud segmentation and excavation volume calculation, 
two issues require consideration: (1) new end-to-end deep learning al -
gorithms need to be developed to directly extract and learn point cloud 
features and produce segmented results without data representation 
transformation[39]; and (2) the use of the TIN-based method to calcu -
late the volume of over-and-under excavation. 
3. Status digital twin of excavation 
To recap, the aim is to develop an approach to evaluate over-and- 
under excavation in large-scale tunnel projects accurately. The pro -
posed approach consists of three significant steps: (1) initialization of 
the status digital twin consisting of an as-designed BIM; (2) automated 
periodic status update with newly added data; and (3) the calculation of 
over-and-under excavation volume using a TIN-based algorithm. The 
framework of the status digital twin is presented in Fig. 1. Notably, the 
digital twin is continuously updated with automated steps each time a 
new scan is registered. 
3.1. Initialization with as-designed building information model 
Autodesk Revit® and Dynamo generated the as-designed building 
information model (BIM) based on computer-aided design (CAD) 
drawings. Dynamo is a piece of visual programming software that runs 
on the Revit® platform for parametric design. Based on the CAD files, 
Dynamo reads the parameters in the drawings, generates surfaces after 
connecting control points, and finally fuses to form a 3D tunnel model. 
The workflow of the ‘as-designed’ BIM is presented in Fig. 2.

1. Compression of Tunnel Face Point Cloud : Fine-grained point cloud data 
generated by laser scanners is usually too heavy to be processed 
directly. An octree-based approach was adopted [20] to compress the 
point cloud to preserve key point cloud information and simulta -

2. Point Cloud Filter : After raw point cloud data compression based on 
the normal vector, the Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) 
method was adopted in combination with space decomposition to 
simplify the input point cloud further while keeping minimum shape 
loss. The space is partitioned into voxels, each containing a subset of 
the entire point cloud. Moreover, the RANSAC can help remove 
outliers from the target surface. The side length of the voxel and the 
minimum number of points inside a voxel are key parameters for 
space decomposition, and the former is proportional to the point 
cloud data simplification rate 
3. Automated Segmentation of Tunnel Face from Point Cloud : Inside a

with homogenous texture, making it difficult to separate by generic 
point cloud segmentation methods. To avoid manual corrections 
after heuristic segmentation methods, an encoder-decoder network 
architecture adapted from the PointNet ++ [40] was adopted to 
achieve accurate point cloud segmentation, taking advantage of the 
shape before the tunnel cross-sections. This state-of-the-art point 
cloud segmentation network can extract features with different 
granularities using a single network. The network employs a

3.3. Volume estimation with TIN 
The final step is to analyze the over-and-under excavation. Fig. 4 
presents the workflow of the TIN-based method for over-and-under 
excavation volume calculation and comprises three sub-steps: 
1. Clipping and Plane Intersection: The ‘as-built ’ and the ‘as-designed ’ 
BIM are 3D mesh models (TINs), which comprise many triangular 
planes with three vertices, and each vertex has a unique coordinate 
value. The calculation commences by defining a clipping plane that 
slides along the central line of the tunnel with a fixed interval. Each

built ’ and ‘as-designed ’ models, and the intersection points are 
connected to become two contours, respectively. These contours are 
then used to form a cross-sectional slice representing the extent of 
over and undercut.

4. Case study 
The expressway project in Guizhou, China, is used to test the feasi -
bility of the proposed status digital twin. As shown in Fig. 5 , the project 
starts in the east of Leishan County (Guizhou, China), passes through a 
series of neighboring towns and villages, and ends at Zhongcheng in the 
north of Rongjiang County (Guizhou, China). The total length of the 
expressway project is 72.45 km, with an estimated budget of 12.29 
billion yuan (approx. US$ 1.84 billion). The project has a total of 18 
tunnels, with four of them longer than three kilometers. 
Fig. 5 shows the distribution of the four long tunnels, which share the 
same design pattern. The geological structure of the tunnels is complex, 
mostly highly weathered sandstone or slate, where the boundary of the 
rock formation and the local section of the occurrence change will lead 
to more overcut and undercut. 
Fig. 6 presents the rock quality distribution for the Leigongshan 
Tunnel, the longest among the 18 tunnels in this project. The tunnel has 
a total length of 4960 m with host rock quality between level IV and V 
according to the Code for Design of Road Tunnel (JTG D70-2004) in 
China (quality levels range from I to VI, a smaller number indicates 
better rock quality), where the uniaxial compressive strength of rocks 
changes between 8 and 25 MPa.

4.2. Data pre-processing 
Dedicated software (i.e., Cyclone) was first used to pre-process the 
obtained tunnel face point cloud data to form a preliminary processed 
xyz file, which provided a basis for subsequent gridding to process the 
original scanned point cloud. 
Automatic point cloud segmentation was achieved using a custom -
ized PointNet ++ algorithm by removing the classification module from 
the pipeline. The network was trained with 21-point cloud samples and 
validated and tested with 6 and 3 samples, respectively. Each point 
cloud sample initially had over one million points and was down- 
sampled to 20,480 points to save memory. The distribution of points 
between the tunnel face and tunnel surrounding rock classes was about 
15 % and 85 %. 
The network was trained with a learning rate of 0.00025 for 60 
rounds, which resulted in an average test accuracy of 0.94 and mIoU 
(mean Intersection over Union) of 0.91. This segmentation network can 
help separate the surrounding rock from the tunnel face and provide 
input for mesh conversion. Fig. 8 presents examples of automatic point 
cloud segmentation. 
Next, mesh processing software Geomagic Wrap 2017 was used to 
achieve further point cloud mesh conversion. The post-processed point 
cloud model was meshed and repaired to form a complete and smooth 
mesh model. Finally, 3D mesh models of the tunnel face and surrounding 
rocks with high geometric precision are obtained, as noted in Fig. 9 .

4.3. Results and analyses 
Three long tunnels were selected for evaluation, and 30 scanned 
samples were collected and compared against manual excavation re -
cordings. The tunnel name, number of samples, level of host rock, the 
thickness of C20 injection, and upper step arc length are shown in 
Table 1 . The pouring volume of shotcrete in all cases in selected tunnels 
was obtained from field records and used as ground truth for over/under 
excavation volume computation (the pouring volume can be different 
from the final volume after solidification but was the only available 
reference data). 
The TIN-based volume estimation method was compared with a 
manual measurement solution. The manual solution works as follows: 
the surveyor chooses a random location along the upper step arc inside a 
random cross-sectional plan and then notes the distance between the 
design surface (indicated by a total station) and the actual surface as 
excavation thickness d. Then, the estimated excavation volume can be 
calculated by (5): 
V = d * l * h (5) 
where V is the volume of over and undercuts in one drilling and blasting 
cycle; h is the length in the direction of drilling and blasting; l represents 
the upper step arc length. The manual measurement method is often 
used as a standard solution for its simplicity. Four sets of average over- 
and-under excavation thickness and four sets of manually calculated 
volumes were collected. 
To verify the effectiveness of the TIN-based volume estimation 
method, it was compared with the manual measurement solution using 
the actual pouring volume as a reference. The results of the comparisons 
are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 9 . 
From Fig. 10 (a), the error of volume calculation of the TIN-based 
approach and manual measurement was 4.54 % and 25.72 %, 
respectively (compared to the actual pouring volume). The error of 
volume calculation in groups 1, 3, and 4 were all less than 20 %, which 
meets the requirements of tunnel construction inspection. However, in 
group 2, the manual calculation error cannot fit the requirements. The 
calculation error of using our proposed approach is significantly smaller 
than the manual measurement error. Fig. 10 (b) shows that the thickness 
of the average over-under-excavation using the status digital twin 
approach and manual measurement was 0.05 m and 0.09 m, respec -
tively. The deviation of the average thickness for over-and-under exca -
vation using the status digital twin was smaller than that of the manual 
measurement under multiple sections. 
5. Discussion 
Digital twins serve as a transformative bridge between the physical 
and digital realms, enabling the acquisition of real-time data concerning 
physical objects ’ status, working conditions, and position. Despite their 
potential advantages across the asset life cycle, quantifying specific 
benefits and business value during construction remains a persistent 
challenge [31] . This research has developed a status digital twin capable 
of accurately determining excavation volumes during the excavation 
process. While immediate quantification of benefits may not be provided 
in this research, it is crucial to recognize and explore additional tangible 
advantages, including enhanced safety, increased productivity, and 
reduced costs. 
The proposed status digital twin significantly enhances informed 
decision-making processes by leveraging its technical attributes, such as 
data integration and semantic data description. Data integration ensures 
decision-makers access current information about the physical state, 
facilitating proactive decision-making. Furthermore, semantic data 
description improves data interpretability, enabling decision-makers to 
comprehend the context and relationships among different variables. 
For example, the attributes derived from the status digital twin primarily 
involve excavation conditions at various locations, specifically the dis -
tribution of over- and under-excavation along contour lines. Such in -
formation can assist on-site managers in intuitively understanding 
excavation quality in different tunnel zones. Additionally, it can aid in 
establishing the connection between excavation parameters and the 
volume of the excavation, optimizing parameters for subsequent exca -
vation rounds.

approach are well-suited for application on-site. The developed TIN- 
based excavation volume calculation method employed a fine-grained 
computation logic and was validated using the actual pouring volume

5.2. Limitations 
Despite the novelty of the research that has been presented, it needs 
to be acknowledged that several limitations exist. Ensuring the quality of 
point cloud data (e.g., ‘noise ’ ) is a challenge. In the case of a tunnel 
project, noise in the ‘Light Detection And Ranging ’ (LiDAR)-generated 
point clouds can materialize from unexpected objects or materials. Thus, 
the ‘noise ’ in the point cloud renders it difficult to represent the tunnel 
perfectly after triangulation. New algorithms are needed to address this 
limitation and improve the point cloud quality [47] . 
We acknowledge that our proposed status digital twin approach has 
been validated only in limited tunnel projects and does not apply to all 
geological conditions. For example, under conditions of abundant water, 
the mechanical parameters, such as the strength of the rock, will 
significantly decrease after it is encountered, and the deformation of the 
surrounding rock will increase, posing several challenges for the on-site 
application of this method [53] . Furthermore, it should be acknowledged 
that the dataset used in this research was imbalanced, negatively 
impacting the model ’ s ability to generalize well to new scenarios and 
unseen data. To address this limitation, there is a need to develop 
resampling techniques (i.e., undersampling and oversampling) and new 
algorithmic approaches (i.e., ensemble approaches). Moreover, the 
deformation of the surrounding rock will increase with the exposure 
time of the excavation face after excavation. In this instance, the accu -
racy of the data obtained during on-site data collection is greatly 
affected by the timing of on-site measurement collection. To address this 
issue, we suggest that the frequency of data collection needs to be 
increased. 
To sum up, the research focuses on introducing a new method for 
assessing the volume of excavation. This method involves a web-based 
application of a status digital twin, emphasizing functionality rather 
than user interface, accessibility, and mechanisms for ensuring data 
accuracy and timeliness. It should be acknowledged that the function (i. 
e., user interface, accessibility) is significant for digital twins, which are 
at the core of operation twins and simulation twins . Therefore, future 
research will shift its focus towards developing operation twins with real- 
time control and management functionalities and creating simulation 
twins for predictive analysis and scenarios. 
6. Conclusion 
This paper proposed a ‘status ’ digital twin to automatically assess a 
tunnel project ’ s over-and-under excavation with its development 
comprising three steps: (1) initialization of the ‘status ’ digital twin 
consisting of an as-designed BIM; (2) automated status update with as- 
built point cloud data; and (3) the calculation of over-and-under exca -
vation volume by a TIN-based geometric method. A large-scale tunnel 
project in Guizhou, China, was used to validate its effectiveness and 
feasibility. 
The results demonstrated that the status digital twin accurately 
evaluated over-and-under excavation with an average thickness of 0.05 
m and an error of volume calculation of 4.54 %, respectively. In this 
case, site managers, for example, can take action to control the exca -
vation process. Evaluating overcut and undercut during excavation can 
result in effective managerial interventions to ensure a tunnel ’ s stability 
and structural safety. 
For future research, the status digital twin model can be extended to 
simulate the excavation process and predict the potential risk caused by 
under and over-excavating. In addition, further research is required to 
consider tunnel face conditions (e.g., distribution of cracks and rock 
layers on tunnel face). The distribution of cracks and rock layers on the 
tunnel face can lead to different blast-hole distribution and further 
control parameter variations, which enables more accurate over-and- 
under excavation control.
