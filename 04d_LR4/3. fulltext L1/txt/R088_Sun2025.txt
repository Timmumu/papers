ABSTRACT
Geological uncertainty ahead of the tunnel face presents significant challenges to the safety and efficiency of
tunnel construction. To address this issue, this paper explores the feasibility of using a digital twin approach to
enhance geological modeling and rock classification. The proposed method integrates multi-source geological
data to construct a multi-dimensional geological virtual model, and applies a fuzzy cloud probability model to
quantify uncertainty, enabling intelligent classification of surrounding rock. Results from tests on five rock
tunnel projects using the iS3 platform show that the overall rock classification accuracy of the method is 92.95 %.
These findings clearly demonstrate that the digital twin approach significantly improves geological transparency,
risk management, and decision-making efficiency in complex rock tunnel environments. Future research will
focus on exploring the application of this method in soft soils, underwater tunnels, and other broader geological
conditions.
1. Introduction
Rock tunnel construction faces complex and unpredictable geolog-
ical conditions that can jeopardize construction safety and affect project
efficiency [ 1 – 7 ]. Advanced geological forecasting plays a key role in
detecting adverse conditions ahead of the tunnel face [ 8 – 11 ]. However,
traditional methods mainly rely on limited data sources and the sub-
jective judgments of geological engineers, which often fail to fully
capture the inherent uncertainty and complexity of rock mass behavior
[ 12 – 15 ]. To address these issues, there is an urgent need for new ap-
proaches that integrate multi-source data, enabling a comprehensive
and real-time interpretation of geological conditions. In recent years,
digital twin technology has emerged as a promising solution in the field
of tunnel construction. This technology integrates multi-source hetero-
geneous data to construct high-precision virtual geological models and
employs intelligent algorithms to enable real-time interactions between
physical entities and their digital twins, thereby providing engineering
managers with intuitive, dynamic monitoring and early warning capa-
bilities. However, most current geological digital twin models lack
rigorous mathematical descriptions of the randomness and fuzziness
inherent in rock mass parameters and do not establish a complete system
for data acquisition, model updating, and decision feedback [ 16 – 18 ].
This limitation restricts their effectiveness in real-time construction
applications [ 19 ].
Based on the aforementioned background and technological status,
this paper focuses on the following research question: How can a closed-
loop digital twin system for Data-Model-Decision be constructed to
achieve real-time and accurate decision of advanced geological condi-
tions within a 30-m range ahead of the tunnel face during excavation?
The “ Data-Model-Decision ” is a concept designed to progress step by
step, starting from data collection, followed by virtual model construc-
tion, and ultimately supporting decision-making, forming a closed-loop
system.
To address this issue, the paper proposes a three-step digital twin
approach: (1) Multi-source information collection and database con-
struction: Geological information, such as water content, lithology, and
integrity, is extracted using image processing and other methods. This
information is aligned in three-dimensional space and transformed into
consistent point cloud data structures stored in a database. (2) Advanced
geological virtual model construction: Point cloud models ahead of the
tunnel face are generated based on the geometric characteristics of the
tunnel face. Geological attributes are assigned to the point cloud model
using the KNN interpolation based on information from the geological
database. (3) Intelligent decision-making for surrounding rock classifi-
cation: The geological attributes of the constructed point cloud models
are used as input to a fuzzy cloud probability model to quantify un-
certainties in the geological information, allowing for the calculation of
rock classification probabilities for each meter ahead of the tunnel face.
The classification is determined based on the maximum probability. The
“ advanced geological conditions ” referred to in this paper pertain to the
geological conditions within a 30-m range ahead of the tunnel face

2.1. Advanced geological forecasting in tunnel construction
Advanced geological forecasting in tunnel construction aims to
identify the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face in advance,
effectively preventing accidents caused by adverse geological factors
[20,21]. Traditional advanced geological forecasting methods, such as
GPR, TSP, TEM, and while-drilling, use different physical principles to
obtain information about the physical properties of the geology ahead of
the tunnel face [22–24]. Under normal conditions, the effective detec-
tion ranges are as follows: GPR (15-30 m), TSP (100-150 m), TEM (30-
60 m), and while-drilling measurements (3-15 m). These methods pro-
vide foundational data for assessing geological conditions, but due to the
complexity of geology, a single method often fails to capture the un-
certainty and complexity of the geology [25]. Currently, tunnel con-
struction sites mainly rely on geological engineers to subjectively assess
the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face based on information
from advanced geological forecasting. There is a lack of a method that
can automatically make decisions regarding the geological conditions
ahead of the tunnel face based on the data provided by advanced
geological forecasting. Therefore, researchers have begun to explore the
integration of multi-source data to make tunnel geology more trans-
parent, such as combining GPR with drilling to complement the ad-
vantages of different methods. Additionally, in recent years, computer
technology has been increasingly applied to geological forecasting,
utilizing data-driven methods to extract valuable patterns from histori-
cal data and improve the accuracy of geological condition predictions
[26]. Wang et al. proposed a semi-supervised learning model based on
while-drilling data, which combines dense convolutional autoencoders
and hybrid deep neural networks. This approach significantly improved
the accuracy and stability of surrounding rock discontinuity detection
[27]. Xue et al. adopted a novel spatiotemporal sequence approach to
interpret the continuously exposed rock mass during tunnel excavation.
They proposed a spatially aware recurrent neural network that uses
historical fracture maps as input to predict the unexcavated sections
[28]. Hansen et al. proposed a machine learning method that uses while-
drilling data to predict the rock type ahead of the tunnel face [29]. These
new methods have significantly improved the accuracy and real-time
performance of advanced geological forecasting. However, challenges
remain in effectively integrating multiple data sources and handling
uncertainties.
2.2. Digital twin in tunnel construction
Digital twin technology is an emerging technology that has rapidly
developed in the engineering field in recent years. By creating a virtual
replica of a physical entity, it connects real-time data from the physical
world with virtual models, enabling dynamic reflection and prediction
of physical processes [30–33]. In tunnel construction, digital twin
technology can integrate geological, structural, and construction data in
real time to create a dynamic virtual model of the construction site. This
allows engineers to identify potential risks in a virtual environment and
optimize construction plans [34–36].
The advantage of digital twin technology lies in its ability to simulate
and predict the constantly changing geological conditions during tunnel
construction with high precision, while also offering powerful data
analysis and visualization capabilities [37]. Zhang et al. installed
Internet of Things sensors on TBM to transfer the TBM’s actual operating
status from the physical object to the digital model [38]. Ye et al. pro-
posed a digital twin-based multi-information intelligent early warning
and safety management platform. This platform sets up communication
lines within the tunnel using multifunctional base stations to automat-
ically collect and transmit multi-source data. It then uses this data to
virtually simulate dynamic construction conditions and surrounding
rock behaviors [39]. Ling et al. integrated digital twin technology into
the implementation of rock tunnel Just-In-Time design and proposed a
scene-entity-relationship-event-control modeling approach. This new
method serves as a cross-domain sharing and reuse model for digital
twin applications [40]. Wu et al. proposed a multi-level voxel repre-
sentation method based on linear segmentation, which efficiently up-
dates and expresses the complex features of the tunnel geological
environment, providing real-time dynamic support for digital twin ap-
plications [41]. However, the construction of digital twins relies on the
efficient acquisition and integration of multi-source data, which de-
mands high data processing capabilities and modeling accuracy. Addi-
tionally, the geological environment in rock tunnel construction is
highly complex and uncertain, making it a challenge to quantify these
uncertainties and make intelligent decisions based on geological con-
ditions in the context of digital twin applications.
2.3. Cloud model in tunnel engineering applications
The cloud model, a mathematical tool for addressing uncertainty and
fuzziness, has been widely used in tunnel engineering applications [42].
The core advantage of the cloud model lies in its ability to convert be-
tween qualitative concepts and quantitative descriptions, making it
particularly well-suited for capturing the variability and uncertainty of
geological conditions [43]. In tunnel construction, the geological con-
ditions are often influenced by multiple factors, such as lithology, water
content, and structural integrity. The interactions between these factors
are complex and characterized by high uncertainty [44–47].
The cloud model uses membership functions and probability distri-
butions to transform the multi-dimensional features of surrounding rock
conditions into a comprehensive probability cloud map, providing en-
gineers with an intuitive basis for risk assessment. Compared to tradi-
tional single-source risk assessment methods, the advantage of the cloud
model lies in its ability to effectively quantify both fuzziness and
randomness, thereby enhancing the accuracy and dynamic nature of
geological condition evaluation. Sun et al. proposed a dynamic risk
assessment system that combines a two-dimensional cloud model for
risk early warning in tunnel construction. This approach effectively
handles the uncertainties in geological conditions and provides a reli-
able decision-making basis for the construction process [48]. Chen et al.
proposed a hybrid method that combines an improved cloud model with
a Bayesian network for dynamic safety analysis and assessment of
adjacent buildings impacted by tunnel construction [49]. Tian et al.
integrated resilience theory with a combined weighted-cloud model to
assess the flood resilience of metro stations [50]. Zhu et al. proposed a
cloud model-based random forest method to address inconsistencies in
mapping monitoring data to the health rate of metro tunnels [51]. In this
study, the cloud model is improved by incorporating fuzzy mathematics
theory, resulting in the development of a fuzzy cloud probability model.
This model is used to quantify the uncertainty of the constructed
geological digital twins and make intelligent decisions regarding sur-
rounding rock classification based on probabilities, providing crucial
support for safety decision-making in rock tunnel construction.

3.1. Multi-source information collection and database construction
This section focuses on extracting geological information from
various sources, such as GPR, TEM, boreholes, geological profile map,
and tunnel excavation exposure, using techniques like image processing.
The extracted data is then converted into a unified point cloud data
structure and spatially registered in three dimensions. This multi-source
geological information is stored in a database, creating a dynamic multi-
source information system that can be updated as new geological data
becomes available.
3.1.1. Lithology information database construction
The construction of the lithology database relies primarily on bore-
hole data and the lithological information exposed during tunnel exca-
vation. Borehole data provides stratigraphic information at various
depths along the tunnel. To enhance the spatial resolution of this data,
line interpolation is used to convert the borehole data into three-
dimensional point cloud data [ 52 , 53 ]. Specifically, borehole depth
data are resampled at a fixed step size (in this study, a step size of 1 m
was adopted), converting the lithology information at each sampling
depth into point cloud data points with precise three-dimensional co-
ordinates. In addition, the geological information exposed during actual
tunnel excavation serves as an essential supplementary data source for
the lithology database. As excavation progresses, the lithological con-
ditions exposed at the tunnel face are recorded on-site and spatially
positioned in three dimensions. Subsequently, these data are integrated
with the existing borehole data and transformed into three-dimensional
point cloud data using the same linear interpolation approach, main-
taining a consistent 1-m step interval. These newly incorporated data
continuously enrich the lithology database, enabling it to be dynami-
cally updated and optimized throughout the construction process, thus
ensuring accurate, real-time representation of geological conditions
ahead of the tunnel face.
Geological profile maps constitute another critical data source. These
maps, drawn by geological engineers, illustrate lithological distributions
along the tunnel axis and embody the engineers ’ understanding of
geological structures [ 54 ]. To effectively convert two-dimensional
geological profile data into three-dimensional point cloud data, this
study adopts a digital processing workflow: (1) RGB values of each pixel
in the geological profile maps are first extracted; (2) Lithology
information is assigned to each pixel by establishing a mapping rela-
tionship between RGB values and lithological categories; (3) Based on
spatial scaling relationships derived from geological profile maps, each
pixel ’ s lithological information is accurately registered in three-
dimensional coordinates; (4) The lithological information, along with
corresponding spatial coordinate points, are formatted into structured
datasets and stored to construct the comprehensive lithology database.
Finally, borehole data, tunnel excavation exposure data, and
geological profile map data are integrated into a unified three-
dimensional point cloud database. After spatial registration and data
consistency processing, the consolidated database accurately reflects the
geological features along the tunnel alignment. The process is illustrated
in Fig. 2 .
3.1.2. Integrity information database construction
The extraction of surrounding rock integrity information ahead of
the tunnel face is a critical step in tunnel engineering, providing
essential data for safety assessments during tunnel construction. GPR is a
predictive geological method based on electromagnetic waves [ 55 ]. The
degree of fluctuation in the GPR electromagnetic waves ahead of the
tunnel face can partially reflect the level of integrity in the surrounding
rock. In this study, image processing techniques are used to extract the
integrity information of the surrounding rock from the GPR data. This
information is then converted into three-dimensional point cloud data,
providing support for subsequent analysis and modeling.
The specific steps for processing GPR images are as follows: (1)
Image preprocessing: To effectively extract key information that reflects
the integrity of the surrounding rock, this study applies morphological
operations. The process starts with erosion, followed by dilation to
remove small and insignificant linear noise features in the image, while
preserving obvious fluctuation regions that reflect rock mass fragmen-
tation. A 4 × 4 square structural element is chosen during the
morphological operation, as its size is suitable for processing fine lines
and small noise points, while retaining the shape of the main black
areas. Through this process, the original GPR electromagnetic wave
image is transformed into a binary image that intuitively reflects the
degree of surrounding rock fragmentation. (2) Fragmentation area
identification: However, the identified main fluctuation areas still
appear wavy. In actual engineering, the fragmentation of the sur-
rounding rock typically manifests as irregular block-like fragments.
Therefore, elliptical fitting is further applied to the contour of each main
fluctuation area to generate elliptical regions that more realistically

3.1.3. Water content information database construction
The extraction of geological water content information is a key
component in rock tunnel construction and geological forecasting,
providing essential data for groundwater management and the safety of
the rock tunnel [ 56 ]. In this study, the image data obtained from TEM
scanning is processed to extract the water content information of the
geology, which is then converted into three-dimensional point cloud
data for subsequent analysis.
The specific process steps for processing TEM images and con-
structing a water content information database are as follows: (1) Image
preprocessing: During the image processing stage, the data is converted
into NumPy arrays for numerical computation and analysis. To ensure
the extraction of valid color information, a color distance threshold is set
to filter out pixels close to white, thereby avoiding noise interference
during analysis. In this study, the color distance threshold is set to 90,
which effectively distinguishes near-white pixels from other colored
pixels. (2) Water content information extraction: Based on the definition
of fissure water pressure for different water-bearing rock types in the
standard for engineering classification of rock mass, the resistivity
ranges obtained from TEM are assigned different water content indices:
resistivity values of 0 – 800 are assigned a water content index of 1.5
(represented by red); 800 – 1600 are assigned a water content index of 1
(represented by yellow); 1600 – 2400 are assigned a water content index
of 0.2 (represented by green); and resistivity values greater than 2400
are assigned a water content index of 0.01 (represented by blue). In
practical engineering applications, the water content indices corre-
sponding to different resistivity ranges should be adjusted according to
the specific conditions of the project and the characteristics of the in-
strument. After establishing the mapping rules for color-resistivity-
water content, all valid pixels in the image are traversed, and their
RGB values are compared to determine the corresponding water content
index. For each identified water-bearing pixel, its coordinates within the
image and the corresponding water content index are recorded and
stored in a list or data table, forming the preliminary results of water
content information extraction. (3) Three-dimensional spatial registra-
tion of water content information: After extracting the water content
information, three-dimensional spatial registration is performed. Based
on the actual acquisition position of TEM and the proportional rela-
tionship between the image and the detection range, the pixel co-
ordinates in the image are mapped to the actual X, Y, and Z spatial
coordinates, forming preliminary three-dimensional coordinate data.
This information includes the spatial location of each pixel and its cor-
responding water content index. These data are stored in a structured
format, constructing the water content information database. The pro-
cess of constructing the water content information database is illustrated
in Fig. 4 .

3.2. Advanced geological virtual model of tunnel
Section 3.2 primarily discusses the process of constructing the
advanced geological virtual model for the tunnel. This section first in-
troduces the key mathematical theory behind the KNN interpolation. It
then explains how the KNN algorithm is applied to construct the
advanced geological virtual model using a point cloud representation.
3.2.1. KNN interpolation
The KNN algorithm is a widely used interpolation method. A detailed
mathematical derivation of this interpolation technique can be found in
the study by Chen et al. and Yang et al. [ 23 , 57 ]. The KNN algorithm
offers superior interpretability and low computational complexity,
making it highly suitable for integration with digital twin technology for
rapid geological condition analysis and feedback. However, currently
popular machine learning algorithms are not suitable for constructing
geological digital twin models. This is because these algorithms typically
require large numbers of target variables for validation, while verifying
geological conditions obtained through advanced geological forecasting
relies on the geological information exposed during tunnel excavation.
Within the prediction range of a single geological forecast, typically only
geological information from one or two exposed tunnel faces can be
collected, which is insufficient for machine learning methods to operate.
Furthermore, the “ black-box ” nature of machine learning algorithms
significantly limits their applicability on construction sites where
stringent safety standards and transparency are essential. Therefore, this
study uses KNN interpolation to construct a geological virtual model.
The basic idea is to calculate the distance between the sample to be
predicted and the samples in the training set, identify the K nearest
neighbors, and make predictions based on the attributes of these
neighbors. The workflow of the KNN interpolation includes the
following steps:

For classification problems, KNN applies a majority voting rule,
selecting the most frequently occurring class among the neighbors as the
interpolated result. In this study, the KNN algorithm is used for con-
structing virtual model of geology through the following steps: First,
features (X, Y, Z coordinates) and target variables (geological condi-
tions) are extracted from a dataset containing spatial coordinate points
and geological states. Next, a K-Nearest Neighbors model is initialized,
with the number of neighbors set to 100. The number of neighbors is set
to 100 because the 3D point cloud model within the 30 m ahead-of-face
region is extremely dense — averaging thousands of samples per meter.
Selecting too few neighbors renders the interpolation overly sensitive to
local noise, causing discontinuities, while selecting too many produces
overly smooth results that obscure critical geological anomalies.
Extensive experiments indicate that classification accuracy remains
most stable when K ranges from 80 to 120. Consequently, K = 100 is
chosen as the midpoint to balance smoothness with the ability to resolve
important spatial variations. The geological properties of the unknown
point are interpolated based on the geological properties of the 100
known points surrounding it. Lastly, the point cloud data is color-coded
according to the interpolated results to visualize different geological
conditions. Through the effective application of the KNN interpolation,
this study offers a solid scientific basis for the dynamic monitoring and
analysis of advanced geological conditions in tunnel projects.
3.2.2. Point cloud model construction
In tunnel construction, building an accurate point cloud model is a
crucial step for achieving dynamic virtual mapping of the geological
conditions ahead of the tunnel face [ 58 , 59 ]. In this study, databases for
lithology, integrity, and water content information are constructed
separately. These databases, comprised of point cloud data, provide a
representation of the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face to a
certain extent.
After constructing the point cloud data, the KNN algorithm is applied
to interpolate the geological properties of the spatial coordinate points
in the point cloud. KNN is a distance-based algorithm that estimates the

state of a point by selecting the K nearest neighbors. In this study, each
spatial point in the point cloud contains three-dimensional coordinates
(X, Y, Z) along with information about the geological condition. For each
new point ahead of the tunnel face, the KNN model calculates its dis-
tance to other known points in the database, selects the K closest
neighbors, and uses their condition values to estimate the lithology,
integrity, and water content of the new point.
In the implementation process, a dense three-dimensional point
cloud is first generated to cover the area ahead of the tunnel face. This
area consists of a semicircular region representing the tunnel crown
cross-section and a rectangular region representing the tunnel bottom. A
series of points are generated along the tunnel direction (Y-axis) at fixed
intervals, and densely distributed in both the radial and angular di-
rections, creating a point cloud model ahead of the tunnel face. Through
these steps, the constructed point cloud cross-sections represent the
geometry of the tunnel face and extend along the tunnel direction at a
fixed step size. Next, for each point, the KNN algorithm calculates its
distance to the existing points in the database and selects the K nearest
neighbors. By computing a weighted average of the condition values of
these neighbors, the algorithm assigns the lithology, integrity, or water
content status to each point in the point cloud model.
To visually present the prediction results, the geological condition
values are visualized using color coding, with different values repre-
sented by different colors. By mapping these colors onto the three-
dimensional point cloud model, the study provides an intuitive display
of the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face, offering a clear and
quantifiable basis for construction decision-making. The workflow for
constructing the point cloud model is illustrated in Fig. 5 .
3.3. Surrounding rock classification intelligent decision-making
Section 3.3 primarily explains how to use the proposed fuzzy cloud
probability model for intelligent decision-making in surrounding rock
classification. This section first introduces the fundamental mathemat-
ical theory behind the fuzzy cloud probability model. Building on this
theory, it then integrates the calculation methods for surrounding rock
classification, creating a process that uses the multi-dimensional
geological virtual model as input, quantifies geological uncertainties,
and ultimately makes intelligent decisions regarding surrounding rock
classification.
3.3.1. Fuzzy cloud probability model

4. Application case
Section 4 presents the engineering application of the digital twin
method for advanced geological conditions in tunnel construction pro-
posed in this paper. This section first introduces the engineering back-
ground of the selected case study, followed by a detailed explanation of
the entire process of applying the proposed method to this project.
Finally, with the help of the iS3 platform, the method is widely applied
to five rock tunnels to verify its universality and reliability.
4.1. Engineering background
The Yanjiashai Tunnel is located along the Lei Bo Kaha Luo to
Yongshan connection line in Yunnan Province, China, and is considered
one of the critical control projects along the entire route. The tunnel is
designed with separate passages, with a maximum depth of approxi-
mately 933 m. The geological conditions at the tunnel site are highly
complex, featuring weak surrounding rocks such as shale and argilla-
ceous limestone. The rock mass is significantly fractured, with well-
developed joints and fissures. The tunnel area is prone to various
adverse geological phenomena, including landslides, karst develop-
ment, and high in-situ stress. The hydrogeological conditions are also
intricate, with groundwater mainly consisting of bedrock fissure water
and karst water, both of which are abundant. The maximum predicted
water inflow in the main tunnel is estimated to be 25.37 × 10

4.4. Universality and reliability verification
To verify the universality of the tunnel construction data-model-
decision closed-loop advanced geological digital twin method pro-
posed in this paper, this study utilizes an intelligent tunnel construction
platform developed based on digital twin technology for complex
mountainous conditions, using the iS3 platform, as shown in Fig. 20 . The
iS3 platform, constructed by the team led by Academician Zhu Hehua, is
a decision service system that integrates infrastructure lifecycle data
collection, processing, expression, and analysis. The algorithm intro-
duced in this paper serves as the core service of this platform. Through
this platform, the algorithm can not only be widely applied across
multiple tunnel projects but also enable real-time acquisition of relevant
data from various tunnels and provide rapid feedback.
Specifically, the iS3 platform can upload real-time advanced
geological forecast data and other relevant information directly from the
construction site. This data encompasses geological details, monitoring
data, and tunnel construction progress, as illustrated in Fig. 21 (a). By
employing the algorithm proposed in this paper, rapid decision-making
regarding the geological conditions ahead of the tunnel face is enabled,
thereby providing the construction team with reliable geological fore-
cast information, as shown in Fig. 21 (b). The platform has already been
successfully applied in several rock tunnel projects. With the broader
implementation of the iS3 platform, the geological digital twin frame-
work introduced in this paper has been deployed in numerous moun-
tainous rock tunnel projects, validating its universality and effectiveness
in complex geological environments.
To further validate the reliability of the proposed framework, a
validation scheme is designed to compare the rock mass classification
decision results obtained through the geological digital twin method
before tunnel excavation with the actual rock mass classifications
exposed after excavation, thereby verifying the accuracy and effective-
ness of the advanced rock mass condition decision-making. First, to
conduct large-scale testing and validation, a total of 539 exposed rock
mass classification data points from five rock tunnel projects recorded
by the platform are collected. These data come from different locations
and depths of the tunnel face, covering a variety of complex rock types
and geological conditions. The exposed rock mass classification data are

5. Discussion
The discussion section mainly explores the applicability, computa-
tional demands, shortcomings, and future research suggestions of the
proposed advanced geological digital twin method. It is hoped that more
scholars will build upon the method presented in this paper and, based
on the suggestions provided in this section, continue to explore related
research fields.

tunnel face and make classification decisions for the surrounding rock at
