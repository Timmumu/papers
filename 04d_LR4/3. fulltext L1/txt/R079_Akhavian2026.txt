 A B S T R A C T
This paper introduces BIM2RDT (Building Information Models to Robot-Ready Construction Digital Twins), an 
AI-assisted pipeline transforming static BIM into dynamic digital twins for construction robot navigation and 
safety monitoring. The framework fuses BIM geometry, unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) visual observations, 
and IoT sensor data to enable automated site monitoring and safety-aware path planning. A core contribution 
is Semantic-Gravity ICP (SG-ICP), a point cloud registration method leveraging LLM-guided semantic reasoning 
to assign BIM-family-specific upright priors, improving alignment robustness in low-feature construction 
scenes. The framework integrates YOLOE open-vocabulary detection for BIM-conditioned perception and 
demonstrates safety monitoring through Hand-Arm Vibration (HAV) sensing written into IFC-compatible digital 
twin representations. Controlled experiments show SG-ICP reduces RMSE by approximately 60%–97% over 
standard registration methods in high-noise scenarios. These capabilities represent an initial step towards 
agentic construction workflows, with LLM-orchestrated pipelines that condition perception, trigger IFC updates, 
and propose navigation constraints.
1. Introduction
The Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (AEC) industry is 
in the midst of a profound digital transformation. Historically charac-
terized as a late adopter of digital technology, the sector has tradition-
ally relied heavily on the experiential knowledge of its professionals. 
However, growing pressures from economic uncertainties due to factors 
such as low productivity, persistent labor shortages, and the increasing 
complexity of modern projects are compelling companies to rapidly 
adopt digital construction management strategies to optimize resources 
and workflows. Market projections indicate that adoption of technolo-
gies such as the Digital Twins (DTs) in the construction market will 
reach $155.01 billion by 2030, with safety applications and worker 
monitoring driving significant growth [1]. For example, Cyber–Physical 
Systems (CPS) integrate computation, networking, and physical pro-
cesses, creating a feedback loop where physical systems are monitored 
and controlled by computer-based algorithms [2]. Technological ad-
vances such as Building Information Modeling (BIM), DT, robotics, and 
Artificial Intelligence (AI) form the basis of a next-generation approach 
to construction management that moves beyond manual processes and 
reactive decision-making [3].

and autonomous decision-making for safe and efficient site navigation.
The framework fuses three data streams: (1) geometric and se-

This study investigates three research questions: RQ1: How can 
semantic information from static BIMs be leveraged to constrain and 
improve point cloud registration accuracy in construction environments 
with ambiguous geometry and limited features? RQ2: To what ex-
tent can an LLM-based reasoning provide physically-informed, object-
specific priors that reduce local-minimum failures in scan-to-BIM align-
ment? RQ3: How can sensor-detected safety activities be semanti-
cally mapped into interoperable digital twin representations to enable 
proactive intervention and context-aware robot planning?
To answer these questions, BIM2RDT implements a three-stage 
perception-to-update loop. BIM semantics are translated into context-
specific prompts for open-vocabulary detection using YOLOE [4], en-
abling recognition of both permanent BIM families and temporary 
site objects without retraining. We introduce Semantic-Gravity ICP 
(SG-ICP), a point-cloud registration method where an LLM assigns 
object-adaptive upright priors per BIM family (e.g., heavy crates vs. 
easily tipped cones), producing physically plausible orientation reg-
ularization. Finally, sensor-detected activities are mapped into IFC 
constructs (IfcEvent, IfcTask), demonstrated via HAV monitoring against 
ISO 5349-1 thresholds [5] as a proof-of-concept. The resulting activity 
layers generate contextual cost maps for robot path planning. The 
specific contributions of this work are:
• A BIM-conditioned perception pipeline using BIM object labels to 
prompt open-vocabulary detection (YOLOE) and define ROIs for 
scan-to-BIM alignment.
• SG-ICP: a registration method using LLM-inferred, BIM-family-
specific upright priors to regularize ICP and reduce
local-minimum failures.
• An IFC-compatible mechanism to write sensor-recognized activi-
ties into the digital twin (via IfcEvent /IfcTask), demonstrated with 
HAV monitoring.
• Experimental validation showing RMSE reductions up to 97% in 
challenging scenarios and its consequences in context-aware path 
planning.

2. Literature review
This review is organized to mirror the BIM2RDT pipeline: (i) dy-
namic construction digital twins, (ii) robotic site monitoring and BIM-
aided navigation, (iii) BIM semantics and ICP-based registration, and 
(iv) AI-based autonomous agents in construction.
2.1. From BIM to digital twin with geometric and operational updates
The evolution from Building Information Models (BIM) to digital 
twins (DTs) in construction represents a paradigm shift towards data-
driven, real-time management of built environments. The defining 
characteristic that distinguishes a true DT from an advanced BIM model 
is this bi-directional, real-time or near-real-time data connection [6]. 
This cyber–physical integration establishes a continuous feedback loop: 
the physical asset is instrumented with sensors that feed data to the 
digital model, and the digital model, in turn, can be used to ana-
lyze, simulate, and control the physical asset. During construction, 
a dominant technical gap is geometric synchronization: maintaining 
an as-built geometry that stays aligned with BIM coordinates while 
temporary objects, staging, and work fronts evolve. This motivates 
scan-to-BIM workflows and automated update mechanisms that can 
operate continuously rather than as occasional manual reconciliation. 
Additionally, a construction digital twin requires an operational link be-
tween the physical site and its digital representation. Prior DT research 
has emphasized IoT-to-model connections for monitoring and analytics, 
often highlighting interoperability and real-time data streams as key 
enablers [6–8]. Wearables and IoT streams enable activity recognition 
and exposure monitoring, but these signals are often managed outside 
the BIM/DT representation, limiting their integration into planning 
and coordination. Hand-arm vibration (HAV) exposure, for example, 
is a representative safety signal because it has standardized evaluation 
metrics and actionable thresholds for wearable-based safety monitoring 
in construction settings (e.g., ISO 5349-1) [5,9].
2.2. Robotics reality capture and BIM-aided navigation
The continuous and accurate acquisition of as-built data is the 
lifeblood of a Digital Twin. While fixed sensors provide valuable data 
streams, mobile robotic platforms have emerged as a primary method 
for comprehensive and autonomous site scanning. Mobile robots pro-
vide repeatable sensing for progress and condition capture, reducing 
the cost and safety risk of manual scanning [10]. In indoor, GPS-denied 
construction environments, reliable autonomy requires robust localiza-
tion and mapping under frequent changes and occlusions [11]. Prior 
work has explored UGV platforms for inspection and monitoring [12], 
and quadruped systems in particular offer superior traversal capability 
for unfinished interiors and cluttered work zones [13,14]. The fusion 
and registration of data collected from multiple scans and different 
sensor modalities (e.g., LiDAR, cameras) into a single, coherent model 
is another complex technical problem [15,16]. To address this, a re-
curring theme is leveraging building structure (planarity, corridors, 
cores) and pre-existing digital models to improve robustness with low 
computational cost. BIM-aided localization and mapping reduce drift 
and provide meaningful anchors, but success depends on accurate 
registration between robot observations and BIM pointclouds.
2.3. BIM semantics and geometric registration with ICP
The accurate alignment of 3D point clouds is a cornerstone of 
robotics, computer vision, and digital twinning for the built environ-
ment. The Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm remains the founda-
tional method for this task, but its limitations have inspired decades 
of innovation. The standard ICP algorithm iteratively refines the rigid 
transformation between two sets of point clouds by minimizing the sum 
of squared distances between corresponding points [17]. Despite its 
widespread adoption, ICP’s performance is highly sensitive to the initial 
alignment and is famously susceptible to converging to local minima, 
especially in scenes with sparse features, repetitive geometry, or poor 
initial overlap [18]. In construction, robust point cloud registration 
(often referred to as ‘‘scan-to-BIM’’) is critical for as-built verification, 
progress monitoring, and digital twin maintenance. Researchers have 
extensively used ICP and its variants to align laser scans of a job site 
with the as-designed BIM model to detect deviations [19]. The struc-
tured nature of building environments, with prevalent planar surfaces, 
makes point-to-plane ICP particularly effective. Global BIM-point cloud 
registration minimizes deviations using ICP, evaluating alignments for 
construction quality control [20]. Lidar inertial odometry (LIO) and 
BIM have been used together for robust robot localization, using ICP-
like methods to map and navigate in building environments [21].

2.4. AI and autonomous agents in construction
Agentic AI systems are defined by capabilities such as proactive 
planning, contextual memory, sophisticated tool use, and the ability 
to adapt their behavior based on environmental feedback [23]. Un-
like automation tools in the built-environment industry that follow 
rigid, pre-programmed instructions, or analytical models that simply 
provide outputs for human interpretation, agentic AI systems are de-
signed to perceive their environment, reason about that information, 
and act autonomously to achieve specified goals [24]. This capacity 
for autonomous, goal-directed action makes them particularly well-
suited for managing the unpredictable and dynamic nature of con-
struction projects. Recent frameworks distinguish between simple AI 
agents (modular, task-specific systems) and true agentic AI, which 
exhibits multi-agent collaboration, dynamic task decomposition, per-
sistent memory, and coordinated autonomy [25]. Industry practition-
ers have operationalized this concept through ‘‘agentic workflows’’ 
where AI systems iteratively reflect, plan, and use tools to accomplish 
complex tasks through multi-step processes rather than single-prompt 
responses [26]. In the construction context, this translates to systems 
capable of perception, planning, and action in unstructured environ-
ments, representing a critical evolution for managing construction exe-
cution complexity. Consequently, the development of intelligent agents 
capable of perception, planning, and action in unstructured construc-
tion environments represents a critical and underexplored research 
frontier [24].
2.5. Summary and gap addressed by BIM2RDT
Existing research provides strong building blocks such as robotic 
scanning, BIM-aided localization, ICP variants, and sensor-based safety 
monitoring. However, these are often treated as separate systems. 
BIM2RDT closes the loop by (i) conditioning perception on BIM seman-
tics to stabilize object identification in dynamic inventories, (ii) improv-
ing scan-to-BIM alignment using SG-ICP with object-adaptive semantic-
gravity priors, and (iii) writing safety-relevant activities into an inter-
operable twin representation that can directly inform robot planning. 
This structure directly motivates the methodology and matches the 
evaluation reported in the experiments.

3. Methodology
This section presents the BIM2RDT methodology in three stages. 
Sections 3.1–3.3 describe the perception pipeline, Section 3.4 intro-
duces the core innovation of SG-ICP, and Sections 3.5–3.7 detail how 
safety activities and geometric updates are written back to the IFC 
model.
3.1. Object detection
We employ the YOLOE open-vocabulary object detector [4]. The 
selection of YOLOE is deliberate and critical to the BIM2RDT frame-
work. Unlike traditional closed-set object detectors, which are limited 
to a predefined set of categories, open-vocabulary models can iden-
tify objects based on arbitrary textual descriptions. This capability is 
particularly suitable for construction sites, which are populated with 
a broad assortment and often variable set of object labels, from stan-
dard BIM components (e.g., columns, beams) to temporary materials 
(e.g., pallets, scaffolding) and unplanned obstacles. YOLOE allows our 
system to be highly adaptable, as it can be dynamically prompted with 
the specific BIM families relevant to the robot’s current operational 
area without requiring model retraining, a significant advantage over 
alternatives.
Semantic information from the BIM or user-specified labels ini-
tializes a textual prompt that biases classification towards the target 
BIM families. The process begins by translating the semantic infor-
mation from the BIM into a concise textual prompt for the detector. 
BIM family names, which can be verbose or contain non-descriptive 
metadata (e.g., ‘‘Steel_I-Beam_W12x26_A992’’), are processed 
using an LLM to extract the core object class. For example, the afore-
mentioned name is simplified to ‘‘steel I-beam’’. Similarly, ‘‘Traf-
fic_Cone_Orange_70cm’’ becomes ‘‘traffic cone’’. The prompt 
concatenates BIM family names, such as ‘‘Concrete Pillar’’ or ‘‘Safety 
Cone’’, with contextual descriptors like ‘‘construction site object’’ to 
bias classification. As an example, for a BIM family ‘‘Wall’’ the prompt 
might be: ‘‘a concrete wall in a building site’’.
This list of simplified class names, derived from the BIM elements 
expected in the scene, forms the textual prompt. For a given region, 
the prompt might be a list such as: [‘‘steel I-beam’’, ‘‘traffic cone’’, 
‘‘wooden crate’’, ‘‘steel barrel’’]. This context-specific prompt biases 
the classification towards the target BIM families, improving detection 
accuracy and reducing false positives. The detector outputs bounding 
boxes with associated class names, which we then map one-to-one 
to the corresponding BIM families. These mappings define regions 
of interest (ROI) that support downstream feature selection and 3D 
registration analyses. In dynamic sites, this semantic biasing reduces 
false positives from clutter, ensuring ROIs align with BIM elements for 
accurate deviation detection.

this issue, many methods have been proposed. One of these methods 
is to introduce an additional source of information in the form of 
gravity alignment to promote a more upright final pose to improve 
point cloud alignment. However, the naive integration of bias can 
introduce error by dominating the point cloud registration optimization 
through excessively minimizing the gravity alignment term. This can 
be detrimental to BIM updates and potentially introduce discrepancies. 
This problem is addressed in BIM2RDT via semantic priors, and is 
explained in the following subsection.

3.4.2. Semantic alignment
We use BIM semantic labels to estimate each object’s susceptibility 
to gravity-induced tipping. Heavier objects tend to have a stronger 
upright bias; if they appear tilted, routine site dynamics or workspace 
drift are less likely explanations. This extends prior pose-informed ICP 
(e.g., IMU-ICP [31]) by replacing sensor-derived pose bias with an 
explicit upright vector (e.g., the gravity vector) and by making the 
bias object-adaptive through BIM semantics. This approach helps in 
reducing errors in tilted but stable objects like heavy equipment. We

3.5. Safety-aware digital twin updates with activity integration
Once the robot-captured point cloud is geometrically registered to 
the BIM using the Semantic-Gravity ICP (SG-ICP) method, the BIM2RDT 
framework extends these capabilities to incorporate real-time activ-
ity recognition for enhanced safety monitoring. Here, it semantically 
links sensor-detected activities to BIM entities using IFC standards. 
Specifically, it leverages IfcTask and IfcEvent (core entities in the IFC 
schema for representing identifiable units of work or activities) as a 
unifying interface, allowing sensor-derived activities (e.g., ‘‘hammer 
drilling with unsafe vibration levels’’) to be treated as compatible IFC 
constructs without requiring explicit temporal scheduling ties.
The integration of dynamic site conditions beyond geometry is 
exemplified by the analysis of worker activities involving hazards such 
as excessive hand-arm vibrations (HAV) during tool operation. We 
draw from prior work on wearable sensor-based HAV monitoring [9], 
which demonstrated the feasibility of attaching wearables to positions 
beyond those specified in ISO 5349-1 [5]. In this work, we expand upon 
the existing methodology and develop a subject-specific approach that 
enables more accurate mapping.
The acceleration signals are transmitted to a central processing 
unit, where the frequency-weighted RMS acceleration 𝑎ℎ𝑣 is calculated. 
This data serves as the input for computing A(8), the 8-h energy-
equivalent frequency-weighted vibration total value, as defined in [5]. 
The initiation of the activity is marked by the creation of an IfcEvent. 
Upon attaining the critical exposure action value (EAV) of 2.5 m/s2, a 
triggering mechanism schedules an IfcTask, thereby assigning a safety 
instructor to intervene and mitigate the vibration-induced workload on 
the affected worker. Following the termination of the activity, another
IfcEvent containing the accumulated daily vibration exposure is created, 
enabling the precise tracking and verification of compliance with ISO 
5349-1 limits. On the backend, we enrich the existing IFC file with 
time-stamped HAV events using IfcOpenShell.
In this work, HAV detection serves two roles: (i) a long-horizon 
occupational exposure signal that is logged and traced in BIM/IFC to 
support safety management workflows and (ii) a short-horizon risk 
signal that enriches information used by robot navigation to reduce 
traversal through high-exposure work zones. Sensor-recognized activ-
ities are written into the twin as Activity Instances that can reuse the

project’s schedule vocabulary (labels, scopes) when present, but remain 
valid even when no schedule file, look-ahead, or CPM alignment exists. 
This mapping exploits IFC’s extensible structure, where sensor activi-
ties are instantiated as new or augmented entities with attributes for 
safety metrics (e.g. 𝑎ℎ𝑣 values and risk flags), ensuring interoperability 
without altering the core BIM model.
Work planning is not typically part of baseline BIM/IFC deliveries, 
so representing it using IfcTask and IfcEvent requires explicit mod-
eling preconditions. BIM2RDT therefore treats IfcTask/IfcEvent as an 
optional runtime enrichment layer that can operate in two modes. If 
an IfcWorkSchedule structure already exists (e.g., from 4D planning), 
BIM2RDT appends HAV-related IfcEvent instances and links them to the 
corresponding existing IfcTask. If no work plan is present, BIM2RDT 
creates a minimal process scaffold for traceability only: IfcEvent in-
stances record observed activity start/end and threshold exceedances, 
and a minimal IfcTask encodes a recommended intervention (e.g., ‘‘Stop 
worker from HAV exposure’’). This schedule-agnostic mode is not in-
tended to reconstruct project scheduling, yet it provides a consistent, 
queryable record of safety-relevant observations.
To support actionable alarms and queries, each event must be 
grounded in a work context. Specifically, the spatial grounding of 
each IfcEvent is achieved through the BIM object that represents the 
vibration source. When the HAV monitor identifies a threshold ex-
ceedance, the system uses the LLM-based semantic mapping (Section 
4.7.1) to associate the event with the most likely source object in the 
IFC model. The IfcLocalPlacement of that object provides the geographic 
reference for the event, and the subsequent IfcTask references both the 
affected worker (identified by sensor assignment) and the spatial zone 
defined by the object’s placement. Real-time alarms are triggered by 
the HAV threshold logic and routed through a web-hosted dashboard, 
where the event location, worker identity, vibration magnitude, and 
recommended intervention are displayed together.
3.6. Activity-proximity cost map
In our method, HAV is associated with a workstation location rather 
than continuous worker tracking. The detection of HAV is assigned to a 
BIM object representing the vibrating source. The spatial placement of 
this BIM object provides the event location. SG-ICP provides an accu-
rate geometric map, and the HAV monitor augments it with contextual 
activity layers. When a high HAV activity is recognized, the LLM agent 
infers the most likely activity family and instantiates a local cost map 
around that object. For a grid cell at (𝑥, 𝑦) and an object reference point 
𝑜 = (𝑥𝑜, 𝑦𝑜), the activity cost decays with distance from 𝑜 as: 
𝑓 (𝑥, 𝑦) = 𝐴 𝑒−𝑑(𝑥,𝑦) (11)
where 𝐴 > 0 is a design parameter (the peak cost at the object) and 
𝑑(𝑥, 𝑦) ≥ 0 is the distance from (𝑥, 𝑦) to 𝑜. Unless noted otherwise, we 
take

but any other distance metric would be valid.
While this produces a costmap that can both qualitatively and 
quantitatively be beneficial for project managers to measure the effects 
and probabilistic areas of HAV activity, it can also be included in robot 
mission planning. Previous works have shown that natural language 
sentiment analysis can directly be used in classical robot path plan-
ning [16]. We later on demonstrated the efficacy of using BIM2RDT in 
path planning scenarios.
3.7. Automated digital twin synchronization via IFC updates
Once SG-ICP computes object transformations in robot coordinates, 
these updates must be written back to the BIM to synchronize the 
digital twin with observed site conditions. This process involves two 
stages: semantic object mapping and coordinate system transformation.
3.7.1. Semantic object mapping
After SG-ICP computes alignment transformations, the system must 
map detected objects to their corresponding IFC elements. YOLOE 
(Section 3.1) outputs object labels. IFC elements are referenced by 
globally unique identifiers (GUIDs). An LLM bridges this semantic gap 
by mapping YOLOE-detected object names to IFC element GUIDs using 
reasoning over object types and descriptions. The following prompt is 
used:
‘‘You are tasked with mapping detected construction site objects to 
IFC building elements based on their names and types.
Detected Objects (from YOLOE):
- Wooden Crate (hierarchy: Environment/Props/WoodenCrate) - ...
IFC Objects (from BIM):
- Crate_Storage_01 (GUID: 2a3b4c5d..., Type: IfcFurniture) - ...
Rules: 1. Only map objects where you are confident there is a match 
2. Use exact names and GUIDs as provided 3. Consider semantic 
similarity 4. If uncertain, set confidence to ‘low’ and explain why 5. 
Return valid JSON’’
The LLM returns structured JSON mappings with confidence scores 
(‘‘high’’, ‘‘medium’’, ‘‘low’’) and semantic reasoning for each match. A 
rule-based fallback provides limited pattern matching when API access 
is unavailable, however, this fallback only succeeds for highly standard-
ized naming and cannot handle the semantic variability common in 
construction sites.
3.7.2. Coordinate system transformation
SG-ICP outputs are converted from robot coordinate frames to IFC 
coordinate conventions and applied to update placements of the LLM-
mapped objects in the IFC model. The rigid transformation resulting 
from the SG-ICP registration represents the alignment of the physical 
object relative to the digital model. To synchronize the Digital Twin, 
this transformation is first converted from the robot’s sensing coordi-
nate frame to the IFC project’s coordinate convention (addressing axis 
orientation and unit scaling differences). We utilize the IfcOpenShell
library to programmatically access and modify the IFC schema. Us-
ing the unique element GUID identified in Section 3.7.1, the system 
targets the specific IfcProduct and updates its IfcLocalPlacement. The 
computed translation and rotation matrices are applied to the object’s
IfcAxis2Placement3D attributes, ensuring that the geometric state of the 
digital asset is persistently updated to match the physical site conditions 
observed by the robot.

minimize the objective function. Since RANSAC is used commonly as a

we can still incorporate the gravity residual inside the optimization. 
This emphasizes the plug-and-play feature of our approach.

5. Discussion
In this section, the obtained results are discussed from both theory 
and practice standpoints.
5.1. Implications of geometric alignment results
The experimental outcomes for SG-ICP highlight its superior per-
formance in aligning robot-captured meshes with BIM models, as ev-
idenced by the substantial reductions in Hausdorff distances across 
diverse viewpoints. Notably, the high improvements in the first three 
scenarios demonstrate how semantic-gravity integration addresses com-
mon ICP pitfalls, such as sensitivity to initial poses in environments 
with ambiguous features, analogous to construction sites with often 
occluded elements like scaffolding or equipment stacks.
This robustness enhances the reliability of digital twins for as-built 
verification, potentially minimizing costly rework by enabling early 
detection of geometric deviations. There can be occurrences where 
ICP does better than the gravity-aligned approaches, such as the last 
scenario. This is expected since the gravity alignment introduces differ-
ent rotations and translations, since it can sacrifice the optimal point 
cloud transformation for a more upright pose. If the correspondences 
are of high quality, this gravity bias introduces higher errors in the 
optimization of the true pose. However, this upright bias serves as a 
compass in noisy environments where the correspondences are poor, 
which can guide more realistic orientations as opposed to the best pose 
under poor pointclouds.
Furthermore, the mixed magnitude of improvements across scenar-
ios with static scaling argues for adaptive priors. Specifically, schedule 
and scene context (when available) or learned scene cues can modulate 
the semantic-gravity weight, preserving the strong performance in dif-
ficult cases without over-constraining easy ones. This aligns with our 
methodology goal of LLM-mediated parametrization, ‘‘common sense’’ 
informs the bias where helpful and abstains where unnecessary.
5.2. Practical implementation of BIM2RDT in construction settings
The successful deployment of BIM2RDT within active construction 
projects requires careful consideration of how this methodology inte-
grates with established project delivery processes, existing technology 
infrastructure, and field operations management. Construction practi-
tioners face unique challenges when implementing advanced digital 
twin technologies that extend beyond pure technical functionality to 
encompass organizational change management, workflow coordina-
tion, and economic feasibility within competitive project environments.
In the preconstruction phases, the methodology shifts BIM coordi-
nation from purely geometric clash detection to semantic planning. 
Coordinators and safety managers must collaborate to identify BIM 
families requiring high-fidelity tracking and map specific safety risks 
(e.g., HAV) to these objects. This integrates safety planning directly 
into the digital model rather than keeping it in siloed documents. 
The construction phase implementation requires coordination between 
field superintendents, safety supervisors, and technology coordinators 
to manage the daily operations of both robotic scanning activities and 
worker-worn sensor monitoring. Superintendents need clear protocols 
for scheduling robot deployment activities that complement rather than 
interfere with active construction operations.
BIM2RDT transforms robot missions from simple documentation 
exercises into intelligent, context-aware inspection protocols. Tradi-
tional robotic scanning follows pre-programmed paths that treat the 
site as unknown territory, requiring exhaustive coverage to capture 
all conditions. In contrast, BIM2RDT-equipped UGVs leverage semantic 
expectations from the BIM to focus scanning resources on high-value 
targets—objects likely to have deviated from design, temporary ele-
ments requiring verification, or zones with active safety-critical work. 
In other words, since perception is BIM-conditioned (target families 
and ROIs) and alignment is stabilized by object-adaptive priors, robot 
missions can shift from broad, exhaustive scanning towards targeted 
and repeatable verification passes.
For project managers and safety personnel, the primary decision 
impact is the ability to move from reactive interpretation of raw pho-
tos/point clouds towards actionable, location-grounded signals. Current 
practice separates safety monitoring (manual observations, incident

6. Conclusion
This paper introduced BIM2RDT, a context-aware, AI-powered
framework that transforms static Building Information Models into 
dynamic, robot-ready digital twins with an emphasis on safety. By 
integrating BIM data, robot-collected point clouds, and IoT sensor 
streams for activity monitoring, the framework provides a robust 
solution for real-time site understanding. The proposed Semantic-
Gravity ICP (SG-ICP) algorithm, which leverages LLM-based reasoning 
to inform physical priors, was shown to significantly improve point 
cloud registration accuracy, achieving RMSE reductions of up to 97% 
over standard methods and ensuring physically plausible alignments. 
Furthermore, the integration of HAV monitoring demonstrated the 
framework’s capability to map safety-critical events to the digital twin, 
supporting safety monitoring and risk-aware planning.
Despite its contributions, the work has limitations. First, the ex-
periments were conducted in controlled settings, and the framework’s 
scalability to large, highly dynamic construction sites with diverse and 
unanticipated objects remains to be validated. This includes practical 
sources of variability that were not captured in the proof-of-concept 
evaluation (e.g., severe occlusions, dust/lighting changes, frequent re-
configuration of temporary works, and intermittent connectivity that 
can affect data transfer and update frequency). Furthermore, the stress 
testing of the open-vocabulary detection needs to be further verified. 
Given that BIMs have unique nomenclature between jobsites, an accu-
rate mapping between them all is essential to the framework’s success. 
The HAV monitoring demonstration uses pre-recorded vibration signals 
replayed in simulation rather than data collected during actual tool 
operation on site. Also, the framework’s dependence on high-quality 
BIM models as a priori maps may limit applicability in projects with

Second, our current ‘‘agentic’’ scope is intentionally bounded for 
two bounded reasoning tasks: assigning BIM-family upright priors for 
SG-ICP and mapping detected labels to IFC GUIDs for synchronization. 
The system employs LLMs for semantic reasoning and orchestrates 
multi-step perception-to-update workflows, but it does not exhibit au-
tonomous long-horizon planning, goal decomposition, or adaptive re-
planning in response to environmental feedback—capabilities typically 
associated with fully agentic systems. Thus, with proper referencing 
for adopting the perspective that Agentic AI is characterized by iter-
ative reasoning and tool use rather than zero-shot execution, ‘‘agen-
tic’’ here describes the system’s use of LLM-based semantic reasoning 
to condition perception and updates, rather than claiming full agent 
autonomy.
Third, the safety component is demonstrated using HAV as an 
ISO-standardized, measurable signal, but it is not intended to repre-
sent comprehensive site safety coverage; broader hazard categories 
(e.g., proximity risks, fall hazards, dynamic exclusion zones) and lon-
gitudinal validation of intervention effectiveness remain future work. 
Extending this to multi-worker, multi-hazard scenarios (e.g., simul-
taneous HAV, heat stress, fall risk, noise exposure across dozens of 
workers) introduces sensor fusion challenges, communication band-
width requirements, and computational complexity not addressed in 
the current work.
