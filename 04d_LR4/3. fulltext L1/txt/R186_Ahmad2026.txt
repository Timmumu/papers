Abstract—Proactive safety systems aim to mitigate risks by
anticipating future potential conﬂicts between vehicles and
enabling early intervention to prevent work-zone-related crashes.
This study presents an infrastructure-enabled proactive work
zone safety warning system that leverages a digital twin (DT)
environment, integrating real-time multisensor data, detailed
high-deﬁnition (HD) maps, and a trajectory prediction model
based on a historical-prediction attention mechanism (AM).
Speciﬁcally, the contributions of this study are in twofolds:
1) developing an infrastructure-enabled vehicle trajectory pre-
diction framework that leverages the historical prediction
network (HPNet) model integrated with Lanelet2 HD maps and
2) developing a proactive safety warning generation application
that incorporates probabilistic conﬂict modeling and vehicle
bounding-box representations. The development and eﬀectiveness
demonstration of the presented trajectory prediction model and
proactive warning system is conducted using a cosimulation
environment integrating simulation of urban mobility (SUMO)
and CAR learning to act (CARLA) simulators. To evaluate the
accuracy of predicted trajectories (PTs), we use two standard
metrics: joint average displacement error (ADE) and joint
ﬁnal displacement error (FDE). Our analyses revealed that
the infrastructure-enabled HPNet model demonstrates superior
performance on the work zone datasets generated from the
cosimulation environment, achieving a minimum joint FDE of
0.3228 m and a minimum joint ADE of 0.1327 m, lower
than the benchmarks on the Argoverse (minimumJoint FDE:
1.0986 m, minimumJoint ADE: 0.7612 m) and INTERACTION
(minimumJoint FDE: 0.8231 m, minimumJoint ADE: 0.2548 m)
datasets. In addition, our proactive safety warning generation
application demonstrates its ability to issue alerts for potential
vehicle conﬂicts.

I. I NTRODUCTION
R
OADW AY safety remains a critical issue, particularly
within work zones where vehicle dynamics and driver
behavior signiﬁcantly vary due to altered roadway conﬁgura-
tions and proximity to workers. Work zones pose unique risks
as drivers navigate narrowed lanes, altered traﬃc patterns, and
speed limit changes, often in close proximity to roadway work-
ers. Work-zone-related crashes have consistently presented
higher fatality rates compared with other roadway segments
[1]. A 2022 Bureau of Transportation Statistics report shows
a 14-year-high crash record of 856 fatal crashes in work zones
[2]. A 2022 nationwide study on highway work zone safety
by the Associated General Contractors of America (AGC)
found that two-thirds of highway contractors have encountered
work zone crashes. In addition, over one-third (38%) reported
project delays due to crashes, and 5% reported worker fatalities
[3]. To address these persistent challenges, proactive safety
strategies have emerged as a promising solution. A proactive
system that anticipates vehicle movements can be a potent tool
for reducing the likelihood of collisions and enhancing overall
safety.
Unlike reactive approaches that respond after an incident,
proactive methods leverage sensor technologies, real-time data,
and predictive analytics to anticipate and mitigate risks before
they escalate. To develop such a proactive safety application,
digital twin (DT) technologies oﬀer a necessary framework. A
DT is a real-time, synchronized virtual representation of assets
and systems in the real world [4]. The ﬂow of information
can occur in both the directions, from physical to virtual and
from virtual to physical, in a DT system. In the context of
transportation, such a system is referred to as a transportation
DT (TDT), where the physical components or processes of the
transportation system have a real-time, synchronized, virtual
representation. Among the many types of TDT, predictive DT
(PDT) is a class of systems that go beyond representing the
real-time state of a physical object; it uses complex modeling
to simulate possible future states of the objects [5], [6].
In this study, we leverage a PDT framework to develop a
proactive safety system tailored to complex, dynamic work

II. R ELATED WORK
This section is divided into twofolds: 1) infrastructure-based
vehicle trajectory prediction and 2) proactive safety warning
generation and risk estimation.
A. Infrastructure-Based Vehicle Trajectory Prediction
Vehicle trajectory prediction is imperative for many safety-
critical applications. The premise of the trajectory prediction
problem is that given past motion states of a vehicle, estimate
the future positions of that vehicle. These motion states typi-
cally include kinematic variables, such as position, velocity,
and acceleration. The prediction algorithms may also use
other relevant information, such as historical driving behavior
or a detailed map of road geometry. Recent studies have
highlighted that infrastructure-based trajectory prediction is
tightly coupled with the quality of upstream vehicle track-
ing and trajectory reconstruction [8], [9]. For large-scale
roadside deployments, raw trajectories are often fragmented
by sensor noise, occlusions, and handovers between sensing
regions. Recently, Liu et al. [8] proposed a multiaccess edge
computing (MEC)-based framework that performs real-time
trajectory calibration, cross-sensor fusion, and short-term tra-
jectory prediction. Short-term prediction typically refers to
3–6 s of prediction horizon in the context of vehicles [10].
This framework demonstrates the scalability and feasibility of
infrastructure-based tracking and short-term prediction.
There are several diﬀerent approaches to predict vehicle
trajectories, which can be broadly classiﬁed into data-driven
approaches [11] using machine learning and deep learning,
probabilistic models [12], Kalman ﬁlters and Bayesian esti-
mators, and physics-based kinematic models [13]. In this
study, we refer to the kinematic and probabilistic models as
traditional approaches to trajectory prediction.
Traditional approaches typically overlook intervehicle inter-
actions that inﬂuence driver behavior [14]. Another important
factor guiding the vehicle’s trajectory is the road geometry.
The geometry of the road inﬂuences drivers as they negotiate
roadway curves and turns. Considering the road geometry
and how vehicles interact with the road shape and topology
provides important cues for the prediction model. A few
traditional approaches consider road geometry as boundary
conditions; however, they lack semantic understanding of a
roadway network [15].
Early data-driven methods used recurrent neural networks
(RNNs), such as long short-term memories (LSTMs), to model
trajectories [16], leveraging their strength in modeling tempo-
ral dependencies. Temporal convolutional networks (TCNs),
an architecture that adapts convolutional neural networks
(CNNs) for sequential data and integration with an AM [17],
have shown improvements in trajectory prediction [18]. An
AM in neural networks allows the model to focus on the
most relevant parts of the input rather than treating all parts
equally. The ability to focus on relevant parts of the input
allows neural networks to achieve “contextual understanding,”
which has led to their use and integration with a wide range of
deep-learning-based models [19].
In addition to sequential or temporal modeling, recent
data-driven deep learning approaches often incorporate map
information to improve understanding of road geometry and
provide useful contextual cues. Two diﬀerent approaches
that include map information in trajectory prediction models
are: 1) rasterized representations of roadway sections and
2) detailed vectorized maps that encode lane topology as
a graph structure. Early eﬀorts in this area used CNNs on
rasterized roadway images, with vehicle trajectories encoded
in the image. These methods demonstrated improved pre-
diction accuracy compared with models that rely solely on
sequential trajectory data [20]. More recent approaches lever-
age HD vector maps [21] that provide rich geometric and
topological information, such as lane boundaries, connections,
and traﬃc rules. These vector maps are typically encoded
as graph structures, where lane segments are nodes, and
their connections, including merging, branching, and priority
relationships, are edges. Graph structure makes it compelling
to apply graph neural networks (GNNs) and graph convolu-
tional networks (GCNs) to trajectory prediction. The trajectory
prediction model LaneGCN, introduced by Liang et al. [22],

Studies such as VectorNet [23], QCNet [24], and HiVT [25]

context in state-of-the-art trajectory prediction models, most
existing infrastructure-based prediction studies continue to rely
primarily on temporal modeling formulations. Representative
approaches include recurrent or hybrid temporal models for
roadside multiagent forecasting [29], [30], transfer-learning-
based predictors adapted for roadside deployment [31], and
transformer-based models that emphasize interaction modeling
without explicit lane-level map encoding [32]. As a result,
the rich map-centric representations that have driven recent
advances in trajectory prediction remain comparatively under-
explored in current infrastructure-based prediction pipelines.
Most trajectory prediction benchmark datasets primarily
target self-driving vehicle applications, where a vehicle must
anticipate the movements of surrounding vehicles to support its
path-planning module and safety. This incentivizes the devel-
opment of sophisticated trajectory prediction models, which
are predominantly evaluated in self-driving contexts. It is also
important to distinguish trajectory prediction from trajectory
planning, as these two problems are often conﬂated in the liter-
ature. Trajectory planning methods aim to generate a feasible
and optimal future path for an ego vehicle under dynamic
conditions and safety constraints, assuming known intent
and controllability. For example, the road obstacle-involved
trajectory planner (ROITP) explicitly incorporates road obsta-
cles, vehicle rollover dynamics, and stability constraints
to generate safe trajectories for autonomous trucks using
optimization-based formulations [33]. While such planning-
oriented approaches are critical for autonomous driving, they
diﬀer fundamentally from trajectory prediction, which seeks
to infer the future motion of surrounding vehicles whose
intentions and control inputs are unobserved.
Recently, HPNet [7] ranked ﬁrst on the INTERACTION
dataset in the trajectory prediction task, due to its novel
triple-factorized attention and the use of historical predictions
to improve temporal consistency. However, the advances in
prediction model architectures are also applicable to other
domains, particularly roadside infrastructure-based trajectory
prediction for safety applications. A key strength of HPNet’s
multimodal output is its ability to capture the inherent vari-
ability in human driving behavior. Human drivers exercise free
will, often making subtle, discretionary choices that result
in slightly diﬀerent yet equally plausible trajectories. For
instance, a driver may choose to navigate a work zone by
slightly adjusting their path or speed, with each choice having
a comparable likelihood. Chen et al. [34] point out the variabil-
ity introduced by human behavior and diﬀerent driving styles
in decision-making environments. HPNet’s multimodal predic-
tions reﬂect this by generating multiple trajectory hypotheses,
each adhering to physical constraints and map context, such
as lane boundaries and temporary signage. These trajectories,
while distinct, show high agreement due to the constrained
nature of work zone environments, where variability is subtle
and bounded by roadway geometry and traﬃc rules. This bal-
ance allows HPNet to model the nuanced decision-making of
human drivers while maintaining temporal coherence and spa-
tial consistency across predictions. The primary contribution
of this study is to demonstrate the feasibility and eﬀectiveness
of using HPNet for infrastructure-based trajectory prediction
in a roadway work zone setting.
B. Risk Estimation and Proactive Warning Generation
Accurate estimation of vehicle conﬂict risk and its trans-
lation into eﬀective warnings remain central challenges in
proactive transportation safety systems. To this end, recent
research has increasingly adopted probabilistic modeling
frameworks to address the uncertainties inherent in vehicle tra-
jectories, driver behaviors, and dynamic traﬃc contexts such as
highway work zones. A foundational principle in modern risk
estimation is trajectory-level probabilistic modeling. Gaussian
processes and hidden Markov models have been combined to
predict vehicle motion under uncertain conditions, producing
collision risk estimates that generalize beyond ﬁxed assump-
tions of linearity or constant speed [35]. Similar probabilistic
approaches have been applied in pedestrian-vehicle contexts,
where Gaussian process regression and maneuver likelihoods
allow for granular conﬂict quantiﬁcation with lower computa-
tional costs than deep learning models [36]. These frameworks
outperform traditional surrogate safety measures (SSMs), such
as time-to-collision (TTC), which often rely on rigid motion
assumptions.
Although postencroachment time (PET) and TTC remain
fundamental to SSM approaches, many researchers have
enhanced their predictive capability by integrating these indi-
cators within probabilistic modeling frameworks that better
capture traﬃc’s inherent randomness. Among these, extreme
value theory (EVT) has emerged as a powerful tool for
quantifying rare but critical safety events [37], [38], [39],
[40], [41], [42], [43]. Alozi and Hussein [44] analyzed large-
scale A V-pedestrian interaction datasets from platforms, such
as Motional and Lyft, using PET and TTC to derive risk
proﬁles. Their ﬁndings indicated an elevated risk of collisions
in the early stages of autonomous vehicle deployment, with
an estimated 4–5.5 collisions per million vehicle-kilometers
traveled (VKT). When contextual variables were added, this
range narrowed to 2.3–3.7 per million VKT, oﬀering greater
statistical conﬁdence. Their results underscore how EVT-based
probabilistic models can accommodate behavioral variability
and provide scalable risk metrics in rapidly evolving trans-
portation systems. A recent framework integrates backward
and stochastic forward reachable sets to distinguish guar-
anteed safe states from probabilistically risky ones, thereby
enhancing both safety guarantees and precision in dynamic
conﬂict identiﬁcation [45]. Such hybrid techniques help miti-
gate overconservatism in earlier reachability analyses, while
supporting conﬁdence-aware risk predictions essential for
real-world deployment. On the other hand, Jiao et al. [38]

III. PDT F RAMEWORK
Fig. 1 presents a PDT framework for proactive work zone
safety using roadside sensor data, which comprises three
layers: 1) physical world; 2) PDT; and 3) communication
gateway. The physical world serves as the bottom layer and
encompasses all the transportation-related physical objects,
such as vehicles, work zone road segments, workers, and
roadside sensors. The top layer represents the PDT, which
consists of digital shadow, digital sibling, and real-time
proactive safety applications. The top and bottom layers are
connected via the communication gateway, enabling real-time
data exchange between them. Roadside infrastructure can be
equipped with computing devices and sensors (e.g., cameras,
radar, and LiDAR) to precisely detect vehicles and workers
and continuously generate their trajectories within the work
zone roadway segment. PDT layer consists of digital shadow,
digital sibling, and proactive safety applications. In the context
of PDT, a digital shadow is a digital replica of vehicles’
and workers’ trajectories. Digital shadows can be created and
updated in real-time by aggregating, synchronizing, and fusing
data from the physical world. Digital shadow receives sensor
data from the physical world, processes it, and stores the
processed data in the digital sibling database, but it cannot
transmit information back to the physical world.
Digital sibling has four basic components: 1) database;
2) Lanelet2 HD Map; 3) HPNet trajectory prediction model;
and 3) predicted trajectories (PTs) for all vehicles and maneu-
ver scenarios. The prediction model can predict all plausible
vehicle trajectories, each with a diﬀerent probability. Finally,
the proactive safety application module calculates conﬂict
probability for all combinations of PT pairs and uses a
conﬂict decision threshold to generate proactive safety alerts.
Sections III-A–III-C detail the development pipeline, predic-
tion model, and proactive safety application.
A. Integration of Simulation, Prediction, and Warning
Generation
This study presents a comprehensive pipeline for developing
a proactive warning system for work zones within a PDT
framework, integrating scenario design, cosimulation, model
training, and warning generation, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The
process begins with the design stage, where road networks and
traﬃc scenarios are created using tools, such as RoadRunner
[46] and Commonroad Scenario Designer [47]. These generate
OpenDrive and Lanelet2 HD maps, along with SUMO network
and route ﬁles, and support microscopic traﬃc ﬂow models
tailored for realistic simulations. At the cosimulation stage, the
SUMO and CARLA simulators interact to exchange vehicle
and traﬃc-light states, creating a realistic environment for
data collection and testing that mirrors real-world implemen-
tation processes. The prediction model training stage involves
generating trajectory datasets from simulations, preprocessing
them, and training a trajectory prediction model. Finally, the
proactive warning generation stage uses the trained model for
online inference, enabling real-time deployment by predicting
future trajectories and generating proactive warnings using
vehicle data.
B. Trajectory Prediction of All Plausible Future
Maneuverings

[7]. The key contribution of the authors is the development
of temporally consistent trajectory prediction of all plausible
future maneuvers. Many conventional trajectory prediction

temporally incoherent predictions where the sequence of pre-

enables HPNet to dynamically emphasize relevant spatial

The model architecture, as depicted in Fig. 3, processes the
inputs through a series of attention-based layers to generate
and reﬁne multimodal trajectory predictions. The process
begins with learnable mode tokens, representing K possible
future trajectories for each agent, which act as queries in AMs.
Temporal attention uses multihead cross-attention, with mode
tokens as queries and vehicle/relative embeddings (E_a, E_e)
as keys and values, to capture historical vehicle dynamics
over time. Spatial attention uses cross-attention with mode
tokens as queries and map/relative embeddings (E_m, E_e)
to integrate roadway context, aligning predictions with lane
constraints. Mode attention further reﬁnes predictions through
three sublayers: agent attention (P
a) models interagent inter-
actions using self-attention on prediction embeddings (P) and
relative features; historic prediction attention (P hp) ensures
temporal coherence by capturing dependencies within PT;
and mode attention disambiguates multiple plausible trajectory
hypotheses by modeling interactions across modes.
The trajectory prediction process unfolds in two stages:
proposal generation and reﬁnement. In the proposal stage,
an MLP (traj_propose) decodes mode embeddings
(m_embs) into initial multimodal trajectory proposals
(traj_propose) in global coordinates, with shape
[(N1, . . . ,Nb), H, K, F, 2], where N1, . . . ,Nb denote agents
per batch, H is the historical timestep, K is the mode, F is
the future timestep, and 2 represents 2-D coordinates. These
proposals deﬁne anchor points by selecting the middle future
timestep (F//2) to compute anchor positions (n_position)
and headings (n_heading), which are transformed into local
coordinates and encoded into anchor embeddings (n_embs)
using an MLP (proposal_to_anchor). In the reﬁnement
stage, anchor embeddings undergo another temporal
attention stage to integrate historical agent data, a spatial
attention stage to incorporate map context, and a mode
attention stage to reﬁne interactions across agents, historical
steps, and modes. An MLP (traj_refine) generates a
correction term (traj_refine), which is added to the initial
trajectory proposal and transformed back to global coordinates
using transform_traj_to_global_coordinate,
yielding the ﬁnal output (traj_output). This two-stage
approach ensures contextually informed and temporally
consistent predictions, overcoming the limitations of
framewise decoupled models.

IV. SUMO-CARLA E XPERIMENTAL SETUP
In this study, we use the SUMO and CARLA simula-
tors, widely accepted open-source tools for traﬃc, vehicle
dynamics, and sensor simulation. SUMO provides realis-
tic large-scale traﬃc simulation capabilities using standard
microscopic traﬃc ﬂow models and is particularly adept at
simulating detailed merging behavior in constrained envi-
ronments, such as work zones [48]. In this study, CARLA
complements SUMO by providing high-ﬁdelity sensor simu-
lation, including lidar, radar, and camera sensors. We adjusted
the car-following and lane-changing models in SUMO to
closely match observed real-world driver behavior within
work zones. These behaviors include realistic acceleration,
deceleration, and merging maneuvers validated by traﬃc ﬂow
studies [49].
The merging behavior approaching a lane closure is partic-
ularly important in work zone scenarios. By default, SUMO
uses the LC2013 [50] lane-changing model to make a decision
to change lane at every simulation step. This model considers
four diﬀerent motivations to make lane changes, namely: 1)
strategic change; 2) cooperative change; 3) tactical change;
and 4) regulatory change. The lane change due to the lane
closure ahead is a strategic change. The default behavior of this
lane-changing model in SUMO is that lane change happens
in an instant. When the model satisﬁes all the criteria for
changing lanes, the vehicle moves to the target lane in an
instant. Instant lane changes are prohibitive as it is necessary
to capture the lane-changing dynamics for our application.
To better suit our study, we use SUMO’s sublane model,
an extension of the LC2013 lane-changing logic that allows
continuous lateral movement across the width of a lane rather
than abrupt, instantaneous jumps. This sublane model, referred
to as SL2015, provides a more realistic representation of lane-
changing behavior by allowing vehicles to gradually shift
their lateral positions over time. In the sublane model, a
road is conceptually divided into many ﬁne-grained lateral
subdivisions (sublanes), allowing vehicles to occupy fractional
positions within a lane or between lanes. This enables mod-
eling lane changes as a time-dependent process inﬂuenced by
vehicle dynamics rather than as a discrete event. To activate
the sublane model, SUMO requires to be conﬁgured with
the -lateral-resolution <FLOAT> parameter set to
a value smaller than the lane width, which is 0.25 m in our
case. With the sublane model enabled, SUMO switches the
lane-changing model from the default LC2013 to the SL2015
model, which can use lane subdivisions for smoother, more
realistic transitions. Once enabled, the vehicles can initiate
a lane change by steering toward the target lane, and the
movement unfolds gradually over multiple simulation steps.
In addition to LC2013 model parameters, the trajectory and
duration of this lateral transition are governed by additional
vehicle parameters, such as lcSublane, lcAssertive, lcPushy,
lcImpatience, lcTimeToImpatience, lcAccelLat, latAlignment,
and lcMaxSpeedLatStanding. These parameters together shape
how vehicles behave during lane changes. For instance, lcSub-
lane controls the vehicle’s preference for staying aligned
laterally within a lane, while lcAssertive and lcPushy deter-
mine how willing a vehicle is to accept tighter gaps or
initiate lane changes in partially blocked spaces. lcImpatience
dynamically scales the assertive and pushy tendencies as
time progresses, and lcTimeToImpatience measures the time it
takes to reach peak impatience. lcAccelLat restricts the lateral
acceleration to reﬂect realistic dynamics, and latAlignment
allows vehicles to aim for speciﬁc oﬀsets within the lane (e.g.,
center, left, or right). Finally, the parameters lcMaxSpeedLat-
Standing and lcMaxSpeedLatFactor control the lateral speed
envelope, scaling lateral velocity based on vehicle speed to
prevent unrealistic side movements. Together, these parameters
enable nuanced, gradual, and context-aware lateral maneuvers
that are critical for simulating realistic merging behavior in
work zones where vehicles should respond to upstream lane
closures. This modeling enhancement is especially useful
because it allows us to accurately capture the interactions
between merging vehicles and through tra ﬃc, as well as
model behaviors such as hesitation, courtesy yielding, and
multivehicle negotiations near the taper zone of the work
zone. In addition, the continuous, dynamics, and time-evolving
lateral motion representation allows for better integration with
trajectory prediction algorithms, which requires smooth and
physically plausible vehicle motions.

V. D ATA GENERATION
Vehicle trajectories are derived from infrastructure-mounted
LiDAR, radar, and camera sensors using oﬀ-the-shelf detection
and tracking components. Camera-based vehicle detection is
performed using a YOLO-11 object detector, LiDAR point
clouds are processed using standard Euclidean-clustering-
based detection, and radar provides complementary range
and radial velocity measurements. Camera detections are pro-
jected onto the roadway using a planar homography under
a locally planar road assumption, while LiDAR and radar
detections are transformed into a common stationary world
coordinate frame via rigid extrinsic calibration. All the world-
frame coordinates are expressed in a projected metric system
using the universal transverse mercator (UTM) projection to
ensure global-coordinate consistency across sensors, trajecto-
ries, and HD maps. Following spatial alignment, detections
are temporally associated using a SORT-based multiobject
tracking algorithm, and trajectory-level fusion is performed
using an extended Kalman ﬁlter (EKF) to jointly estimate
vehicle position, velocity, and heading over time. The result-
ing fused trajectories are preprocessed to match HPNet’s
input format, including ﬁxed-length historical sequences, agent
attributes, and alignment with the Lanelet2 HD map, enabling
infrastructure-based trajectory prediction. All three sensor
modalities collect data at a frequency of 10 Hz, which facili-
tates high temporal resolution and continuous vehicle tracking.
In our simulation setup, we also collect ground-truth data for
all vehicles at 10 Hz (0.1-s time interval), which we use to
train the HPNet model.
The structure of the INTERACTION dataset [28] served
as a reference when building our dataset from simulation
data. The dataset consists of comma-separated-value (CSV)
ﬁles, each containing a 4-s-long scenario. Since the data
are recorded at 10 Hz, and there are 18–22 vehicles at any
given time in the simulation, each CSV ﬁle has approximately
800 rows of data. Data snapshots recorded at 10 Hz (100-ms
intervals) are referred to as a frame. So, a 4-s scenario has
40 frames, where the ﬁrst ten frames are considered as input
and the subsequent 30 frames are ground-truth labels. A total
of 20 000 s of driving simulation data were generated, which
results in 5000 individual cases, each stored as a separate
CSV ﬁle. These cases collectively represent approximately
5.56 h of continuous simulated driving. The CSV ﬁles are
indexed by “case
id” (e.g., trajectory data case 10.csv).
