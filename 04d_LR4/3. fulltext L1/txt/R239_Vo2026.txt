 A B S T R A C T
Construction remains one of the most hazardous industries, with tower crane operations posing significant 
risks under limited visibility and dynamic site conditions. This paper proposes a calibration-free vision-
based framework for safety-oriented 3D localization using crane-mounted monocular cameras. The D2S-MDE 
framework recovers metric-scale 3D worker positions from a single RGB image by enforcing geometric 
consistency between camera relocalization and monocular depth estimation. Sparse metric depth anchors 
from scene coordinate regression are used to align dense monocular depth, enabling robust metric depth 
reconstruction under dynamic viewpoints. The estimated 3D positions are integrated into a BIM-aligned digital 
twin in NVIDIA Omniverse for real-time safety monitoring and risk assessment. Experiments demonstrate 
decimeter-level camera pose accuracy (0.012 m/0.019◦) in real scenes and achieve a Height-Normalized 
Localization Score of up to 92.67% at 14 m, with risk assessment accuracy reaching 94.19%. This performance 
highlights the framework effectiveness for practical deployment in real construction environments.
1. Introduction
Construction is one of the most hazardous industries, accounting 
for a disproportionate share of occupational injuries and fatalities [1]. 
According to the statistics from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the 
fatal injuries in construction sectors in 2023 were 1075 cases, leading 
to the top fatal rate among industries. Beyond the tragic human losses, 
such accidents also lead to economic burdens. For example, the Occu-
pational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) estimated that the 
workplace injuries cost almost $1 billion per week in 2023 [2]. These 
statistics underscore the critical need for effective safety management 
and proactive hazard monitoring on construction sites.
In the construction areas, the safety of workers working around 
heavy constructions machines, specifically tower crane, is particularly 
critical because these machines have very large sizes and their opera-
tors normally have limited visibility. Therefore, it would be a thorny 
problem for people to avoid potential hazards from these kinds of 
machinery while maintaining the continuous operations, especially 
in a crowded and dynamic environment. As a result, developing an 
advanced monitoring system for real-time visualization and safety risk 
awareness should be taken into account as priorities.

In the past decade, vision-based hazard monitoring has gained 
significant attention due to advances in computer vision. Early studies 
primarily focused on 2D worker and equipment detection for con-
struction safety monitoring using CCTV and crane-view imagery. For 
example, Price et al. [3] developed a multisensor crane monitoring 
system for blind lift operations, while Sutjaritvorakul et al. [4] pro-
posed a data-driven worker detection framework using crane-view 
cameras. Recent studies further addressed challenging construction 
scenarios such as overlapping worker detection [5], long-tailed object 
detection under domain-shifted environments [6], and multiview tra-
jectory analysis for construction-object monitoring [7]. Other studies 
extended safety monitoring to the 3D level. For example, Kulinan 
et al. [8] estimated 3D worker coordinates within a BIM platform, 
while Lee et al. [9] employed stereo vision for multi-worker localization 
and tracking. To support dynamic camera systems, Wang et al. [10] 
combined visual odometry and monocular depth estimation for crane 
monitoring, whereas Fu et al. [11] proposed a 2D–3D matching pipeline 
for crane boom-end localization. Han et al. [12] further integrated 
UAV monitoring with digital twin environments for large-scale worker 
management. In parallel, BIM- and digital-twin-based safety manage-
ment has attracted increasing attention. Tak et al. [13] and Shahnavaz

Despite the remarkable progress in advancing safety monitoring 
technologies, several limitations persist. Firstly, there is fragility and 
computational inefficiency in image-reference-based 2D–2D and 2D–
3D matching. A reference-based method retrieves candidate reference 
frames from the entire video, establishes 2D–2D correspondences, and 
then lifts inliers to 2D–3D using a prebuilt SfM point cloud followed by 
PnP. Construction-site imagery often contains repetitive structural tex-
tures (rebar and scaffolding), environmental disturbances (dust or rain), 
and motion-induced blur, which can degrade the reliability of 2D–2D 
feature matching. Moreover, pipelines that perform 2D–2D match-
ing prior to 2D–3D lifting typically incur substantial computational 
overhead, posing challenges for real-time edge deployment. In this 
paper, we suggest bypass reference-frame retrieval by directly matching 
the 2D query image to a pre-stored 3D point cloud, achieving more 
noise-robust localization with markedly lower computational overhead. 
Beyond matching-based localization, recovering reliable depth infor-
mation from monocular cameras remains another challenge. Existing 
monocular depth estimation methods often depend on externally pro-
vided camera poses and heuristic pixel filtering strategies, which can 
lead to inaccurate depth prediction and scale ambiguity. As a conse-
quence, object-level 3D localization becomes unstable when 2D detec-
tions are projected using mismatched scaled depth values. Although 
sparse 3D descriptors are sometimes reconstructed in these pipelines, 
they are rarely exploited as geometric constraints to regularize dense 
depth estimation. In addition, most existing 3D localization paradigms 
are built upon multi-view or stereo vision assumptions, where depth 
is inferred from synchronized cameras or temporally adjacent frames. 
While effective for static surveillance systems, these assumptions break 
down in crane-mounted or mobile camera scenarios, where fixed stereo 
baselines are unavailable and temporal multi-view reconstruction in-
troduces latency and instability due to insufficient viewpoint variation 
between consecutive frames. In parallel, existing multi-object 3D safety 
monitoring systems typically visualize workers, machinery, and safety 
zones independently, without explicitly modeling their spatial inter-
actions within a unified environment. Furthermore, although digital 
twin technologies have been widely applied in civil engineering ap-
plications such as Structural Health Monitoring (SHM) [16,17], few 
studies integrate real-time 3D localization with interactive digital twin 
environments capable of rule-based hazard evaluation and spatial risk 
visualization.
As a result, 3D localization for dynamic construction cameras must 
be reformulated as a per-frame inference problem that jointly reasons 
about camera pose and scene depth from a single RGB image. However, 
this reformulation introduces two fundamental challenges: (i) camera 
relocalization must be lightweight and robust without relying on expen-
sive point-cloud-based matching pipelines, and (ii) depth information 
must be inferred in metric scale despite the inherent ambiguity of 
monocular depth estimation. Addressing these challenges is essen-
tial for enabling real-time, calibration-free 3D safety monitoring with 
dynamically mounted cameras.
Unlike conventional multi-view or calibration-based pipelines,
crane-mounted safety monitoring requires per-frame metric 3D local-
ization under continuously changing viewpoints. In this setting, neither 
fixed stereo geometry nor repeated manual calibration is practical. To 
address this challenge, we formulate crane-mounted safety monitoring 
as a geometric consistency problem between scene coordinate regres-
sion and monocular depth estimation. Specifically, scene coordinate 
regression provides sparse metric geometric anchors through camera 
relocalization, whereas monocular depth estimation provides dense but 
scale-ambiguous depth predictions. By reconciling these two repre-
sentations at inference time, the proposed framework enables metric 
3D localization from a single moving crane-mounted camera without 
explicit camera calibration. We propose a calibration-free deep-learning 
framework that enables 3D localization from a single crane-mounted 
RGB camera by enforcing coordinate consistency at inference time, 
as summarized in Table 1. The key contributions of this study are 
summarized as follows:
• We introduce a calibration-free vision framework for crane-
mounted cameras that enables metric 3D worker localization un-
der dynamically changing viewpoints without requiring manual 
camera calibration or additional sensing hardware.
• We propose a scale-consistent depth recovery strategy that rec-
onciles dense monocular depth predictions with sparse metric 
depth anchors derived from camera relocalization. This approach 
provides a practical mechanism to recover metric-scale depth 
under dynamic camera conditions commonly encountered on con-
struction sites.
• We validate the proposed framework through experiments on 
real construction site data and demonstrate its integration with 
a digital-twin environment for rule-based safety monitoring and 
hazard evaluation.
2. Related work
This section reviews previous studies on camera relocalization, 
3D vision-based localization, and digital-twin-based safety monitoring 
in construction environments. Existing approaches and their limita-
tions are discussed to position the proposed framework within current 
research trends.
2.1. Camera relocalization on safety monitoring
Camera relocalization is the task of estimating a camera’s 6-DoF 
pose from a single RGB image and can generally be categorized into 
three groups. The first category is image-retrieval-based localization, 
where query images are matched against geo-referenced image data-
bases using global descriptors or place-recognition methods [19,20]. 
Subsequent studies improved retrieval robustness using synthesized 
views and learned global representations [21,22]. The retrieved ref-
erence images are then used to approximate the camera pose [23]. 
Although this approach is computationally efficient, it depends heavily 
on dense reference-image coverage and often struggles under unseen 
viewpoints or environmental changes. Structure-based approaches in-
stead estimate camera poses through explicit 2D–3D correspondences 
between query images and reconstructed scene geometry. Representa-
tive methods include feature metric localization [24], pixel-level pose 
refinement [25], hierarchical localization [26], and graph-based fea-
ture matching frameworks such as SuperGlue and SuperPoint [27,28]. 
These methods generally achieve high localization accuracy by lever-
aging geometric constraints, but they require dense 3D maps and com-
putationally expensive feature matching during inference. To address 
these limitations, end-to-end deep learning methods based on Scene 
Coordinate Regression (SCR) have been introduced. Early SCR ap-
proaches employed lightweight regression networks to directly predict 
scene coordinates from RGB images [29,30], while more recent studies 
improved robustness and scalability for large-scale environments [31,
32]. Since SCR-based methods estimate camera poses directly from 
predicted scene coordinates without explicit image retrieval or dense 
point-cloud matching, they provide a lightweight and practical solution 
for dynamic-camera scenarios in hazard monitoring.
Camera relocalization has been widely applied in robotics and au-
tonomous vehicles for navigation. Recognizing its potential, researchers 
have also explored its application in heavy construction equipment, 
particularly tower cranes. One early approach is proposed by Jing 
et al. [33], who align live camera frames with a point cloud map 
to approximate the crane camera pose. They generate a wide-angle 
synthetic reference image from the point cloud and employ 2D feature

boom tip through geometric alignment. However, localization errors 
may increase when query images differ significantly from the reference 
set, resulting in insufficient feature correspondences between images 
and the sparse point cloud. As a result, the method requires highly 
overlapping images in the dataset to ensure full scene coverage and 
reliable localization.
2.2. 3D vision-based localization in safety monitoring
Compared with 2D visual detection, the use of 3D spatial infor-
mation is widely acknowledged to better represent the interactions 
between workers and machinery on construction sites. The most com-
mon approaches introduce spatial localization frameworks based on 
stereo vision systems, among which Lee et al. [9], Cai et al. [34], and 
Zhang et al. [35] are representative examples. Generally, the method 
relies on the intersection view of at least two cameras simultaneously 
and includes four steps: camera calibration to estimate intrinsic and ex-
trinsic parameters, 2D object localization to obtain image coordinates, 
object matching where visual information is associated across images 
based on epipolar geometry constraints, and triangulation which inte-
grates the previous results to recover 3D coordinates of target entities. 
Despite their effectiveness, these methods still require significant man-
ual calibration effort and accurate multi-view data association, which 
remains challenging in complex construction environments. In addi-
tion, existing stereo camera systems primarily focus on ground-level 
hazards rather than overhead risks, making them less suitable for crane 
operations.
To address this gap, the use of single camera, in collaboration 
with other sensors and AI-driven techniques, is a potential solution to 
estimate 3D entity coordinates from dynamic cameras. For instance, 
Zhang et al. [36] used a single monocular camera and geometry 
feeded from drone. After estimating the drone camera poses by Semi-
Direct Visual Odometry, they combine adjacent frames with Multi-View 
Stereo method to generate depth map to ground plane, enabling back-
projection of 2D detections into 3D coordinates based on known camera 
height and ground geometry. Meanwhile, Shen et al. [37] propose 
a direct approach, namely the Self-supervised Monocular Depth Es-
timation Network (SNDEU), which predicts both depth maps and 
unknown camera intrinsics from raw video sequences. The detected 2D 
results, including bounding boxes and instance segmentation masks, are 
projected into 3D space to form a pseudo-LiDAR point cloud, which 
is subsequently filtered using a density-based clustering algorithm to 
extract object-level 3D coordinates. Another method introduced by Han 
et al. [12] who links 2D detection results across UAV-captured video 
frames using multi-object tracking and maps them onto a coarse 3D 
photogrammetry model using UAV GPS data. However, unlike UAV 
systems equipped with accurate GPS, crane-mounted cameras lack 
predefined camera parameters, and their motion is often non-ideal, 
as intermittent stops during operation can negatively affect localiza-
tion performance. Although depth estimation has shown promise for 
addressing these challenges [38], monocular depth predictions often 
suffer from scale ambiguity [39], particularly at long distances. There-
fore, by incorporating sparse scene descriptors obtained from Scene 
Coordinate Regression (SCR), the proposed framework has the potential 
to overcome these limitations and provide more robust 3D localization 
in dynamic construction environments.

2.3. Digital twin-based hazards visualization
Digital Twins (DTs) are increasingly recognized as a bridge that 
integrates real-time data from sensors and other sources with man-
agement systems, enabling dynamic monitoring, risk assessment, and 
decision-making support. In construction safety management, DTs can 
be combined with computer vision and Building Information Modeling 
(BIM) to digitize risk assessment, facilitating real-time monitoring of 
workers’ locations and safety compliance within a virtual environ-
ment [8]. Moreover, DTs provide an effective means not only for 
visualizing site operations but also for supporting task assignment and 
resource allocation [12,40].
DTs have significant potential to improve crane operation safety 
due to their ability to provide diverse types of data. Although it had 
been applied in certain cases, for example as analysis stability of tower 
crane hoisting, the most powerful feature is to provide the interaction 
throughout entities has not been properly utilized. To address this 
gap, we extend these concepts by integrating camera relocalization 
and monocular depth estimation outputs into a digital twin workspace 
developed in NVIDIA Omniverse, which has been shown effectively 
in progress monitoring [41] but in safety management. By mapping 
relocalized camera poses and depth-derived 3D coordinates of detected 
entities onto a virtual site model, the DT enables real-time visualiza-
tion of spatial relationships among the crane boom, site workers, and 
hazardous zones. This spatial context is critical for assessing potential 
collision risks.
3. Methodology
This study proposes a vision-based framework for estimating the 
3D positions of workers operating around crane zones by integrating 
camera relocalization and monocular depth estimation, as illustrated 
in Fig. 1. First, a top-down query image (a) is provided as input to the 
D2S camera relocalization module (b), which utilizes a graph attention 
network with scene coordinate regression to estimate the camera pose. 
Sparse scene descriptors obtained from this module are reprojected 
into the image plane to generate sparse depth anchors. In parallel, 
the same image is processed by a vision transformer-based monocular 
depth estimation module (c) to produce a dense relative depth map. 
The sparse depth anchors are then used to perform geometric scale 
reconciliation (d), aligning the relative depth map with metric scale 
to obtain a metric depth map. Subsequently, objects of interest are 
detected using the real-time object detection model RTMDet (e), which 
extracts 2D bounding boxes of workers in the image. By retrieving 
depth values from the metric depth map and applying back-projection 
using camera intrinsic parameters, the corresponding 3D positions 
of the detected entities are computed (g). Finally, the estimated 3D 
positions are spatially registered with the preloaded BIM model within 
the NVIDIA Omniverse environment (f). This enables digital twin-based 
visualization and real-time safety assessment for crane operations (h).
3.1. D2S-based camera relocalization
This section describes the proposed D2S-based camera relocalization 
framework for estimating crane-mounted camera poses from monocular 
images. The overall process includes data collection, scene coordi-
nate regression training, and camera pose estimation using 2D–3D 
correspondences.
3.1.1. Data collection
To estimate new camera poses in a specific environment, a scene 
coordinate regression model requires two types of input data for train-
ing. First, 2D images with known camera intrinsic parameters and a 
sparse reconstruction point cloud are required. Top-down view images

3.1.2. D2S training
Extrinsic parameters play a crucial role because they provide in-
formation about the camera position (𝑥𝑐𝑎𝑚, 𝑦𝑐𝑎𝑚, 𝑧𝑐𝑎𝑚 ) and orientation

spatial layout of 3D entities. In practice, the orientation is represented 
by quaternions, which are later converted to rotation matrices for 
coordinate transformation, as described in Section 3.2.3. To determine 
6-DoF camera poses from crane-mounted images, we adopt the D2S 
framework [42], a lightweight regression-based relocalization method 
that directly maps sparse 2D keypoints to their corresponding 3D scene 
coordinates. Instead of relying on dense 3D point clouds and descriptor 
matching as in traditional feature-matching pipelines, D2S is trained to 
predict 3D coordinates from keypoint descriptors using a compact neu-
ral network. The network architecture consists of a multi-layer graph 
attention module that allows descriptors to communicate and model 
spatial context, followed by a shared multi-layer perceptron (MLP) 
that regresses both 3D coordinates and reliability scores for each key-
point. Each descriptor predicts exactly one 3D point, and the reliability 
score is trained using a supervised loss function to distinguish robust 
and stable features from unreliable ones, such as dynamic objects or 
blurred regions. Ground-truth 2D–3D correspondences are obtained 
from a Structure-from-Motion (SfM) model built on the training data, 
and losses are computed by comparing predicted 3D points with the 
corresponding SfM coordinates, as shown in Fig. 2. Images and SfM 
point clouds are used to train the D2S model offline. During inference, 
the pretrained model extracts 2D image features and regresses their 
corresponding 3D scene coordinates. The predicted correspondences 
are then solved using a PnP-based algorithm to estimate the camera

The trained D2S model is then used to estimate new camera poses 
from unseen query images. Given an input image, keypoints are ex-
tracted using SuperPoint, and their descriptors are passed through 
the trained D2S network to predict their 3D locations and confidence 
scores. A subset of high-confidence 2D–3D correspondences is selected 
based on the predicted reliability, and the camera pose is estimated 
using a RANSAC-based Perspective-n-Point (PnP) algorithm. This in-
ference process avoids the need to store or access dense 3D models, 
enabling fast and scalable pose estimation. The lightweight nature of 
the model makes it highly suitable for deployment in tower crane 
applications, where fast and accurate localization is essential for safe 
operation.
In the proposed framework, scene coordinate regression is not 
only used for camera pose estimation, but also serves as the geomet-
ric anchor of the overall pipeline. The scene coordinates predicted 
by the relocalization module define a sparse metric reference space 
that is later exploited to resolve the scale ambiguity of monocular 
depth estimation. This role is critical in crane-mounted monitoring, 
where camera viewpoints vary continuously and explicit recalibration 
is impractical.
3.2. 3D object localization
This section outlines the processing steps to recover metrically 
consistent 3D object positions from crane-mounted monocular im-
ages, including depth prediction, object detection, and geometric back-
projection into the world coordinate system.
3.2.1. Monocular depth map estimation
To estimate the 3D position of detected objects, it is necessary to 
obtain depth information that relates image observations to the camera 
coordinate system. Unlike static camera systems where stereo or multi-
view geometry can be exploited, crane-mounted monitoring operates 
under continuously changing viewpoints. Therefore, the system re-
quires a mechanism to infer per-frame depth information from a single 
image. The Depth Anything model [43] is adopted as the monocular 
depth estimation backbone to generate dense relative depth predictions 
for each input image. However, monocular depth estimation inherently 
suffers from scale ambiguity, meaning the predicted depth values are 
relative rather than metric. While the model was not explicitly trained 
on crane-mounted datasets, its training set includes drone and elevated-
view images, making it suitable for similar top-down perspectives. 
As a result, this model allows the system to recover generalized per-
pixel depth information without the need for stereo cameras or LiDAR 
sensors, making it well-suited for crane-mounted monocular setups.

A key challenge of monocular depth estimation is the inherent 
ambiguity of absolute scale. As a result, the predicted depth map pro-
vides only relative depth values and cannot be directly used for metric 
3D localization. This limitation becomes particularly critical in crane-
mounted monitoring scenarios, where accurate spatial relationships 
between workers and suspended loads must be computed in metric 
space. The scale ambiguity problem in monocular depth estimation 
has been extensively studied in prior work. Early approaches such as 
Eigen et al. [44] learn depth relationships using scale-invariant training 
objectives, while later methods estimate depth jointly with camera 
motion through self-supervised learning frameworks [45,46]. These 
approaches have demonstrated that reliable dense depth structures 
can be recovered from monocular imagery. However, the predicted 
depth values are typically defined only up to an unknown global scale, 
which limits their direct use for metric 3D localization in real-world 
environments.
To recover metric scale required for spatial reasoning, the proposed 
framework augments the monocular depth prediction with geometric 
constraints derived from the reconstructed scene. Specifically, sparse 
3D points obtained from the SfM reconstruction are reprojected into 
the image plane using the estimated camera pose. This reprojection 
produces sparse but metrically accurate depth anchors at selected pixel 
locations. These anchors serve as reliable geometric references that 
allow the dense monocular depth map to be aligned with the metric 
scene coordinate system, enabling absolute-scale depth recovery while 
preserving the dense spatial structure predicted by the monocular depth 
estimator.

3.2.2. 2D object detection with top-down view camera
Object detection is a fundamental step in 3D estimation, as it 
provides object classes, identities, and pixel-level locations, which 
are subsequently transformed into 3D coordinates. Detecting workers 
or construction machinery from a crane-mounted camera presents 
unique challenges due to the elevated top-down viewpoint, scale dis-
tortion, and frequent occlusions. While most object detection models 
are trained on horizontal or vehicle-mounted perspectives, recent stud-
ies have shown that modern detectors based on RetinaNet [36,47] 
and YOLO [12,48] can perform effectively on aerial and top-down 
views captured by crane-mounted cameras or drones . In this study, 
we adopt a one-stage detector, Real-Time Model of Object Detection 
(RTMDet) [49], to achieve a balance between detection accuracy 
and inference efficiency. Compared with existing one-stage detectors, 
RTMDet leverages large-kernel convolutions and a more balanced com-
putational design between the backbone and neck, enabling efficient 
contextual feature extraction while maintaining high inference speed 
without sacrificing accuracy. Therefore, it is well suited for detecting 
small and occluded objects in top-down construction scenarios.
The RTMDet model is trained on a large-scale dataset constructed 
from images collected from crane-mounted cameras and drones,
along with publicly available surveillance data from the VisDrone 
dataset [50]. To further enhance data diversity, a synthetic dataset 
representing realistic construction environments is generated under 
varying weather and lighting conditions, simulating different object 
scales and noise patterns observed on actual construction sites. The 
model is then trained on a hybrid dataset comprising both real and 
synthetic images.
After training, the model outputs bounding boxes in the form of 
(𝑥𝑐𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟 , 𝑦𝑐𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟, 𝑤, ℎ) in pixel coordinates. Given the top-down view-
point, the positions of workers and lifted objects are approximated 
using the center points of the bounding boxes (𝑥𝑐𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟 , 𝑦𝑐𝑒𝑛𝑡𝑒𝑟), as il-
lustrated in Fig. 3. These points are used as representative image 
coordinates (u, v) for subsequent back-projection, as described in Sec-
tion 3.2.3.

3.3. Digital twin visualization
After recovering metrically consistent 3D object positions, the pro-
posed framework establishes a digital-twin-based safety monitoring 
pipeline for spatial registration, hazard-zone estimation, and rule-based 
risk assessment.
3.3.1. Spatial registration
Although 3D worker coordinates can be recovered from camera 
re-localization and depth maps, these positions are originally defined 
in the local reference frame of the SfM-based D2S space. In contrast, 
the BIM model imported via the Revit–Omniverse connector preserves 
its own global metric coordinate system with real-world orientation, 
unit scale, and origin definition. Therefore, a synchronization step is 
introduced to establish a transformation between the SfM coordinate 
system and the BIM coordinate system. Through this transformation, 
both the estimated camera poses and the reconstructed 3D worker 
coordinates can be consistently represented in the BIM-based real-
world space, enabling subsequent safety monitoring and validation as 
described in Section 3.3.4 and Section 4.
The registration problem can be formulated as estimating a rigid 
transformation matrix 𝐓 ∈ R4×4 that minimizes the discrepancy be-
tween the point cloud generated by D2S and the BIM geometry. 
To achieve this, salient geometric features are extracted from both 
datasets. Candidate correspondences are then established using fea-
ture descriptors and matched via nearest-neighbor search. Since these 
matches may contain outliers, a RANSAC-based estimator is first used 
to compute a robust initial alignment. Finally, the transformation 
is refined using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) algorithm, which 
iteratively minimizes point-to-plane distances until convergence. The 
resulting matrix 𝐓 is subsequently applied to transform all relocalized 
worker positions and safety zones into the BIM coordinate system, 
enabling spatial synchronization within the digital twin. The relation-
ship between the transformation matrix, the reconstructed point cloud 
space, and the BIM coordinate space is illustrated in Fig. 4.

3.3.3. Omniverse-based deployment
After achieving synchronization, the 3D localization data are di-
rectly integrated into NVIDIA Omniverse to enable Digital Twin (DT) 
functionality. NVIDIA Omniverse is a state-of-the-art development plat-
form for building and operating 3D applications and services at scale 1u 
[56], and has been proven to be a potential solution for digital integra-
tion among various types of data [41]. In this study, we adopt NVIDIA 
Omniverse USD Composer [57] as the runtime environment for inter-
active site visualization and safety assessment. USD Composer provides 
advanced scene assembly, high-fidelity RTX rendering, and Python 
scripting support, enabling seamless mapping of camera poses and 
depth-derived 3D worker positions into the digital twin environment. 
This capability not only facilitates accurate spatial synchronization but 
also supports dynamic simulation of worker movements and potential 
collision scenarios.
In NVIDIA USD Composer (version 2023.2.5) [57], the prebuilt 3D 
BIM model is directly imported while its original coordinate reference 
and scale are preserved through the Omniverse Revit Connector. This 
ensures a one-to-one spatial correspondence between the real-world 
construction site and the digital twin environment. Subsequently, all 
3D entities, including worker positions and projections of hazard fall 
zones, are aligned from the D2S relocalization space to the Omniverse 
USD Composer environment by applying the transformation matrix 
𝐓. Simultaneously, the Replicator API, supported by the Omniverse

3.3.4. Risk assessment
In the final stage of digital twin illustration, risk level assessment 
is performed to quantify safety conditions around the crane opera-
tion zone. Beyond visualization, the digital twin performs rule-based 
spatial reasoning and risk evaluation on vision-derived 3D data. In-
stead of computing safety distances externally and re-importing the 
results, a Decision-Support Layer is established via the Python API 
in USD Composer to compute Euclidean distances and perform rule-
based risk evaluation directly within the digital twin environment, 
thereby reducing latency and data transfer overhead. These distances 
are then compared with the critical dangerous radius 𝑟𝑐𝑟𝑖𝑡 and medium 
dangerous radius 𝑟𝑚𝑒𝑑 to define the risk level for each entity, as detailed 
in Table 2. In addition, each worker is annotated with a specific color 
based on the defined risk level using the Material Definition Language 
(MDL) extension in NVIDIA Omniverse USD Composer, while a Python 
script is used to summarize all hazard classification results and report 
the current safety status. In parallel, the risk assessment is also updated 
in the 2D detection camera window, ensuring consistent visualization 
in both 2D and 3D. In the final step, a historical report is automatically 
generated; this file records all worker information in hazard zones, 
including date, time, positions, and safety status for further analysis. 
The process is repeated whenever data from a new frame are updated, 
ensuring real-time monitoring capability, as illustrated in Fig. 6.

4.1. Scene coordinates regression model performance
In order evaluate the performance of camera relocalization, we con-
duct experiment by training multiple datasets including both simulation 
and a real construction project using D2S-based platform [42], as listed 
in Fig. 8. In the first experiment, we set up a testbed at a construc-
tion site located in Miryang-si, Gyeongsangnam-do, Republic of Korea. 
Aerial-view videos of the scene were captured using a DJI Mini 2 Pro 
drone with a resolution of 1920 × 1080, and the dataset was divided 
into 75% training data and 25% testing data. In the second experiment, 
we evaluate model performance on synthetic datasets representing 
construction activities generated by Twinmotion. This software can 
replicate realistic structural elements and visual variations under differ-
ent weather conditions, making it suitable as a testbed for vision-based 
deep learning models. In this study, we simulate a camera trajectory 
that mimics drone-based surveying to generate synthetic datasets under 
daytime conditions with clear skies, ensuring that D2S can capture 
as many scene features as possible (Fig. 7). After that, the testbed 
includes videos recorded from trolley-mounted cameras in varied time 
milestones and three basic weather conditions. This experimental setup 
reflects real construction scenarios, where site surveying activities are 
typically conducted once or twice per week, while heavy machinery 
operations occur more frequently. The principle of the second experi-
ment is that the datasets used for training and testing in both real and 
synthetic scenarios are recorded along independent trajectories, where 
the training set is used for scene reconstruction and the testing set is 
applied for safety-related evaluation. Therefore, the model performance 
must generalize across varying conditions on construction sites.

4.1.1. Qualitative results
Figs. 9, 10, and 11 illustrate the qualitative performance of the 
D2S-MDE framework under both real and synthetic environments. As 
shown in Fig. 9, the camera relocalization results on a real construction 
site demonstrate that the predicted camera poses (red) are almost 
identical to the ground-truth trajectory (green). Despite the inherent 
complexity of construction environments, including dynamic workers 
and equipment, the framework is able to reconstruct a continuous 
camera path with minimal drift. This confirms that the proposed D2S-
MDE framework is well suited for clear-visibility conditions within the 
same domain, which correspond to the primary operational settings 
of crane-mounted and aerial cameras on construction sites. Fig. 10 
illustrates performance under moderately degraded conditions, includ-
ing day-rain, day-fog, and night-clear scenarios. In the rain scenario, 
most predicted camera poses remain close to the ground truth in both 
position and orientation, indicating moderate robustness to visual noise 
caused by rain streaks and surface reflections. Under foggy daytime 
conditions, larger pose deviations are observed, likely due to reduced 
visibility of structural features, which weakens the reliability of scene 
coordinate regression. Nevertheless, for a subset of frames, the overall 
trajectory structure is still preserved, suggesting partial robustness 
when degradation is not severe. Similarly, in night-clear scenes, the 
system is able to recover camera poses for the majority of frames, 
confirming that sufficient illumination alone can still support reliable 
localization.
Fig. 11 highlights the most challenging conditions, characterized 
by both low illumination and severely reduced visibility. In night-fog 
scenarios, localization performance degrades substantially, with fre-
quent pose failures and large deviations from the reference trajectory. 
This behavior indicates that the proposed vision-based relocalization 
pipeline reaches its operational limits when critical visual constraints 
are lost. These results emphasize that, while the framework performs 
reliably under clear or moderately degraded conditions, its applicability 
under extreme weather and lighting conditions remains limited.
Overall, these qualitative results highlight a clear relationship be-
tween training data characteristics and deployment performance. As 
described in the data preparation stage, the D2S relocalizer is pri-
marily trained using drone-captured and synthetic overhead videos 
recorded under clear daytime conditions, which provide rich struc-
tural features and stable illumination. Consequently, the framework 
achieves its highest accuracy and trajectory consistency when tested 
on scenes that share similar visual conditions, such as real construction 
sites and synthetic day-clear scenarios. When applied to out-of-domain 
environments, including different weather or illumination conditions, 
the pipeline can still function under certain constraints. In particular, 
night-clear scenes remain partially compatible with the learned scene 
representations, as sufficient structural visibility is preserved despite 
the absence of daylight. However, scenes involving severe visibility 
degradation, such as dense fog or combined night-fog conditions, lead 
to substantial performance degradation due to the loss of reliable 
visual cues required for scene coordinate regression. These observa-
tions indicate that the proposed pipeline inherently favors clear-sky 
conditions and performs most reliably when the training and testing 
domains are visually consistent. While limited generalization to unseen 
domains is possible under clear visibility, the results confirm that 
clear-weather scenes represent the preferred operational regime of the 
current D2S-MDE framework.
