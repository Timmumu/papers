Abstract 
During the construction of new Austrian tunnelling method (NATM) tunnels, inherent multi-source uncertainties may lead to sub-
stantial economic losses and project delays. To address this issue, a simulation system enabled by digital twin (DT) technology was devel-
oped. Guided by the concept of ﬁve-dimensional DT, a four-layer system architecture was established, including perception, 
transmission, modelling, and alternation layers. An integrated discrete event simulation (DES) model was then developed as the core 
of the system using structured system analysis. The model accounts for both geological and construction-related uncertainties, including 
routine delays and accident recovery durations. Geological uncertainties are represented through a stochastic geological modelling
approach based on the Markov random ﬁeld (MRF). Construction-related uncertainties are addressed using a tunnel vulnerability eval-
uation method based on the analytic hierarchy process – decision-making trial and evaluation laboratory (AHP–DEMATEL) method,
along with the ﬁtting of delay probability density functions. Finally, the proposed system was validated and evaluated in a real-world
tunnel project. The results demonstrate that the system eﬀectively analyzes multiple uncertainties, accurately estimates the construction
schedule, enhances communication with the construction site, and ensures the safe and eﬃcient completion of the project.
Keywords: NATM tunnelling; Digital twin; Process simulation ; Markov random ﬁeld; Tunnelling uncertainty

1.1 Bac kground
The new Austrian tunnelling method (NATM), due to 
its extensive applicability and economic advantages, has
become a principal tunnelling methodology ( Rabcewicz, 
1964). However, tunnel construction is inherently inﬂu-
enced by multifaceted uncertainties, which pose signiﬁcant
challenges to project execution and safety.
Liu et al. (2018) reported that 60% of accidents result 
from human error, 10% from natural causes, and 30% from
their combination. Sousa and Einstein (2021) categorized 
tunnel construction uncertainties into internal and external 
types. Internal uncertainties primarily involve conventional 
delay factors, such as variability in site organization and
management, worker skill levels, and health conditions
(Ye et al., 2023 ). For instance, the Heathrow Express Rail 
Link project in the UK faced a three-month delay due to 
human error. Although frequen t, these factors typically
have a limited impact and follow continuous probability
distributions ( Yu et al., 2017 ). In contrast, severe uncer-
tainties relate to construction accidents such as rockfalls, 
collapses, ﬂoods, spalling, and excessive deformation
(Sousa & Einstein, 2021 ). Despite their lower frequency, 
these incidents pose substantial safety and economic risks, 
exempliﬁed by the 14 000 m 3 collapse in Nepal’s Khimti I

1.2 Related works and problem statement
From a theoretical perspective, the repetitive nature of 
tunnel excavation cycles positions process simulation mod-
elling (PSM) as an eﬀective approach for analyzing con-
struction uncertainties ( Rahm et al., 2016 ), particularly in 
optimizing resource allocation and construction sequenc-
ing ( AbouRizk, 2010 ). Vick and Einstein (1974) were 
among the ﬁrst to apply PSM in tunnel engineering. Since 
then, ad vanced PSM tools have been developed, including
CYCLONE-CPM (
Bi et al., 2015 ), SD-DES ( Dang et al.,
2018), and CSM2020 ( Khetwal et al., 2022 ). Recent devel-
opments emphasize reﬁning simulation techniques and 
integrating complementary analytical tools to bette r align
with practical engineering demands, as outlined in Table 1. 
Among these methods, discrete event simulation (DES) 
stands out for its ability to model and analyze the complex-
ity and interdependence of events or processes, oﬀering
greater accuracy and ﬂexibility (
Abdelmegid et al., 2020 ). 
DES simulates events at discrete time points, triggeri ng
changes in the system’s state ( Sharma, 2015). When com-
bined with Monte Carlo (MC) simulation, which generates 
random variables for stochastic analysis, DES eﬀectively
addresses both probab ilistic and deterministic problems
(Metropolis & Ulam, 1949 ).

Sousa and Einstein (2021) emphasized that, aside from 
human management errors, unforeseen geological condi-

Geological conditions have also been integrated into sev-
eral PSM models. The decision aids for tunneling (DAT)
employ discrete information points along tunnel align-
ments to dynamically update geological conditions, thus

ally, Yu et al. (2017) integrated Bayesian networks within 
the CYCLONE model to systematically incorporate con-

studies on geological uncertainty modelling predominantly 
focus on one-dimensional an alyses along the tunnel axis,

The modelling of geological uncertainty is principally 
divided into two main research directions ( Wang et al.,
2018b). The ﬁrst employs geostatistical methods like the 
Kriging method, sequential Gaussian models, and 
multiple-point geosta tistics to describe variables regionally
through variogram functions (
Bian et al., 2024 ). However, 
these models often fall short in capturing the complex 
cyclicity and environmental interplays in lithological
sequences (
Bi et al., 2015 ). The second approach utilizes 
Markov models, such as Markov random ﬁeld (MRF)
and chain models (
Wang et al., 2024a ), to construct 
stochastic geological proﬁles. This method has been 
applied in the assessment of slope and tunnel stability
(Wang et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018b ). MRF methods, 
aligning closely with strata formation processes and spatial 
heterogeneity, provide more interp retable geological
insights compared to variogram-based approaches (
Elfeki 
& Dekking, 2001; Wang, 2020 ). Additionally, by discretiz-
ing geological proﬁles using MRF, the resulting units align 
with the cyclical process of DES simulation, thereby 
enhancing the integration of model modules. Conse-
quently, analyzi ng geological uncertainty via MRF
emerges as a logical progression towards achieving more
accurate simulation results (
Cho, 2007; Fent on &
Griﬃths, 2008 ). 
With the rapid advancement of internet-based informa-
tion technology, new possibilities have emerg ed for inno-
vating tunnel PSM models ( Phoon et al., 2022 ). Current 
PSM models largely remain theoretical and disconnected
from on-site realities ( Persson & Araldi, 2009 ), leading to 
slow, inaccurate responses to site conditions and limiting 
their ability to provide actionab le guidance to stakehold-
ers, thus reducing their practical value (
Yu et al., 2023 ). 
The core issue lies in the lack of eﬀective interaction and 
deep integration between construction sites and digital
models ( Li et al., 2024a ). In the context of transitioning 
towards Industry 4.0 ( Phoon et al., 2024 ), the introduction 
of digital twin (DT) technol ogy oﬀers a potential solution.
The contemporary consensus deﬁnes DT as a theoretical 
ﬁve-dimensional framework comprising physical en tities,
virtual entities, connections, DT data, and services (
Liu 
et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2018). DT describes, maps, and pre-
dicts the semantic, geometric, and behavioral characteris-
tics of physical entities through virtual representations
and data-driven models ( Samaniego et al., 2020 ), thereby 
enabling functions such as real-time perception, dynamic 
control, and information sharing (
Tao et al., 2019b ). Since 
its introduction to tunnel engineering around 2020, DT has 
been the focus of recent studies exploring its application
(Opoku et al., 2021 ). Ye et al. (2023) developed a DT-
based early warning and safety management platform, sim-
ulating tunnel construction and geological scenarios. Fang 
et al. (2024) developed a state digital twin model that inte-
grates building information models and point cloud data to
assess over-and under- excavation during the tunnel con-
struction process. Shen et al. (2022) devised a DT-based 
design methodology for tunnel interior lighting and deco-
ration, merging virtual reality (VR), numerical simulations,
and ﬁeld experiments.
These studies highlight DT’s potential to integrate phys-
ical construction sites into the virtual domain, enabli ng
cost-eﬀective lifecycle simulation and optimized construc-
tion ( Li et al., 2024a ). However, the application of DT in 
tunnel PSM modelling remains largely unexplored, with 
signiﬁcant challenges in translating DT from theory to 
practice, including integrating diverse data sources, ensur-
ing data interoperability in complex underground environ-
ments, and developing systems for dynamic analysis and
feedback services. Additionally, its practical eﬀectiveness 
requir es further investigation.

1.3 Obj ectives
To address the research gap, the speciﬁc objectives of 
this study are as follows: (i) Propose a PSM model capable 
of considering multi-source uncertainties in NATM tunnel 
construction; (ii) Develop a DT framework to provide 
timely and eﬀective guidance for tunnel construction; (iii)
Explore the application eﬀects of an intelligent construc-
tion system integrating the DT concept and PSM in a
real-world case.
Therefore, an innovative DT-enabled simulation system 
for NATM tunnel construction considering multi-source 
uncertainty is developed. The main contribution of this 
paper is as follows: (i) An integrated PSM model was devel-
oped to structurally simulate NATM tunnel construction, 
capturing its components and construction processes; (ii) 
Modules were developed for the PSM to enable the evalu-
ation of geological and construction-related uncertainties, 
including those related to conventional delays and acci-
dents; (iii) A ﬁve-dimensional DT framework for tunnel
construction guidance was proposed, integrating percep-
tion, transmission, modelling, and service layers. The
research ﬁndings have been validated in a NATM tunnel
project in Yunnan Province, China, demonstrating signiﬁ-
cant savings in both construction time and cost.

2 Methodo logy
The methodology for developing the DT-enabled simu-
lation system comprises two main components: the estab-
lishment of a system fram ework and the construction of
an integrated PSM model, as illustrated in
Fig. 1. Drawing 
upon the concept of the 5D-DT, the system framework is 
structured into four interconnected layers: the perception 
layer, transmission layer, modelling layer, and the alterna-
tion layer. To systematically address uncertainties inherent 
in the construction process, the modelling layer incorpo-
rates the integrated PSM model, which serves as the analyt-
ical core of the digital domain. This model consists of three 
interrelated modules. Module I addresses geological uncer-
tainty by integrating borehole data with prior geological 
knowledge and employs MRF to simulate geologic distri-
butions. Module II evaluates the risk of construction acci-
dents by integrating geologic data with engineering
measurements and expert judgments, utilizing the analytic
hierarchy process – decision-making trial and evaluation
laboratory (AHP–DEMATEL) method to assess accident
vulnerability levels. Module III synthesizes the uncertain-
ties from geological conditions and con struction accidents 
with conventional delay factors, using the DE S and MC 
simu lation to forecast construction productivity and poten-
tial delays.

2.1 Digital t win framework
Based on the concept of the ﬁve-di mensional DT model 
MDT = (PE, VE, Ss, DD, CN) ( Tao et al., 2019a ), which 
corresponds to physical entities (PE), virtual entities 
(VE), services (Ss), twin data (DD), and connections 
(CN), we developed a 5D DT-enabled process simulation
system framework tailored for guiding NATM construc-
tion, as illustrated in Fig. 2 . At the implementation level, 
the framework comprises the perception layer (sensing 
PE and collecting DD), the transmission layer (facilitating 
bi-directional CN), the modelling layer (constructing VE),
and the alternation layer (oﬀering Ss and providing CN
from VE to PE), as depicted in Fig. 3. 
The perception layer serves as the foundational element 
of the system architecture, designed to eﬃciently collect 
DD from onsite PE (e. g., workers, construction machin-
ery, tunnel structures, and geology). To enable comprehen-
sive data collection, an array of methods has been
implemented on-site, including wearable data loggers,
machinery parameter data collection systems, mobile data
collection vehicles, multifunctional sensors, and video 
surveillance. Additionally, the perception layer integrates 
relevant documentation from both pre-construction and 
construction stages. To manage these heterogeneous and 
multi-source data, the system incorporates a conﬁgurable 
data access service framework, supporting modular expan-
sion and multiple data access methods such as SQL Server,
NetMQ, SignalR, WebAPI, Excel, and device protocols.
Fig. 2. Five-dimensional DT conceptual model.
The transmission layer, representing the CN dimension 
of DT, enables seamless integration across its components. 
It eﬃciently relays data associated with geographic loca-
tions (GIS coordinates), engineering sections (3D identi-
ﬁers), and database indices. The layer utilizes a robust 
wireless network composed of multimodal self-organizing 
nodes, which are made up of access points (APs), direc-
tional antennas, and wireless bridges, powered by battery 
packs. Nodes placed every kilometer enable real-time 
transmission of construction data to a base station at the
tunnel entrance, centralizing the data for the control cen-
ter. This setup enables real-time monitoring of tunnel
safety and progress while connecting site personnel and
machinery to the Internet for immediate access to analyti-
cal results and system alerts.
The modelling layer creates a corresponding VE based on 
PE and DD. This layer integrates structured data, 2D draw-
ings, and 3D models using graphic engine technologies such

The uppermost layer, the alternation layer, delivers Ss to 
guide tunnel construction. Serving as a directed CN from 
VE to PE, it ensures real-time feedback of analytical results
and supports on-site collaborative operations. Based on the
infrastructure smart service system (iS3) framework ( Zhu 
et al., 2018 ) and cloud technology, it oﬀers interactive ser-
vices via desktop (web) interfaces and mobile applications, 
ensuring compatibility with devices like laptops, desktops, 
phones, and tablets. It enables the immediate communica-
tion of data-driven model outcomes, such as geological 
analysis, construction progress, and risk assessments, to 
stakeholders, including workers, engineers, managers, and
experts. By facilitating dynamic interactions and informed
decision-making for onsite personnel, it also provides
access to tailored historical data, streamlining project
management.
2.2 Process simulation model development
2.2.1 Structured analysis and modelling
In tunnel and linear infrastructure construction, engi-
neering tasks often adhere to a sequential, repetiti ve pat-
tern, establishing a predeﬁned operational sequence
(O¨ kmen & O ¨ ztas,, 2008 ). To enhance the accuracy and 
depth of the PSM model for NATM co nstruction, we
adopted the systems modeling language (SysML)
(Friedenthal et al., 2014 ). This innovative language 
employs a variety of diagram types to depict system 
requirements, physical constraints, structures, and behav-
iors (
Dang et al., 2018). Speciﬁcally, we used the block def-
inition diagram (BDD) to detail the construction site’s 
structural elements and fundamental units, termed 
‘‘blocks.” Additionally, the sequence diagram (SQ) illus-
trated the dynamic interactions and operational logic
among these blocks, as depicted in
Figs. 4 and 5 .
Fig. 4. Block deﬁnition diagram for NATM tunnel construction site.
In the BDD depicted in Fig. 4, each on-site PE is deﬁned 
as a unit (block) ( Dang et al., 2018 ). The construction site 
primarily comprises four fundamental units: personnel, 
machinery, structure, and geology. The collaborative exe-
cution system integrates specialized personnel and various 
equipment. In the Labour block, the crews are divided
based on tasks, although on-site personnel may take on
multiple tasks. As depicted in
Fig. 5 , the execution logic 
of NATM construction unfolds in the following sequence: 
(i) Initiation stage: The construction cycle begins, and 
workers utilize horizontal drilling machines or geophysical 
methods to assess the geological conditions ahead. In cases 
of loose or fractured strata, advanced support structures 
are implemented. (ii) Drilling and blasting: Under the engi-
neers’ drilling and charging directives, the drilling crew 
operates drilling jumbos for borehole creation, followed 
by the blasting crew and explosive loading trucks executing 
charging and blasting. (iii) Safety and clearance: Post-
blasting, a safety inspection interval is observed, after 
which the excavation team employs shovels and conveyors
for debris removal. (iv) Primary support: The lining crew
applies initial shotcrete via wet shotcrete machines and
erects preliminary support structures, including steel
frames, anchor bolts, and steel mesh. (v) Secondary lining:
Utilizing the lining trolley, the team lays the waterproof
layer and performs secondary lining of the arch wall. Fol-
lowing each cycle, engineers assess the outcomes and initi-
ate the subsequent cycle, a process that repeats until tunnel
construction completion.

2.2.2 Double-loop process simulation
Leveraging the DES model, we formulated a simulation 
execution strategy, outlined in Fig. 7 . Reﬂecting the 
NATM tunnels’ sequential and cyclical excavation process, 
we established three distinct simulation clocks: a global 
model clock (clock-g), a single simulation clock (clock-s), 
and an excavation cycle clock (clock-c). These clocks utilize 
a next-event time advancement mechanism to propel the 
simulation. This setup allows for precise monitoring and
documentation of simulation activities at the global level,
for individual simulations, and for each speciﬁc excavation
cycle. The methodology unfolds as follows:
(1) Outer loop (MC Simulation): The outer loop consists 
of N s iterations of the complete tunnel construction
MC simulation. Firstly, the model is initialized with

2.4 Uncertain delays analysis
As described in Section 1, the uncertainties in the con-
struction process mainly include conventional delay factors 
and construction accidents. The conventional delay factors 
can be described through continuous probability distribu-
tions. In contrast, the causes and impacts of construction 
accidents display greater complexity and uncertainty. 
Therefore, we focused on exploring key factors aﬀecti ng 
tunnel vulnerability, such as hydrogeological conditions, 
rock properties, and rock mass structure. We employed
the AHP–DEMATEL method to model the interactions
among these factors, assessing tunnel vulnerability in each
excavation cycle. Based on this assessment, we further ana-
lyze the probability of construction accidents and the
potential delays they may cause.

2.4.2 Consequence analysis of accidents
In the DES simulation of NATM tunnel construction, 
representing process durations with ﬁxed values is imprac-
tical due to conventional delays. Probability distributions 
and MC sampling are essential to account for this uncer-
tainty. Distribution ﬁtting, using EasyFit software (version 
5.5) in this study, identiﬁes the best-ﬁtting probability dis-
tribution based on ﬁeld-collected data. The identiﬁed distri-
bution was then used in AnyLogic’s MC module and 
distribution library to generate random discrete values
for simulation. To focus on the central 90% probability
range, values within the 5th and 95th percentiles of the
cumulative distribution function were retained. This study
assumes an absolute errorx< 5, a conﬁdence level
b= 95%, and 2500 simulation iterations.
In tunnel construction risk management, a comprehen-
sive analysis that addresses both the likelihood and conse-
quences of potential accidents is crucial. Analysis of 
engineering failure statistical databases and scholarly 
research reveals that accidents, while critical, are rare 
within the construction process. The occurrence probabil-
ity of such accidents varies exponentially across diﬀerent
vulnerability levels. Referring to relevant studies, we utilize
the probability scale (
Liu et al., 2015; Yu et al., 2017 ), as 
shown in Table 3 . This scale oﬀers verbal and numerical 
representations of accident probabilities, making it ideal 
for assessing low-probability events. Probability ranges 
are categorized by vulnerability levels, with speciﬁc values
determined through a linear transformation of assessment
scores.
