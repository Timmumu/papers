Defect Trigger Identification in a Construction Digital 
Twin Using Quality Linked Data and Iterative 
Deviation Computation
Fenghua Tian1; Ningshuang Zeng2; Zirui Li3; Qingzi Ge4; 
Qian Wang5; and Qiming Li6
Abstract: Current quality management in construction faces several challenges, including insufficient precision in control, unclear allocation 
of quality responsibilities, and a lack of long-term management mechanisms. As the industry shifts from a problem-solving after occurrence 
approach to a proactive paradigm, identification of potential quality defects and risks has become a primary prerequisite. This study introduces 
the concept of quality defect triggers, detectable, modelable, and controllable precursors to quality issues that operationalize risk factors 
and can be visualized within a construction digital twin (DT). Building on an exploration of their evolutionary mechanisms, the study 
develops an integrated framework that combines ontology and linked data technologies to identify quality defect triggers through multisource 
heterogeneous data integration, comprising three core components: (1) the evolutionary mechanisms of defect trigger as the theoretical 
foundation and underlying principles for computation; (2) an ontology-based four-layer semantic model encompassing quality standards, 
quality data, defect triggers, and construction processes, implemented using the GraphDB graph database to achieve semantic linkage; 
and (3) a quality defect trigger identification and iterative deviation computation engine in construction DT. By unifying data modeling, 
semantic mapping, and automated reasoning, the framework provides a systematic and practical approach to early quality defect identification. 
Its effectiveness was validated through a real-world prefabricated composite-slab installation case, comparative experiments, and expert 
evaluation, demonstrating its applicability and potential to support proactive, data-informed quality management in construction practice.

Introduction
Over the last decade, the architecture, engineering, construction, 
and operations (AECO) industry has made substantial investments 
in digital transformation. Building information modeling (BIM) has 
become the mainstream modeling tool, Internet of Things (IoT) sen-
sors continuously capture on-site process data, and digital twin (DT) 
technologies are increasingly used for visualization of geometry, 
progress, and operations (Afzal et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024b).
Governments and industry associations in various countries have 
successively issued digital delivery and information management 
guidelines to promote data collection and sharing throughout the 
entire project life cycle. For example, the Ministry of Housing 
and Urban-Rural Development of the People’s Republic of China 
(2022) introduced the 14th Five-Year Plan for the Development 
of the Construction Industry, and a German-based consulting firm 
(Roland Berger 2016) published the report Digitization in the Con-
struction Industry: Building Europe’s Road to “Construction 4.0,” 
which outlines the digital transformation of the construction sector.
However, multiple international surveys have indicated that the 
direct cost of rework still accounts for 11%–15% of total project 
investment, while claims and schedule delays caused by quality 
defects are even on the rise ( Love et al. 2009; Olanrewaju 2025). 
This phenomenon highlights that despite the growing availability 
of digital tools and the accumulation of quality data, construction 
quality issues have not declined.
Although current quality management practices incorporate risk 
control measures (risk identification, inspections, sampling, and 
approvals), significant gaps persist in linking abstract risks to 
concrete events, tracking the rapid evolution of risks, and ensuring 
the implementation of proactive measures ( Guo et al. 2025; Jeon 
et al. 2023; Mohamad and Tran 2021). Quality management benefits 
from the mature domain of safety in construction, which distin-
guishes among risk, hazard, and accident and develops proactive 
identification methods (Chen et al. 2025b; Li et al. 2018; Pereira 
et al. 2018 ; Yang et al. 2012 ). However, unlike safety, quality 
is an intrinsic project attribute that extends beyond construction 
completion or handover (Bowen et al. 2002; Mallawaarachchi and 
Senaratne 2015).

Due to its long duration and complexity, the mechanism of 
quality defect formation and evolution does not fully align with 
the hazard–accident discontinuous mechanism in the safety field. 
The formation of defects may involve a long time span and be 
influenced by the cumulative effects of quality deviations, while 
also being triggered by various events. This includes both the risk–
incident disruptive effect ( He and Atangana Njock 2023 ; Okonta 
et al. 2024) and the risk–(deviation/defect trigger)–defect gradual 
effect (Faqih and Zayed 2021 ; Ma et al. 2021). The former has a 
severe impact on quality incidents and safety, and the latter affects 
quality attributes over the long term, extending into the operational 
phase and throughout the building’s life cycle. However, this cou-
pled mechanism is underexplored in existing research, and current 
quality hazard inspections in practice, being experience-driven and 
checklist-based, struggle to capture finer-grained hazard elements, 
including defect triggers and deviations.
Although current individual innovations and technologies, such 
as defect detection (Guo et al. 2021; Liang et al. 2023), quality data 
integration (Kovacs and Micsik 2021; Yan et al. 2025b), and design 
specification checks (Ma et al. 2024; Sydora and Stroulia 2020), are 
well-developed and capable of addressing existing quality issues, 
they often are not related to preventive measures and interpretabil-
ity. These approaches tend to emphasize the identification of issues 
at later stages rather than addressing the underlying latent factors 
that could trigger defects.
Technically, the challenge lies in developing methods that 
can capture proactive measures and provide the necessary inter-
pretability for early-stage quality hazard identification in con-
struction projects. This requires addressing the complexities of 
integrating diverse data sources, such as model files [(Industry 
Foundation Class (IFC)/BIM metadata], real-time sensor readings 
(e.g., concrete strength, temperature–humidity, vibration records), 
and inspection reports (PDF scans of concrete-cube tests and 
rebar reinspection results) to event and decision logs (modifica-
tion orders, and image records) ( Choi and Lee 2022 ; Iqbal et al. 
2024; Tuhaise et al. 2023 ). Current DT development provides a 
solid foundation for integrating and presenting both structured and 
unstructured quality data with model views ( Cai et al. 2021; Liu 
et al. 2024b ; Ramonell et al. 2023 ; Yan et al. 2025b ), and can 
incorporate checking algorithms to simulate the accumulation of 
deviations and improve the early-stage identification of defects.
Therefore, this study focuses on investigating the underlying mech-
anisms of quality defects and adapting application technologies to 
develop new methods, aiming to achieve the following objectives:
1. Define the concept of quality defect triggers, explore the risk–
(deviation/defect trigger)–defect–incident evolutionary mecha-
nism with an emphasis on the precursory nature of defect triggers 
and deviations, and clarify their identification characteristics at 
a finer granularity.
2. Develop a hierarchical framework that integrates construction 
processes, quality standards, quality data, and quality defect 
triggers, and apply ontology-based approaches to model their 
relationships, ensuring contextual adaptation.
3. Integrate multisource heterogeneous quality data and construct 
quality linked data (QLD) for identifying defect triggers within 
the context of construction DT, facilitating more precise and 
contextually relevant identification.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 
“Related Work” reviews the latest research progress on quality 
defect trigger identification and highlights the research gaps that 
this study aims to address. Section “Research Methodology” 
presents the methodology proposed in this paper. Section “Frame-
work of Quality Linked Data for Defect Trigger Identification” 
elaborates on the overall framework, key functional modules, and 
technical details of the defect trigger identification framework based 
on QLD. Section “Implementation and Validation” presents a case 
study on the prefabricated composite-slab construction process to 
evaluate the functional feasibility, effectiveness, and practical value 
of the proposed framework in engineering practice, based on com-
parative experiments and expert interviews. Section “Discussion” 
discusses the theoretical contributions and practical implications of 
this study. Finally, section “Conclusion” summarizes the paper and 
outlines directions for future research.
Related Work
Quality Issue Evolution Mechanism and Defect 
Trigger Identification
In the construction field, quality management can draw on the 
more mature domain of safety management, which offers analytical 
perspectives for distinguishing among risk, hazard, and accident. 
According to reviews on safety management in construction (Alkaissy 
et al. 2020; Sousa et al. 2014; Zhou et al. 2015), early safety research 
began with accident analysis studies that examined numerous accident 
cases and developed causation models (e.g., Abdelhamid and Everett 
2000; Suraji et al. 2001) and statistical analyses (e.g., Huang and Hinze 
2003; Shin et al. 2018). Subsequently, the focus shifted to accident 
prevention studies such as design for safety (e.g., Behm 2005; Yang 
et al. 2012), and later progressed toward risk evaluation research (Li 
et al. 2017; Lu et al. 2021).
Previous construction safety studies have recognized two stages in 
elaborating the mechanisms underlying the evolution of safety issues. 
The first stage, i.e., the context-general stage, conceptualizes the 
relationships among risk, hazard, and accident at a macro level. Early 
safety research, based on accident investigations, sought to develop 
generalizable risk management theories (Patterson and Neailey 2002). 
Here, risk precedes hazard, defined abstractly as the uncertainty 
affecting objectives, a latent indicator of potential hazards (Albert et al. 
2020), consistent with general risk management standards such as 
ISO 31000:2009 (ISO 2009) (Sui Pheng and Wee 2001). The second, 
context-specific stage, defines these relationships at a micro level, 
emphasizing how specific hazards and risks emerge within project 
environments. In this stage, hazard precedes risk, as identifiable 
domain hazards (Carter and Smith 2006; Perlman et al. 2014) are used 
to assess risk through probability and consequence severity.
Unlike progress or safety, quality is an intrinsic attribute of a 
project that does not end with the completion of construction or 
the handover of the project. Because of its long time span and the 
complexity of influencing factors, the current mechanism of quality 
defect identification is more analogous to the first stage in safety 
research. Fig. 1 illustrates the underlying mechanism of quality 
issue evolution, developed with reference to the mature frameworks 
established in safety management (Carter and Smith 2006).
The evolution mechanism among quality risk, hazard, defect, 
and incident is not linear, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Nevertheless, 
this study uses risk–(deviation/defect trigger)–defect–incident to 
represent it conceptually. According to the mechanism established 
in safety research, hazards in the quality domain can be categorized 
into consequence-oriented and process-oriented types, i.e., devia-
tion and defect triggers. The deviation that occurs during quality 
transmission partially corresponds to a near miss in safety studies 
(Carter and Smith 2006). When it exceeds standard requirements, 
it is classified as a quality failure (Barber et al. 2000); when it does 
not exceed but may cause potential impact, it warrants an early 
warning (Li et al. 2025; Yao et al. 2021). In contrast, a quality defect 
trigger refers more to an event-type occurrence or the underlying 
condition that gives rise to it, encompassing nonconforming states,

Existing techniques and approaches such as knowledge model-

foundation for construction quality issue preventive identification

identifying hazards at the granularity of deviations and defect 
triggers (Chen and Luo 2014; Liu et al. 2024b; Sheng et al. 2020); 
(2) a methodological foundation for aggregating and validating 
quality-related data with higher precision thresholds ( Kovacs and 
Micsik 2021; Ma et al. 2024; Yan et al. 2025b); and (3) an environ-
mental foundation for representing the virtual-physical scenario 
with model views in which deviations and defect triggers emerge 
(Cai et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2020).
Knowledge Modeling and Data Integration
In the AECO industry, knowledge modeling and data integration 
have evolved from early database and expert systems (McGartland 
and Hendrickson 1985 ) to more sophisticated and semantically 
enriched approaches. The adoption of BIM and open standards 
such as IFC marked a major step toward digital and interoperable 
project environments, which formalized domain concepts and sys-
tematically constructed building data (Antunes et al. 2024; Bellon 
et al. 2025; Matarneh et al. 2022). In recent years, technologies such 
as the semantic web, ontologies, linked data, and knowledge graphs, 
and digital twin have enabled the more effective integration and 
reuse of heterogeneous data from multiple sources (He et al. 2025; 
Liu et al. 2024a, b; Yan et al. 2025a; Zhong et al. 2020).
Ontology and knowledge graph technologies have been widely 
applied to information integration and data sharing ( Ramonell 
et al. 2023), safety management (Fang et al. 2020), quality manage-
ment (Xiahou et al. 2025), cost control and schedule management 
(Pfitzner et al. 2025), and intelligent construction equipment (Chen 
et al. 2023). In construction quality management, ontologies and 
knowledge graphs play two main roles: they formalize quality 
standards and managerial experience into reusable knowledge 
bases. Although these bases are built via semantic modeling, recent 
studies showed that large language models (LLMs) can extract 
structured knowledge from engineering texts.
Using prompt engineering and retrieval-augmented generation 
(RAG), LLMs identify entities, relations, and clause semantics, 
then align them with knowledge graphs to improve verifiability. 
This approach has been validated in contract review, safety accident 
analysis, quality inspection, and engineering Q&A ( Isah and Kim 
2025; Liu et al. 2025a, b; Zheng et al. 2025). When knowledge 
bases are integrated with BIM and computer vision technologies, 
these tools support design model compliance checks ( Zhang and 
El-Gohary 2017), quality management Q&A system development 
(Chen et al. 2025a ), identification of construction quality issues 
(Lee et al. 2016; Liang et al. 2023), and traceability of quality issues 
(Jing and Li 2024; Kim et al. 1999).
Linked data is a technological specification used for publishing 
and connecting structured data on the Web, aiming to enable data 
to be automatically read, understood, and inferred by machines 
through standardized methods (Bizer et al. 2023; Mountantonakis 
and Tzitzikas 2020). The common implementations are Web Ontol-
ogy Language (OWL) and Resource Description Framework (RDF) 
(Bizer et al. 2023).
In AECO, it enables interoperability among BIM/GIS models, 
IoT sensor streams, and documentary artifacts, supporting cross-
domain data integration ( Pauwels et al. 2017b; Shen et al. 2024). 
For construction quality management, it is used to integrate hetero-
geneous quality data, check construction/quality rules, enrich BIM 
semantics (Bassier et al. 2024; Werbrouck et al. 2020), and link 
facilities management (FM) systems with BIM components ( Kim 
et al. 2018). Ontologies supply the shared vocabulary, and knowl-
edge graphs instantiate and connect entities, forming the semantic 
backbone; recent work has moved from ontology construction 
to BIM integration, intelligent reasoning/analytics, and decision 
support for construction robotics ( Farghaly et al. 2023 ; Pauwels 
et al. 2017a).
Graph databases are purpose-built, graph theory–based storage 
systems for highly connected data. They enable fast traversal,

Rule-Based Checking in Construction Digital Twin
Current information modeling techniques in the AECO industry, 
such as BIM, are predominantly aligned with the object-oriented 
paradigm, focusing on the structured representation of entities, 
relationships, and rules through data schemas, typically imple-
mented in shared databases or standardized data formats ( Choi 
et al. 2016 ; Manzoor et al. 2021 ). It has enabled the transfor-
mation from human-understandable representations of construc-
ted assets to machine-interpretable information, while offering a 
well-established framework and infrastructure for the storage and 
retrieval of as-designed data. As a result, a range of methods 
and tools for verifying as-designed data (e.g., collision checking 
and code compliance checking) and comparing as-built data with 
as-designed data (e.g., construction progress updating, safety warn-
ing, and quality tracing) have been developed and are increasingly 
applied in practice (Ma et al. 2023; Sydora and Stroulia 2020; Wang 
et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2023, 2020).
Rule-based checking is the underlying method of the aforemen-
tioned applications that involves formalizing codes, standards, or 
heuristic guidelines into machine-executable rule sets, combined 
with software engines for automated compliance verification. The 
core process involves developing a rule base that converts quality 
acceptance standards and industry expert knowledge into executa-
ble rules. For instance, Guo et al. (2021) defined a rule database 
based on facade evaluation standards and used deep learning tech-
nology to detect facade quality defects; Völker and Shokouhi (2015) 
transformed the physical characteristics of concrete honeycomb 
defects into quantitative thresholds and used multisensor data 
to assess concrete quality; Kang et al. ( 2020) encoded practical 
knowledge of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP) system 
installation into parameters for a Scan-to-BIM workflow, employ-
ing point cloud scanning technology to identify installation defects.
Rule-based checking in a modeled environment, such as BIM, 
primarily involves as-designed (virtual) and as-built (real-world) 
data. The development of construction DT has unlocked the poten-
tial to incorporate full life-cycle timelines and multiple dimensions 
of data, integrated with a multilevel knowledge graph with accumu-
lated instantiations (Listl et al. 2024; Ramonell et al. 2023). Through 
dynamic mapping and intervention mechanisms via simulation and 
learning techniques, it has extended the scope and capabilities 
of current building information modeling. Therefore, rule-based 
checking can be applied in a construction DT for more precise 
quality control, integrating data or data structures from the Work 
Breakdown Structure (WBS) and Work Packages (WP), as well as 
various ontologies and knowledge graphs ( Liu and Lu 2020 ; Stas 
and Abrishami 2024).
Research Gaps
Due to established conventions and practices in quality manage-
ment and defect prevention, deviations can be identified through 
quality inspections and data comparisons. However, the methods 
for identifying defect triggers remain less developed. Many studies 
have focused on the causation of quality defects, which are typically 
categorized into direct causes and root causes, covering areas such 
as organizational influences, defect supervision, and behavioral 
preconditions (e.g., Josephson and Hammarlund 1999 ; Lambers 
et al. 2024 ; Talebi et al. 2021 ). However, most of these studies 
are qualitative and statistical analyses, including long-term project 
research and observation, rather than timely identification methods. 
For early identification methods, existing approaches often focus 
on real-time defect detection (e.g., Guo et al. 2021 ) and on-site 
inspections with quality hazard checklists (e.g., Choi and Lee 
2022), neglecting the underlying evolution mechanism and lacking 
accurate, preventive interventions.
From a technical perspective, several studies have incorpo-
rated emerging innovations into quality hazard management 
(e.g., Jahanshahi et al. 2013 ; Zhong et al. 2025 ), extending the 
safety-domain logic of hazard–accident transitions to quality man-
agement contexts. These studies provide valuable methodological 
references; however, quality hazard identification is characterized 
by a gradual and evolutionary process of quality formation, which 
necessitates a more comprehensive consideration and reflection 
of elements such as hazardous events (corresponding to defect 
trigger) and near misses (corresponding to deviation) in the quality 
management domain.
Therefore, this study, based on the mechanisms of quality for-
mation and evolution, i.e., risk–(deviation/defect trigger)–defect–
incident, reconstructs and recombines technical approaches 
(e.g., quality data integration and checking) and management meth-
ods (e.g., process patterns assignment in DT), aiming to improve 
the granularity of identification, time span, data utilization, and 
interpretable presentation of potential quality defects.
Research Methodology
The methodology guides the design and validation of a framework 
for identifying quality defect triggers. Fig. 2 summarizes three 
stages. Stage 1 conducts a literature review to define the research 
problem and objectives. Stage 2 develops a DT-enabled concep-
tual framework based on the physical–data–virtual architecture, 
comprising (1) a defect trigger evolution mechanism; (2) an identi-
fication ontology for consistent semantics; (3) a QLD module that 
integrates multisource heterogeneous data from the physical layer; 
and (4) a deviation calculation and defect trigger identification 
module. Stage 3 implements a prototype and validates it in a prefab-
ricated composite-slab installation case, and semistructured expert 
interviews then assess the framework’s definitions and functions, 
ensuring practical relevance.

Evolution Mechanisms of Quality Defect Triggers
The evolution mechanisms of defect triggers primarily refer to 
the process through which defect triggers form and evolve into 
quality defects and quality accidents. Quality defects and quality 
accidents are the main manifestations of construction quality non-
conformance, with accidents representing a more severe form. 
Preventing quality defects is essential for achieving high-quality 
construction outcomes and depends on identifying and eliminating 
defect triggers.
Defect triggers can be classified into six categories across three 
dimensions. Based on the quantifiability of the defect trigger, they can 
be divided into deviation-type defect triggers and non-deviation-type 
defect triggers (Chi et al. 2015). Deviation-type defect triggers are 
quantifiable trigger factors to quality defect, characterized by meas-
urable parameters such as material nonconformities, environmental 
deviations, and measurement errors. Non-deviation-type defect trig-
gers refer to trigger factors that are not directly characterized by 
specific quality deviation parameters, such as managerial deficiencies, 
inappropriate construction processes, human misjudgment, and com-
placent or lax managerial attitudes. Based on the objective formation 
process, they can be classified into defect triggers based on physical 
evolution mechanisms and defect triggers based on management 
evolution mechanisms (Lambers et al. 2024). Based on the relationship 
of their evolution, they can be categorized into defect triggers based 
on causal relationships and defect triggers based on correlational 
relationships (Aljassmi et al. 2016; Lee 2019).

Construction of Defect Trigger Identification Ontology
The identification of quality defect triggers requires mining and 
integrating heterogeneous, dispersed quality data from quality risk 
sources. To unify and semantically link these data, this study adop-
ted a knowledge-graph solution: a domain ontology (TBox) that 
formalizes concepts, properties, and relations, and an instance layer 
(ABox) that populates them with project data for integration and 
reasoning (Abu-Salih and Alotaibi 2024). Within this solution, this 
study proposes a four-layer framework—quality standards, quality 
data, defect triggers, and construction processes. The standards layer 
specifies evaluation criteria [e.g., ISO 9000 (ISO 2015) and project 
codes]; the data layer aggregates sensor streams, BIM models, 
and inspection records in multiple formats; the defect trigger layer 
captures physical, managerial, and hybrid causes; and the process 
layer structures construction activities as the basic identification units. 
Linking these layers anchors triggers in their spatial, temporal, and 
logical contexts, enabling precise identification and prevention. Fig. 5 
illustrates the hierarchy of quality data and defect triggers.

Quality Linked Data Development and 
Deviation Calculation
Ontology-based QLD is an effective means to integrate multisource 
heterogeneous quality data ( Sheng et al. 2020 ). In this study, 
the Construction_DeTrig_Identification_Ontology serves as the 
TBox, and source files are mapped to ABox instances to realize 
unified semantics and linkage. From a data format perspective, 
the primary data types include IFC (.ifc extension), PDF (.pdf 
extension), CSV (.csv extension), and JPEG (.jpg extension). Along 
the temporal dimension, data are organized into four phases: 
design, production and transportation, on-site construction and 
installation, operation and maintenance. Along the spatial–proce-
dural dimension, a top-down WBS/WP scheme decomposes the 
project into four levels: division works, construction zones, con-
struction processes and specific tasks. Each quality record is tagged 
with a unique WBS code, enabling precise spatial positioning 
on site and temporal alignment with process progression. The 
WBS structure is shown in Fig. 8. The Hoisting task is coded 
P001-PrefabricatedStructure-1#-08F-A-02-03, and a prefabricated 
beam component is coded P001-PrefabricatedStructure-1#-08F-A-
YZL-02-1, where YZL-02-1 denotes the first component of the 
second prefabricated beam type.
The core task is to align real-world engineering data with the 
TBox so that as-built and criteria data can be structurally populated 
in the ABox. Dedicated templates were developed to convert source 
data into RDF. As-built records (activities, results, equipment, 
indicators) are mapped to SOSA classes—Observation, Result, 
Sensor, and ObservableProperty. Design values and specification 
limitations become instances of criteria data. Four main formats 
(IFC, PDF, CSV , and JPEG) are parsed with format-specific scripts 
(e.g., Pandas + RDFLib, PyPDF2 + spaCy + RDFLib, IfcOpenShell, 
OpenCV + RDFLib) and transformed into RDF triples. The source 
files are stored in MinIO, and their URLs are embedded in the RDF 
for traceability. The mapping templates are illustrated in Fig. 9.
After completing the transformation of multisource heterogeneous 
quality data, the converted RDF files were imported into the GraphDB 
graph database along with the TBox Construction_DeTrig_Identifica-
tion_Ontology. Separate named graphs were created to represent the 
ABox and TBox, denoted as the instances graph and the ontology 
graph, respectively. SPARQL queries were implemented to retrieve the 
ABox quality data and traverse the relational logic defined by the TBox. 
This approach enabled automated calculation of quality deviations by 
comparing as-built data and criteria data within the unified semantic 
framework. The pseudocode illustrating the SPARQL queries used for 
this process is provided in Algorithm 1.

Defect Trigger Identification Based on Single Deviation 
Criteria Verification
To achieve comprehensive and accurate identification of quality 
defect triggers, the defect trigger identification workflow should be 
established within the fundamental construction elements of build-
ing projects. According to the Unified Standard for Construction 
Quality Acceptance of Building Engineerings [GB 50300-2013 
(MOHURD 2013)], construction projects are hierarchically divided 
into division works, subdivision works, and subitem works, with 
subitems representing the most basic construction processes. Qual-
ity inspection is conducted at predefined checkpoints and includes 
material acceptance, key construction process inspections (covering 
concealed works, installation work, and measurement work), and 
subitem work acceptance.
Each checkpoint involves extracting as-built data and criteria 
data from QLD to calculate deviations, whose magnitude and 
frequency are analyzed to determine whether they constitute defect 
triggers. Once all subitem works are completed and accepted, the 
entire division work undergoes spot-check acceptance inspections. 
Accordingly, this study proposes that the fundamental unit for 
defect trigger identification is the acquisition and comparison of 
QLD at key inspection points, supporting a quality deviation-based 
approach for automated defect trigger identification. The proposed 
workflow is illustrated in Fig. 10.
According to the evolution mechanisms of defect triggers, a 
quality deviation may constitute a defect trigger when its magni-
tude closely approaches the tolerance limits defined by relevant 
standards or when its frequency becomes abnormally high. To sup-
port identification, three key indicators are proposed: the required 
value, the reference value, and the reference rate. Specifically, a 
quality deviation exceeding the required value is classified as a 
quality defect. When the deviation exceeds the reference value but 
remains within the required value, it is considered a defect trigger. 
Additionally, if the frequency of a deviation surpasses the reference 
rate, it may also be identified as a defect trigger due to its cumulative 
impact. The identification criteria for quality defect triggers are 
illustrated in Fig. 11.
Current AECO industry standards lack a systematic approach 
to reference value. To address this gap, this study introdu -
ces two reference thresholds, drawing on critical alerting con-
cepts from environmental monitoring and industrial quality 
control domains:
1. Reference value: The reference value is established at the 90th 
percentile of historical deviation distributions. This threshold 
ensures that only the most extreme 10% of observed deviations 
are classified as potential defect triggers. The 90th percentile 
of historical deviation distributions has been widely applied in 
SPC, nonparametric SPC, and water quality monitoring ( Lake 
et al. 2002; Qiu 2018; Saghir and Kosztyán 2023). The FHW A 
source has been verified as Burati et al. ( 2003), optimal proce-
dures for quality assurance specifications, FHW A-RD-02-095, 
full payment is made only when the percent within limits (PWL) 
quality level reaches 90 (Bennett and White 2025; Burati et al. 
2003; Federal Aviation Administration 2018).
2. Reference rate: Based on the Pareto principle ( Durdyev and 
Ismail 2012), a 20% occurrence threshold is set for identifying 
systemic risk. Based on statistical analyses of AECO noncon-
formance (NCR) and quality defect records, respectively, Love 
et al. (2018) and Del Solar et al. (2016) reported that a minority of 
quality events or construction stages leads to most defect occur-
rences, approximating the Pareto principle. Accordingly, this 
study adopts the Pareto principle to determine the reference rate. 
If the frequency of similar quality deviations in a specific process 
or inspection batch exceeds 20%, the condition is flagged as a 
potential defect trigger.
