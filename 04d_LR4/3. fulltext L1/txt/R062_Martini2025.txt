through a distributed co‑simulation approach for crisis management

Abstract
The increasing frequency and severity of significant risks to public safety posed by natural disasters or human-induced events 
have underscored a critical need for evaluating the vulnerability of urban regions with a focus on their essential infrastruc-
tures. This paper presents a novel methodology for the virtual representation of infrastructure vulnerabilities and functional 
impairments during hazard situations. Thereby, focus is on the mapping of interdependencies among critical infrastructure 
systems and the cascading effects that can arise from failures within these heterogeneous sectors by means of a digital twin 
representation. An integration of simulation models for urban infrastructure components, particularly in relation to the 
built environment and emergency response systems is introduced. Leveraging a modular co-simulation architecture, the 
framework facilitates the analysis of cascading effects across multiple infrastructure systems, such as water, electricity, gas, 
and telecommunications. As a proof-of-concept example, urban flooding due to heavy rainfall is considered to illustrate the 
framework’s capabilities in predicting system states and assessing structural impacts on critical infrastructures as well as 
consequential ramifications for emergency relief units. The findings contribute valuable insights, thereby advertising the 
utilization of the presented methodology in decision-making and training resources aiding the enhancement of the resilience 
of urban environments against both natural and intentional threats.
Keywords Digital Urban Twin · Socio-technical systems · Distributed co-simulation · Crisis management
1 Introduction
Evaluating the susceptibility of urban regions and their 
essential infrastructure to both natural disasters—like floods, 
intense rainfall, as well as severe storms—and human-
induced events—such as terrorist attacks—has become a 
vital area of research in recent years. This is largely due to 
the global rise in the frequency and severity of these inci-
dents. Failures or disruptions in critical infrastructure can 
lead to serious consequences, including ongoing shortages 
and major threats to public safety. Of particular concern are 
the hidden interdependencies and unexpected cascading 
effects among various critical infrastructure systems, which 
may endanger citizens.
To improve the resilience of urban areas and their criti-
cal infrastructure, it is crucial for policymakers, agencies, 
governments, and infrastructure providers to gain a deep 
understanding of not only the vulnerabilities and failure risks 
of individual infrastructures but also the potential impacts

Previous research efforts have identified risks and hazards

marize the current state of knowledge and challenges related 
to community resilience and system interdependencies.
1.1 Research gap
Although researchers have documented some interdepend -
encies among critical infrastructure systems, these findings 
have rarely been applied to integrate simulations of the 
components within an urban system. Some projects have 
created tools like SAT (Severity Assessment Tool) (Hayes 
et al. (2022)) and CReDo (Climate Resilience Demonstrator) 
(Wang et al. (2022)) to assess and visualize these interde-
pendencies for decision-making purposes. However, specific 
functionalities of different critical infrastructures and the 
built environment are typically modeled through data-driven 
methods rather than engineering approaches, which may fail 
to recognize the complexity of cascading effects in disas-
ter scenarios. Furthermore, coupled simulations primarily 
focus on essential infrastructures like water, electricity, and 
gas networks (e.g., Deshmukh et al. ( 2011); Praks et al. 
(2015), while interdependencies with the built environment 
and emergency response systems are often neglected. In 
this context, there is a need for simulation frameworks that 
facilitate the integration and communication among inter -
disciplinary simulation environments. Previous efforts, such 
as the Interdependent Networked Community Resilience 
Modeling Environment (IN-CORE) (Lindt et al. (2023)), 
mandate the integration of various services, e.g., including 
analyses of buildings with fragility curves and simulations 
of infrastructure networks under various hazard scenarios. 
However, the methods used for simulation are not discussed 
comprehensively, and emergency response systems, which 
are critical for effective training, are omitted.
Regarding the efficient realization of interdisciplinary 
co-simulations, Restel (2023) introduces the concept of 
SimulationOps that proposes a collaboration process (from 
creating and combining simulation components to deploy -
ing, configuring, and running simulations). This process uti-
lizes the concept of distributed “as-a-service” platforms to 
achieve its objectives. Numerous co-simulation models and 
platforms have been proposed. The most widely recognized 
concept is the High Level Architecture (HLA), publicly 
available as a set of standards (IEEE (2010)). This concept 
describes a federated system of multiple simulators, along 
with a common runtime infrastructure to facilitate com-
munication and synchronization. One example of a simula-
tion platform that uses the HLA approach in the domain 
of mobility and communication is Eclipse Mosaic (Schrab 
et al. (2023)). HLA-based systems synchronize the steps for 
a simulation in a global fashion but lack something like an 
event stream approach allowing for each service to execute 
its next step independently if the necessary input is avail-
able, which can boost runtime performance. The Functional 
Mock-up Interface (FMI) (Modelica Association (2025)) is a 
standard for exchanging models for tightly coupled co-sim-
ulations based on XML and the C programming language. 
Maestro (Thule et al. (2019)) is one example utilizing FMI 
for co-simulating cyber-physical systems. However, FMI 
does not provide the development and deployment flexibil-
ity to support the iterative development, deployment, and 
usage process. The Cybersecurity & Information Systems 
Information Analysis Center’s approach to “Modeling & 
Simulation as a Service” (MSaaS) (Siegfried et al. (2018)) 
emphasizes the use of cloud computing and service-oriented 
architectures to deliver modeling and simulation capabilities 
effectively. “As-a-service” refers to a self-service approach, 
where users can access and use services on-demand. Gütlein 
and Djanatliev (2020) presents an architecture for MSaaS, 
which focuses on Apache Kafka for communication between 
and coordination of components participating in a simula-
tion. In summary, the existing “simulation-as-a-service” 
approaches already address distribution, on-demand usage, 
or using diverse programming languages, but do not support 
the event streaming approach envisioned here.
1.2 Innovation
To fill these gaps, a new methodology has been established 
to enable the virtual representation of infrastructure vulner-
ability and functional impairments in urban districts during 
hazard situations. It is built upon the concepts presented in 
Martini et al. (2024). Its modular architecture and integra-
tion with the Fraunhofer Open Source SensorThings API 
Server (FROST) (Hertweck et al. (2019)) make it adaptable 
and capable of being expanded to include additional hazard 
scenarios and infrastructure components. This versatility 
enables various applications, such as decision-support tools 
for authorities and infrastructure operators, as well as train-
ing resources in public safety.
The framework integrates multiple simulation modules 
for different critical infrastructures and services, including 
water, electricity, gas, and telecommunications to form a 
comprehensive digital twin representation of key infrastruc-
tures in urban areas. Each module is built on engineering 
models that are based on physical principles, standards, or 
(georeferenced) real-world data, allowing for reliable pre-
dictions about the modeled system’s state under certain 
conditions. Analyses of scenario-specific building damage 
using probabilistic engineering models as well as an agent-
based simulation of emergency operations, modeling the 
rapid responses of emergency units to critical incidents are 
also integrated in the framework. This combination allows

The co-simulation platform emphasizes a strict separation

2 Co‑simulation framework
The following section presents the design of a distributed co-
simulation framework that has been developed to facilitate 
interdisciplinary research and development.
2.1 Design objectives
The primary objective of the development effort is to estab-
lish a framework which enables time-based co-simulation 
in a distributed environment. The framework is designed 
to support the development and utilization of simulation 
components by an interdisciplinary team of researchers and 
developers. Integrating existing simulator systems, each with 
its own user interfaces, simulation control, and communica-
tion facilities poses a major challenge.
The framework is designed to be scalable and flexible, 
possessing the following key characteristics: (i) simplicity 
of use and extensibility, (ii) capability for the development 
of complex simulation scenarios, and (iii) support for the 
execution of such scenarios on local machines, cloud-based 
infrastructures, or hybrid environments.
The initial considerations have been based on existing 
approaches such as serverless (stateful functions), event-
driven, and service-based architectures. A central objective 
is to completely separate application and platform function-
ality. Core data processing functionality is to be separated 
from the following aspects.
• Control flow: simulators are required to surrender, for 
instance, time control facilities, so that they could be 
controlled from the outside.
• Data flow: simulators are to relinquish control of data 
exchange to facilitate their seamless integration into flex-
ible data processing pipelines.
• Communication technology: a simulator’s algorithmic 
component needs to be separated from any integrated 
communication mechanisms, to facilitate its deployment 
in diverse runtime environments.
• Deployment specifics: a simulator’s functionality needs 
to enable cross-platform execution, allowing it to run on 
various environments, including local machines, cloud 
services, and hybrid configurations.
2.2 Framework components
The framework comprises four major types of components: 
(i) processing units, (ii) services, (iii) applications, and (iv) 
orchestrator(s).
Processing units implement simulation algorithms or 
other data processing tasks as passive and independent 
building blocks providing a common Application Program-
ming Interface (API) to facility controlling their execution 
from the outside. Processing units can be written in diverse 
programming languages.
Services encapsulate processing units and provide life 
cycle management and inter-process communication inter -
faces. They offer mechanisms for creating unit instances, 
initializing them for a simulation run, and invoking their 
functions to apply external data, execute subsequent steps, or 
query their state. This enables running multiple simulation 
algorithms in a coordinated manner in distributed environ-
ments and allows processing units to participate in multiple 
concurrent simulations for various users, objectives, and 
parameter sets.
Applications describe simulation scenarios. In a typi-
cal co-simulation scenario, services are interdepend-
ent, i.e., the output of one service serves as the input of 
another. Figure  1 illustrates this principle in form of a 
block diagram based on Fundamental Modeling Concepts 
(FMC) (Knöpfel et al. (2006)) using an example of four

interdependent services collectively executing the steps 
of a simulation run. Applications specify the required 
simulation services, their dependencies, initial state con-
figurations, and simulation parameters, including model 
time, simulation duration, step widths, and other relevant 
settings.
Orchestrators accept application descriptions and initiate 
and execute corresponding simulation runs. Consequently, 
an orchestrator establishes connections to the requisite ser -
vices and invokes them sequentially (until the application’s 
termination condition is met), while ensuring that data nec-
essary for a service to execute a specific step are retrieved 
and provided in a timely manner.
Given that the present research primarily focuses on con-
necting simulators from diverse domains, the following sec-
tions concentrate on how the concept of processing units can 
be used to implement the necessary functionalities.
2.3 Processing units
Processing units are designed to implement simulation algo-
rithms as self-contained, passive components that are inde-
pendent of a specific execution environment. At its core, 
a unit is essentially a function f (stateti−k
, ti) ↦ stateti
 (step 
function) that takes a model state and a corresponding point 
in model time and produces a subsequent state. There is a 
well-defined interface for initializing and retrieving model 
data and for applying external events to a model state, ena-
bling the units to be used in a cooperative environment. A 
processing unit instance manages its own local model inde-
pendently of any other unit. It may use external resources 
such as databases to store and retrieve its model data. Units 
may also use shared read-only resources such as lookup ser-
vices. Units can be written in any programming language 
that has a language binding to the framework; currently this 
includes JavaScript, Python, Java, Kotlin, and C#.
A processing unit implements the following API.
• init (options): initializes the state of the unit with 
the given options.
• apply (time, data): applies the given external 
data to the state of this unit for the given point in model 
time.
• step (time): computes state changes for the given 
point in model time.
• query (time, filter): queries the state for the 
given point in model time using a unit-specific query/
filter expression.
• release (): releases all resources held by this unit.
An API contract exists that stipulates additional conditions 
under which a unit’s functions are executed. For instance, 
the init function is called at most once before any other 
function; the apply function may be called between 0 and n 
times per time step directly before the corresponding step 
function; the step function is called exactly once for a time 
step; the query function is called at most once per pair 
(time/filter); and the release function is executed at most 
once after any other API call.
Depending on data flow patterns, processing units can be 
classified into three types.
• Generators produce data, they, however, do not accept 
external events. Examples include clocks, replay, inter -
polator, or control units.
• Collectors accept external events, however, do not pro-
duce any data. Examples include consoles, archives, dis-
plays, or monitors.
• Transformers do both, they accept external events and 
produce data. Examples include simulators, filters, and 
data converters.
2.4 Unit interaction
Processing units are self-contained, that is, they have the 
freedom to model and represent data internally without 
external constraints. However, to facilitate data exchange 
with other units, a common data model must be established 
or a mechanism has to be provided for converting data 
between different models.

3.1 Model overview
Digital models with simulation capabilities of the distinct 
components of infrastructure networks present in the con-
sidered urban area—namely gas, power supply, drinking 
water, and telecommunications—alongside emergency 
response and building vulnerability, constitute the build-
ing blocks of a digital twin representation of critical infra-
structure in urban contexts for evaluating its interdepend-
ent functionality and resilience. Consequently, a dedicated 
simulation tool is required for each component, tailored to 
fulfill its specific requirements and operational functions. 
Here, modeling of supply infrastructure operations and 
assessment of the building vulnerability is based on physi-
cal principles like mechanics, thermodynamics, electrody -
namics, and hydrodynamics as well as derived engineer -
ing models. For the dynamic simulation of the emergency 
response, an agent-based modeling approach is employed. 
Reconnaissance is exemplified by input gathered through 
spontaneous volunteers, while a suitable visualization 
dashboard is introduced. Each tool operates autonomously, 
serving as an independent simulation instrument, which 
will be detailed in the subsequent sections for all compo-
nents. This subsection also serves as a demonstration of 
the framework’s capability of and flexibility in incorporat -
ing and connecting versatile, heterogeneous, interdiscipli-
nary building blocks, based on specifically developed or 
existing tools. Hence, operational and implementational 
details, underlying theoretical footing as well as required 
embedding and inputs together with provided outputs are 
summarized for each individual unit to be employed in 
this exemplary multilateral modeling of the urban con-

similarities in physically grounded modeling of supply 
infrastructure systems (e.g., mapping of gas, power, and

3.1.1 Simulation of extreme contingencies in urban gas 
distribution networks
Numerical simulation of natural gas grid operation can pro-
vide useful aid for decision support in economic as well 
as safety considerations. Within current energy sectors, 
natural gas infrastructures still play a prominent role while 
offering a potential vantage point for hydrogen expansion, 
e.g., by step-wise hydrogen blending. Concomitant with its 
importance, the safety of gas supply has particularly been 
shifted into focus due to acts of sabotage as well as political 
sanctions.
The hydraulic gas-network simulation module employed 
in the presented co-simulation framework is developed as a 
Python module predicated on Ganter et al. (2023) as follows.
Natural gas grids can be represented as networks com-
prising nodes—indicative of sources, consumers, junctions, 
etc.—that are interconnected by directed, one-dimensional 
edges symbolizing pipeline segments, compressors, regula-
tors, and valves. The resulting network graph, enriched with 
operational parameters such as source pressures, consumer 
demands, and the characteristics of compressors, regulators, 
and valves, as well as pipeline attributes including lengths, 
diameters, inclinations, and roughness values, constitutes the 
input for the gas module. The dynamics governing natural 
gas, a mixture of gaseous hydrocarbons, are dictated by the 
principles of thermodynamics and hydrodynamics, ensuring 
the conservation of mass and momentum under the influ-
ences of inertia, friction, gravity, and external forces from 
components such as compressors (Ganter et al. (2023)). 
Applying these principles to each pipeline section and each 
node, a system of coupled nonlinear algebraic equations that 
delineate the system state in terms of the average flow rate 
through each section and the pressure at each node can be 
derived (cf. Ganter et al. (2023)).
Upon receiving an attributed gas-network graph as input, 
the gas module automatically constructs the corresponding 
system of nonlinear algebraic equations based on the topol-
ogy and attributes of the input graph. To ensure mathemati-
cal solvability, the viability of the input graph is scrutinized, 
and the flow-regulator concept introduced by Ganter et al. 
(2023) is employed to facilitate physical solutions that may 
diverge significantly from design-point operations. For 
enhanced robustness, the solution is derived through an 
iterative linearization process combined with a least-squares 
optimization method.
The incorporation of the gas-network simulation module 
into the co-simulation framework builds on the deliberations 
presented in Martini et al. (2024). The module is imple-
mented as a simulator unit transforming gas-network-graph 
data to corresponding numerical values for the hydraulic 
quantities describing the flow characteristics within the net-
work (i.e., flow rates and pressures). Hence, specific data 
converter units are required when connecting the gas-net-
work simulation module to other interdependent simulation 
modules in order to, e.g., interpret received data on power 
supply in terms of the operational state attributes of power-
dependent gas-network elements or to map the resulting 
numerical values of the gas-flow characteristics to the sup-
ply status of gas consumers.
3.1.2 Simulation of urban power distribution grids
The simulation of electrical power grids requires granular 
modeling of their network components to detect critical 
infrastructure contingencies due to damaged power assets 
or insufficient power supply. In the context of power system 
analysis, the network is modeled as nodes (bus bars) that 
host generators and consumers, along with edges (branches) 
that represent lines and transformers. To determine volt-
age amplitudes and angles, from which the power flows 
and system losses can be derived, this configuration can 
be expressed as a mathematical problem known as the load 
flow problem. In this context, power flow equations define 
the balance between power injections and consumptions at 
each bus bar. In addition to the interdependencies of the 
individual elements, other relevant parameters, such as the 
impedance and length of the lines and the nominal power 
of transformers and generators, are incorporated into these 
equations (cf. Grainger et al. (2016)).
The variety of analysis and simulation tools for power 
systems capable of solving the load flow problem is reviewed 
in Martini et al. (2024). The presented power grid module 
uses Pandapower (Thurner et al. (2018)) for its easy integra-
tion into existing Python code and performance in complex 
networks (Grishin et al. (2024)). In addition, Pandapower 
has a standard type library for common elements of the 
power grid with standard type parameters.
The power grid module either uses a Pandapower network 
model as initial input or generates one based on geodata. To 
create a computable network model, the geodata must meet 
specific requirements: (i) The data must represent a coherent 
network model in which all nodes and edges are connected. 
(ii) Consumption must be covered either by generators or 
external networks. (iii) All type parameters of the elements, 
such as nominal power, resistance, and reactance, must be 
clearly defined. (iv) There must be a defined slack bus bar 
that serves as a reference point for the voltage and power 
balance.
The power system is simulated in an event-oriented way, 
allowing for the representation of load changes and grid 
topology adjustments. After each modification, a load flow 
calculation is performed with a subsequent check for com-
ponent overloads and voltage band violations, adjusting the 
network as needed until all boundary conditions are met.

3.1.3 Simulation of drinking water networks
Drinking water distribution networks are complex systems 
composed of interconnected components. These networks 
can be represented as directed graphs, where the nodes cor-
respond to infrastructure elements, such as junctions, reser-
voirs, and tanks, while the links represent pipeline sections, 
pumps, and valves that connect the nodes. Each network 
component has specific parameters, such as flow rates, pres-
sure levels, and diameters, which are essential for simulat-
ing the system’s behavior. Hydraulic simulations are used 
to accurately model and optimize the performance of these 
networks. The goal of these simulations is to model water 
flow and calculate key parameters, like pressure, velocity, 
and flow rate at different points. These simulations are based 
on fluid dynamics principles, where the flow through each 
pipe follows the laws of conservation of mass and energy.
Sophisticated computational models are employed to sim-
ulate hydraulic conditions, with one of the most widely used 
tools being EPANET (Rossman et al. (2020)). EPANET is an 
open-source software that models both the hydraulic behav-
ior and water quality dynamics of drinking water distribution 
systems. It solves nonlinear algebraic equations based on 
flow equations and applies hydraulic principles to simulate 
the steady-state and dynamic behavior of the network.
EPANET serves as the computational engine for vari-
ous water network simulation platforms. Tools like WNTR 
(Klise et al. (2017)) and QGISRed (Martínez Alzamora et al. 
(2019)) extend EPANET’s capabilities; however, they come 
with limitations, such as the lack of usability for non-pro-
grammers or the requirement to operate within specific envi-
ronments (Martini et al. (2024)). HydroDyn Map was devel-
oped to overcome these challenges, combining EPANET’s 
computational strength with .NET and C# technologies to 
provide a more accessible and user-friendly interface. The 
tool also integrates spatial data by enabling the assignment 
of geographic coordinates to EPANET elements within a 
specific Spatial Reference System (SRS) (Chang (2018)). 
This integration enhances simulation accuracy by incor -
porating real-world geographical data, enabling advanced 
spatial analyses, such as including population density in 
the simulation to assess the system’s efficiency in serving 
different areas. Additionally, HydroDyn Map supports the 
storage of network data and parameters in a PostGIS data-
base (PSC & OSGeo (2023)), making it easier to manage, 
query, and integrate with other data sources. By bridging 
hydraulic simulations with spatial data, this tool provides 
a more seamless and comprehensive approach to modeling 
drinking water distribution networks.
Although initially developed for Windows, the software 
can be adapted to run on any platform compatible with the 
.NET framework. By offering the option to deploy Hydro-
Dyn Map within a Docker container, it ensures complete 
independence from the user’s host architecture. Furthermore, 
the software wrapper supports a range of standard interfaces, 
enhancing interoperability and ease of integration.
The water module in the co-simulation framework uti-
lizes HydroDyn Map as a tool to simulate drinking water 
networks. The simulation module can receive information 
about the operational state of network elements and send 
parameters, such as head, pressure, demand, and demand 
deficit, as output. In order to connect with other modules, 
data converter units are required to enable the water module 
to interpret modifications to its network introduced by other 
modules and to return the updated state of the drinking water 
network.
3.1.4 Simulation of (LTE/5 G) cellular network coverage 
in disaster‑stricken areas
The cellular network communication module aims to 
simulate cellular communication outage based on critical 
infrastructure failure. Cellular networks are a fundamental 
component of modern urban infrastructure, serving critical 
roles for individuals, essential services, and public systems. 
Citizens depend on cellular networks (in the absence of 
Wi-Fi) for receiving warnings, initiating emergency calls, 
and accessing crucial disaster-related information. Criti-
cal infrastructure systems, such as distributed electricity or 
water networks as mentioned before, rely on these networks 
for remote operations and management, ensuring continuous 
functionality even during disruptions. Critical infrastructure 
operators face significant disruptions in the absence of reli-
able cellular communication, as they rely on it to coordinate 
field staff and ensure seamless operations.
Network simulation is a commonly known methodology 
that is adopted not only in research but also widely in the 
private/public sector. It is therefore beneficial for novel co-
simulation approaches to include and integrate scientifically 
validated approaches and tools. This includes, next to sci-
entifically evaluated network-validation approaches (Wru-
lich et al. (2008)), the real-world impact on network models 
and protocols, such as discussed in Jardosh et al. (2005) 
and Hahn et al. (2015). Notably, the simulation of cellu-
lar networks has been variously explored in prior research, 
encompassing link-level and system-level analyses in Rupp 
Environment Systems and Decisions (2025) 45:40 
 Page 9 of 21 40
et al. (2016) or Virdis et al. (2014) based on OMNeT++, as 
well as system-level approaches as presented in Ikuno et al. 
(2010) and Johnson (1999) utilizing MATLAB. However, all 
of these methods are not bound to one specific tool but are 
rather tool-agnostic. Further, coupling various simulation 
tools in an integrated manner to investigate communication 
protocols, networks, and network coverage is not a novel 
approach and has been a successful simulation approach, 
such as in V-to-X related research (Protzmann et al. (2017)), 
next to other research domains. The here-presented network 
simulation module calculates the effects on cellular network 
coverage in the target area that arise from critical infra-
structure failure, such as a large-scale power outage, cable 
destruction, or destruction of buildings. Based on this input, 
the module generates geographical forecasts that resemble 
the remaining coverage within the disaster-stricken area 
and additionally evaluates the citizens’ remaining ability to 
either receive public alerts and warnings or initiate emer -
gency calls (Lukau et al. (2023)) using their cell phones.
The simulation module itself is implemented in a system-
of-systems architecture with multiple integrated subsystems, 
databases, and submodules bound to specific tasks. The sub-
module that is bound to network calculations is the core 
element of the module and includes a code-level variation of 
the NS-3-based discrete-event simulation framework (Riley 
and Henderson (2010)). This submodule recreates the net-
work topology based on real-world infrastructure data that 
contains the positions and configurations of each mobile 
base station in the target area. The source-code variation 
of the NS-3 framework enables the dynamic integration, 
load-balancing, and scalability as well as parallelization 
of large scenarios. It further enhances each simulation run 
with a specific simulation state. This provides the ability to 
dynamically simulate various scenarios and changing net-
work topologies of the public cellular network infrastructure 
and also enables dynamic establishment of the network sce-
narios and signal processing calculations based on chang-
ing scenario requests. The LTE architecture modules and 
signaling models as well as functionalities that are used in 
the NS-3 variation have been introduced and validated in 
Giuseppe et al. (2012) and Baldo et al. (2011).
Integrated into the co-simulation framework, the module 
is implemented as a processing unit with its subservices and 
submodules as self-contained and masked components. It 
is initialized with an internal database of buildings, base 
stations, and their configurations that equally resemble 
their real-world position and configuration in the simulated 
scenario. The simulated base stations are then configured 
with the respective height (with respect to building height), 
transmission power, frequency bands, bandwidth, and cor -
responding frequency channels. In a running co-simulation 
(i.e., the simulation of a blackout in the target area), the 
processing unit internally monitors the power supply state 
at each base station. Upon receiving new power supply 
information the current internal simulation state is applied, 
altering functioning/non-functioning base stations. Since the 
base stations atop the buildings are supplied by the build-
ing’s own power supply system, the power outage directly 
influences the antennas on the roof. As this information 
originates from different processing units (i.e., simulation 
of power distribution grids), it may have been altered by an 
external co-simulation framework converter that translates 
simulation results from electricity-related to communica-
tion-related domains. The applied topological change in 
the network is recalculated by a single internal simulation 
run, resulting in an updated coverage area as a new simula-
tion result. To determine the new coverage area or size of 
each cell per base station, the uplink and downlink between 
simulated user equipment (i.e., smartphones) and function-
ing/non-functioning base stations in the area is simulated. 
The calculated Received Signal Strength Indication (RSSI) 
measured at each user equipment, as well as the simulated 
cell-selection process of each user equipment, define the 
geographical coverage area in which users are predicted to 
either have service or not. The simulation results are served 
back into the co-simulation framework toward coupled pro-
cessing units through dedicated data converter units.
3.1.5 Simulation of the emergency response
In the presented framework, most of the components 
describe different technical systems that are quite entan-
gled with one another, but there is another important aspect 
when modeling failures in the critical infrastructure in order 
to understand their impact on society and daily life. While 
some failures are fixed automatically or can be resolved 
remotely by the operators, other failures must be resolved 
by humans, either because of their complexity or quantity. 
Incorporating this human interaction into the co-simulation 
framework allows for a more comprehensive description of 
the socio-technical systems.
Human interaction involves repairing and maintaining 
parts of the critical infrastructure and affiliated machines as 
well as the work of authorities and organizations responsible 
for public safety. Their simulation is typically achieved with 
Discrete-Event Simulations (DES) or Agent-Based Simula-
tions (ABM).
Discrete-event simulations examine the behavior of 
complex systems by modeling a set of events (e.g., failures, 
emergency calls) that interact with the system at specified 
time points. These simulations operate along a timeline 
of discrete time steps, generating a system state for each 
time step (Mahdiraji et al. (2022)). Agent-based simula-
tions focus on replicating individual behaviors through 
parameterized agents (e.g., people, vehicles) that act as 
state machines by perceiving, processing, and reacting to 
 Environment Systems and Decisions (2025) 45:40
40 Page 10 of 21
their environment (Andrae and Pobuda (2021)). This agent-
level perspective enables a detailed analysis of microscopic 
processes in a complex system, such as compliance with 
response time targets (Bandyopadhyay and Singh (2016)).
There are various simulation tools available for this task: 
The simulation software MATSim (MATSim Contributors 
& MATSim Association ( 2025)) is a multi-agent simula -
tion framework designed for traffic simulation in urban 
areas. It can be used to estimate emergency response times 
based on underlying traffic conditions (Koch et al. (2020)). 
Similarly, SUMO (Alvarez Lopez et al. ( 2018)) is a traf -
fic simulation tool that can be applied to simulate traffic 
incidents and emergency service vehicles (Li et al. (2024)). 
Other agent-based simulation frameworks are provided with 
AnyLogic, NetLogo, and Repast (The AnyLogic Company 
(2025); Wilensky (1999); Argonne National Laboratory 
(2022)). Anylogic is a framework which allows to replicate 
the procedural steps of systems, for example the patient flow 
in an emergency department (Murtaza Sara et al. (2024)). 
NetLogo provides agent-based simulations as a black box, in 
which a patient flow can be modeled through an emergency 
department (Godfrey et al. (2023)). Repast Suite enables 
the agent interaction in a domain-specific point of view to 
reproduce the behavior in certain scenarios (Cabrera et al. 
(2012)).
The emergency response simulation was developed as a 
discrete-event simulation to handle incidents with unknown 
entry time combined with an agent-based simulation 
approach for dynamic representations of first responders, 
vehicles, detectors, etc., and a domain-specific agent defi-
nition of a control center. It is implemented in the Kotlin 
programming language in a domain-driven design (figure 4). 
Incidents stand for failures, accidents, or other events. They 
are detected by some kind of sensors or humans. These 
detectors report the incident to a control center at which 
a number of tactical units are dispatched to resolve the 
incident.
Incidents can be generated within the emergency response 
simulation or are received from other modules of the co-sim-
ulation framework. The detectors obey different policies and 
logic ranging from simple automated alarms to humans call-
ing the emergency hotline. When the incidents are reported 
to the control center, there is a different set of policies and 
logic dealing with them and dispatching available units on 
operations to resolve the incidents. All simulation logic must 
be adapted to the designated modeled area and the applied 
policies.
The emergency response simulation is a processing unit 
as described above. It receives inputs in the form of cloud 
events with information about new incidents, namely their 
properties and locations. The output comprises updated 
information of its entities: incidents, operations, tactical 
units, and depots.
Exemplary, in the application in section  3.3, a heavy 
storm leads to structural problems of buildings and criti-
cal infrastructure, buildings get flooded, streets and bridges 
are impassable, and the electricity and communication net-
work fails in certain areas. Additionally, there are weather-
unrelated incidents which emerge within the emergency 
response simulation based on calibration data of the mod-
eled area. The incidents due to the heavy storm are received 
from external co-simulation modules. For example, flooded 
buildings and impassable streets trigger a set of police, fire, 
and medical emergency units. These tactical units drive to 
the incidents based on current road conditions and possible 
road blocks, then they work on the incidents with their indi-
vidual policies and logic. When the incidents are resolved, 
the units drive back, bring injured people to hospitals or get 
assigned to new operations.
The emergency response simulation allows studying sev-
eral different aspects in a scenario. It can vary by
• different initial conditions, e.g., time of day and spatio-
temporal availability and distribution of resources and 
forces,
• different policies and logic of the simulation agents 
(detectors, control center, units), e.g., from fixed pro-
cesses, control theory, or reinforcement learning,
• different inputs from the other co-simulation modules.
Studying simulations under varying conditions enables a 
better understanding and forecasting of the crisis situation. 
It also helps to evaluate and improve disaster management 
plans, distribution of resources and staff planning, and can 
be used in training environments for staff in operational 
headquarters.

3.1.6 Hazard vulnerability assessment of the built 
environment
Flood hazard assessment of buildings and structures com-
prises various methods and approaches. A comprehensive 
overview that summarizes the current state of the art and 
provides detailed information about the methods is presented 
in Martini et al. (2024); Vetter et al. (2024); Neuhaeuser 
et al. (2024); Rosin et al. (2024).
In the present research, an approach is developed that 
enables the site- and building-specific quantification of flood 
damages and building vulnerabilities. This is accomplished 
using categorizable, generalizable, and scalable physical 
damage models tailored for extensive urban structures but 
applied at the building level. A key aspect of the solution is 
the development of generic building models that reflect typi-
cal characteristics related to structure, materials, and con-
struction methods, thus eliminating the need for specific data 
and calculations for each individual building. This facili-
tates a more efficient assessment of flood impacts on urban 
environments. Vulnerability is assessed for a representative 
building drawn of a portfolio of structures with similar char-
acteristics. The urban mixed-use pattern is categorized into 
various buildings classes, such as typical 19th/20th century 
masonry residential buildings or reinforced concrete admin-
istrative structures built after 1945 (Vetter et al. ( 2024)). 
Variability in geometry, material strength, and other relevant 
parameters within each class is incorporated into the analy-
sis through probabilistic distributions (Neuhaeuser et al. 
(2024)).
The assessment framework comprises three sequential 
modules presented in figure  5. The first module utilizes 
open geodata to categorize buildings and develop geomet-
ric models, while literature research addresses data gaps. 
A parametric modeling approach is employed for simula-
tion-based probabilistic analyses, starting with automated 
identification of building types and the generation of para-
metric base models which are then modified for structural 
analyses (Vetter et al. (2024)). The second module prepares 
structural models for probabilistic analyses and conducts 
the analyses (Neuhaeuser et al. (2024)). The module allows 
for various loading scenarios, including standard loads and 
those resulting from hazardous events, such as flooding, by 
varying flood height and flow velocity. Structural models 
representing specific building classes are derived by con-
sidering uncertainty and adjusting input parameters accord-
ing to statistical distributions. The third module collects and 
evaluates results to derive fragility curves, describing the 
probability of reaching or exceeding certain damage levels 
based on intensity measures (Neuhaeuser et al. (2024)). All 
developed software tools are fully integrated, enabling auto-
matic execution of modules for seamless integration into the 
co-simulation framework. This semi-automated procedure 
facilitates the development of fragility curves for assessing 
building vulnerability in urban contexts, as exemplified in 
Rosin et al. (2024).
To integrate into the co-simulation framework, georef-
erenced classifications of buildings and respective fragil-
ity curves for the building types are submitted. The latter 
are then overlaid with water levels within the co-simulation 
framework. As a result, the damage probability is deter -
mined spatially and event-dependently for each building 
type using the corresponding fragility curve.

Information crowdsourcing is arguably already applied 
routinely for supply infrastructure: consumer reports 
using phone calls, apps, or dedicated websites are a com-

close to the consumers, as for example, in low-voltage 
electricity networks (Al-Kanj et al. (2023)). Crowd-

studied for weather data (Barras et al. (2019); Kempf and

Schnebele et al. (2014)), the structural stability of built 
structures (Tsai et al. ( 2020)), and even social and psy -
chological issues of the population (Biegert et al. ( 2019)).
In this work, the usage of volunteer-submitted informa -
tion as input data for the co-simulation is explored. The 
crowdsourcing is modeled after the setup of smartphone 
apps for volunteer coordination (e.g., Betke et al. (2024); 
Holzhüter et al. (2024)): spontaneous volunteers receive 
dedicated information retrieval tasks and submit situa-
tional information using the app. The volunteer-submitted 
information is collected, processed, optionally visualized, 
and incorporated into the co-simulation.
The volunteer module is triggered when a task for 
information retrieval for an area is received. This task is 
broken down into individual subtasks for each volunteer 
in the corresponding area. Data converter units map the 
reported information accordingly to be used as input for 
other downstream modules.
3.1.8 Data filtering, aggregation, and visualization
The simulation models presented above generate huge 
amounts of data, especially in complex simulation scenarios, 
where multiple critical infrastructures are interconnected. 
While this extensive output is rich in information, it can 
be overwhelming and hinder timely decision-making. To 
manage this complexity effectively, the data need to be bro-
ken down into manageable and relevant subsets. Through 
targeted data aggregation, data filtering and processing, and 
user-specific visualization, stakeholders can filter out key 
insights from the extensive data generated by simulations, 
enabling them to make informed decisions quickly and effi-
ciently (Winter et al. (2024)).
In a complex co-simulation, all simulation modules pro-
duce valuable data that can be used for both external and 
internal purposes. The first step in managing these data is 
internal filtering, which is based on specific domain knowl-
edge and determines what information should be shared. 
This process ensures that only relevant data contributes to 
the creation of a comprehensive digital twin that represents 
the complex simulation scenario. Naturally, the available 
data vary, as each simulation module produces its individual 
output formats. The use of a common data model, the OGC 
SensorThings API (Liang et al. (2021)), a well-established 
open-source data model, overcomes the hurdles of different 
data formats. A dedicated data collection unit, based on the 
FROST API Server (Hertweck et al. (2019)), is responsi-
ble for aggregating all available data. This unit also acts 
as a storage system that provides a complete representation 
of the simulated scenario. However, this extensive collec-
tion often contains excessive information that is not neces-
sary for various use cases. Further processing is therefore 
essential to generate customized visualizations and enable 
targeted information transfer. This is achieved through vari-
ous visualization tools that can be seamlessly integrated into 
the presented framework or used as complementary tools to 
ensure that users receive the most relevant insights for their 
specific needs.
The web-based information system ELD (Elektronische 
Lagedarstellung, engl. Electronic Situation Dashboard) is 
already available within this framework. It links a wide 
range of data to support decision-making and is specifically 
designed to assist staff in crisis management by provid-
ing the necessary tools for effective analysis and response. 
Based on open standards, the ELD enables easy integration 
of external data sources and facilitates the collection, man-
agement, and visualization of raw data, thereby increasing 
its versatility (Fraunhofer IOSB (2025, 2022)). Within the 
co-simulation, the ELD serves as a visualization tool, as 
one of its strengths is its support for data with spatial refer -
ences for integration and visualization on maps. This capa-
bility ensures that users can effectively visualize complex
