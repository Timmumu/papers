Abstract
While Digital Twins offer transformative potential, their efficacy for real-time control
is constrained by the slow data acquisition and the high computational intensity re-
quired to process raw datasets like point clouds. This paper identifies these critical
bottlenecks—specifically the latency between data capture and actionable insight—and
proposes a refined theoretical framework for near-real-time automated progress monitoring
and quality reporting. Building on the findings of the NORMENG project and informing
the subsequent AutoGreenTraC project, this research synthesizes state-of-the-art advance-
ments in reality capture, including LIDAR, SfM-MVS, and 360-degree vision. The study
highlights a fundamental divergence in stakeholder requirements: the need for millimeter-
level precision in quality control versus the demand for high-velocity documentation for
progress monitoring. A key innovation presented is the shift toward neural rendering
techniques to bypass the computational delays of traditional photogrammetry and enable
immediate on-site visualization. By structuring a tiered processing hierarchy that combines
lightweight edge analysis for immediate safety and progress monitoring with asynchronous
high-fidelity Digital Twin updates, the framework aims to establish a single source of truth.
Keywords: Digital Twin; automated progress monitoring; reality capture; computer vision;
AI; robotics; UAS; point cloud; edge AI
1. Introduction—The Shift from Manual to Digital and Automated
The construction industry, a cornerstone of global infrastructure development, fre-
quently encounters challenges in project management, often stemming from inefficient
progress monitoring combined with lacking and reactive quality reporting measures and
rather descriptive productivity assessment techniques [1]. This inefficiency contributes to
significant cost overruns, schedule delays, and a staggering $570 billion in direct rework
costs annually within the trillion-dollar construction industry, alongside an additional
$440 billion in indirect costs [ 2,3]. While the theoretical foundations of Digital Twins
(DT) and Building Information Modeling (BIM)—pioneered decades ago [ 4,5]—promise
transformative potential, their practical efficacy for near-real-time construction control has
been severely constrained by two primary bottlenecks: the slow velocity of physical data
acquisition, reliant on labor-intensive manual or scanning methods prone to delays and
errors [1,6,7], and the high computational intensity required for processing vast, unstruc-
tured datasets like point clouds [ 2,8]. These substantial financial implications highlight

a critical need for advanced technological integration to enhance project oversight and
operational efficiency.
This shift from manual to automated oversight is driven by the need for objective
performance benchmarks, as seen in the NORMENG project. While the project utilized
high-tech modalities like LIDAR and UAS photogrammetry to move away from the subjec-
tive descriptive productivity assessments, it encountered a critical reality: simply capturing
data is insufficient for real-time control. The “norming” process—calculating constructed
volumes and labor efficiency—was hindered by the sheer latency of processing these vast
datasets, effectively turning a high-tech solution back into a slow, reactive one. Conse-
quently, the rationale for this paper’s focus on processing speed stems directly from these
field experiences, where the goal of “instantaneous” norming was unmet due to current
computational constraints.
It is expected that real-time monitoring technologies will deliver comprehensive oper-
ational visibility of the construction processes through an integrated digital infrastructure.
Progress monitoring integrated with DT, IoT sensors, visual-based data collection processes,
and technologies is expected to enable data-driven decision-making that directly impacts
schedule and cost control [9]. The development of advanced, automated systems for real-
time progress monitoring and quality reporting remains a significant challenge within
the construction industry [6]. Traditional methods, largely reliant on manual inspections
and reporting, are time-consuming and susceptible to human error, but with the lack of
educated and reliable expert knowledge, the construction industry is in dire need of a
switch to digital technologies [6,10].
Within the scope of this research, “near-real-time” is operationally defined as a spec-
trum of processing latency dictated by a hybrid edge-cloud architecture, ranging from
milliseconds for critical on-site safety interventions to minutes and hours for daily progress
verification. This stands in contrast to the traditional asynchronous processing and manual
reporting, where the identification of issues depends on the supervisor’s capability to
detect issues and assess the level of completion without complex assessments or, on the
other hand, analysis of vast unstructured datasets with currently available data analy-
sis processes, which frequently require hours to days. Consequently, “reporting” in this
framework encompasses a multi-modal delivery of insights tailored to these timeframes,
including instantaneous automated safety alarms, rapid on-site 3D visual deviations, and
continuous cloud-based DT dashboards.
Although digital processes are anticipated to provide seamless access to project data
and facilitate expedited decision-making, their practical implementation frequently encoun-
ters substantial obstacles in achieving real-time data integration and generating actionable
insights. This consequently impedes timely and automated progress monitoring, effective
discrepancy and quality reporting, and accurate productivity assessments. Essentially,
the current state of digitalization often falls short of its anticipated benefits, particularly
in delivering the promised swift and precise progress monitoring capable of mitigating
project schedule delays and cost overruns [7].
Currently, in the construction industry, there is a paradigm shift occurring. While
construction monitoring and control are still relying on labor-intensive manual methods,
they are slowly transitioning to automated, vision-based sensing technologies to improve
accuracy and timeliness in project management [1], aiming to provide a single source of
truth. Here, in the paper, the single source of truth is used as a presentable, reviewable,
and documented result of the construction process, which can be an information provider
immediately during the construction process but also further on down the line, where the
construction process is not visible anymore. Still, digitalization technologies are lagging
behind the expectations of their users.

2. Context and Motivation
2.1. NORMENG Project as Problem Identification Phase
The NORMENG project was launched to tackle this urgent requirement by developing
an automated system for resource standardization in energy-efficient construction. In a
situation where a standardization framework is missing, discrepancies in productivity and
material consumption are to be expected. To resolve these challenges, the project comprises
two primary phases: the initial one conducted in-depth industry studies to evaluate pre-
vailing practices and devise a reliable method for estimating material quantities, workforce
demands, and machinery capacities, thereby establishing the groundwork for precise, de-
pendable, and versatile standards. Specifically, the NORMENG project aimed to transition
from manual, subjective productivity assessments to objective, data-driven “norming”—the
calculation of precise labor and material efficiency based on digital evidence. However, the
practical application of LIDAR, UAS photogrammetry, and continuous monitoring revealed
a significant disparity between data collection and data utility. While these modalities suc-
cessfully captured the “as-built” state, the high computational intensity and manual effort
required to process these results for norming purposes created a feedback loop that was
too slow for active site management. This technical realization—that the limits of existing
digital workflows were obstructing the goal of real-time productivity measurement—serves
as the primary catalyst for the broader technical framework proposed in this research.
A pivotal component of the second phase entailed the development of a pilot construc-
tion site in Sošice, Croatia, erected solely for the NORMENG project (Figures 1 and 2). This
controlled environment facilitated the evaluation of methodologies for progress and quan-
tity monitoring, alongside norm standardization, with emphasis on real-time tracking of
on-site activities using various technologies like LIDAR, UASs, handheld cameras, etc. The
Sošice pilot underscored both the strengths and shortcomings of these approaches in quan-
tifying constructed volumes and formulating contemporary standards for energy-efficient
construction. The initiative concluded with practical field trials in collaboration with con-

2.2. AutoGreenTraC Project as Solution Developing Phase
The relationship between the NORMENG and AutoGreenTraC projects is one of
sequential evolution: NORMENG established the empirical requirements for construc-
tion “norming” but exposed the critical limitations of current digital workflows, while
AutoGreenTraC serves as the technological response to those limitations. Specifically, NOR-
MENG demonstrated that while high-precision data could be captured, the latency between
acquisition and reporting to stakeholders—investors and construction firms—rendered the
data reactive rather than proactive. Building on these documented limitations, AutoGreen-
TraC was launched to develop a streamlined data flow strategy. Its primary objective is to
reduce the temporal gap between site capture and actionable reporting by moving away
from traditional, heavy computational processes toward the tiered edge-cloud architecture
described in this framework.
While the NORMENG project confirmed the capabilities and accuracy of SfM-MVS,
it also exposed its limitations regarding processing time and the inability to provide
immediate feedback for active site management. AutoGreenTraC shifts the research focus
from static resource standardization to dynamic, accelerated transfer of precise information
from the physical site to the DT.
The project integrates advanced data acquisition technologies—including autonomous
UAVs, 360-degree vision, LIDAR, and IoT sensors—with AI-driven computer vision al-

gorithms designed to automate the detection of construction elements and materials. By

3. Problem Statement: Digitalization Expectations vs. Reality
The architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) industry is currently in a trans-
formational phase characterized by the need to rapidly digitize physical assets and to
integrate “Construction 4.0” methodologies [4,5]. Despite these advancements, widespread
adoption of digital solutions for automated progress monitoring and quality assurance
remains limited [8].
The research demonstrates that construction professionals have high expectations for
digitalization technologies in progress monitoring and quality reporting. Here, the focus is
on ease of use, real-time monitoring, and data accuracy [13–15]. However, implementation
challenges, particularly high technological costs for specialized hardware and software,
complexities in managing voluminous point cloud data [16,17], and organizational resis-
tance stemming from steep learning curves in data processing [ 18,19] create significant
gaps between expectations and actual deployment [ 4,8]. The research suggests success-
ful digitalization requires not merely technology adoption but integrated organizational
change, comprehensive training, and clear value demonstration.
The analysis reveals a clear divergence in stakeholder requirements: a rigorous de-
mand for millimeter-level precision in quality reporting, predominantly addressed by
terrestrial and mobile LIDAR systems [20–22], contrasts with an escalating need for high-
velocity, streamlined documentation for progress monitoring, currently dominated by
360-degree camera solutions [21,23,24]. SfM-MVS provides a hybrid approach that balances
photorealistic visual fidelity with moderate geometric accuracy (typically sub-centimeter
under optimal conditions), though its performance remains highly sensitive to environmen-
tal factors, such as illumination, surface texture, and image overlap, as well as processing
parameters [25,26].
To resolve technical ambiguity regarding “real-time” monitoring, this framework
distinguishes three distinct latency components:
• Capturing latency: The time required for physical site data acquisition. While contem-
porary TLS has reduced this from weeks to hours, it remains a bottleneck for daily
monitoring compared to 360◦ cameras.
• Processing latency: The time required for data registration, denoising, and AI-driven
semantic extraction. This includes the “stitching” of point clouds, which can exceed
the capture time itself (e.g., 5 h of capture vs. 8 h of processing).
• Integration latency: The “Information Gap”—the time required to align processed
data with BIM and update the Digital Twin for stakeholder decision-making.
Our framework specifically targets the reduction in processing and integration latency,
acknowledging that capturing latency is often dictated by hardware and site accessibility.
In Section 3.1, the authors present the digitalization process that is currently the
industry standard with its positive and negative sides, whereas in Section 3.2, the users’
(construction industry) expectations are presented. These two aspects of the digitalization

processes are usually not well aligned when it comes to the needs of progress monitoring

3.1. Digitalization Process
3.1.1. Light Detection and Ranging (LIDAR): The Benchmark for Geometric Precision
LIDAR remains the standard for geometric accuracy in construction. Operating
on the principle of emitting pulsed laser light and measuring the time-of-flight for its
return, LIDAR systems generate highly dense point clouds with millimeter-level precision,
capturing intricate details of “as-built” conditions [2,6].
Terrestrial laser scanning and manual photogrammetric documentation, while geo-
metrically precise, are inherently labor-intensive—requiring multiple scan positions and
setups—discontinuous, and often disruptive to ongoing site operations [ 27–30]. TLS has
historically been a time-consuming process, often requiring several days for the compre-
hensive scanning of large facilities [ 27–29]. Solely for the Sošice experimental building,
a scanning protocol took from 1 to 5 min per scanning position (Figure 3). Here, for the
scanning protocol, the construction stopped for at least 15–50 min for the scanning session.
On the other hand, the LIDAR scanning project shown in Figure 4 is a construction site
stretching over 8000 m 2 with six partially completed houses, which took a solid 5 h of
recording and more than 8 h of stitching. Nevertheless, recent technological advancements
have significantly recalibrated expectations regarding the speed of data acquisition. While
conventional surveying methodologies historically necessitated weeks for extensive site
documentation, contemporary TLS systems are now capable of achieving equivalent data
capture within a timeframe ranging from hours to a few days.

Despite its precision, LIDAR faces resistance due to several inherent limitations. The
most significant is data heaviness [ 16,31]. A 20 GB file(s) for a single section is not un-
common, creating massive display and transfer issues [17]. Handling these massive point
clouds requires specialized hardware and software, often creating a bottleneck where only
a few “power users” can access and manipulate the data.
Another limitation is occlusion. LIDAR is a line-of-sight technology, meaning it can
only measure what it can “see.” Complex construction sites with clutter, equipment, and
temporary structures require many scan positions (setups) to avoid “shadows” in the data,
increasing field time (Figure 5). Experts often must plan scan locations meticulously to
ensure complete coverage, adding to the logistical complexity of the operation [30,32].

3.1.2. SfM-MVS and Photogrammetry: The Visual-Geometric Hybrid
SfM-MVS algorithms enable the reconstruction of 3D geometry from 2D image se-
quences by first estimating camera poses and a sparse point cloud via SfM, followed by
dense reconstruction via MVS [25,26,33]. This methodology has seen increased adoption,
driven by the proliferation of unmanned aerial vehicles and high-quality consumer cam-
eras, which democratize access to 3D data generation [14]. Nevertheless, for expert users,
SfM-MVS occupies a specialized niche, primarily distinguished by its visual fidelity and
its capacity for rapid large-area coverage, rather than its absolute geometric precision.
While photogrammetry offers visual richness and broad coverage, it inherently struggles
with accurately delineating model boundaries and ground surfaces, particularly impacting
analyses requiring precise structural information [34,35].
The quantitative reliability of SfM-MVS exhibits considerably greater variability com-
pared to Light Detection and Ranging, being largely contingent upon environmental factors
such as illumination, surface texture, and the degree of image overlap. In controlled bench-
mark investigations, for instance, mapping a 20 m trench, SfM-MVS utilizing drone imagery
achieved satisfactory scaling accuracy and horizontal georeferencing when supported by
robust Global Navigation Satellite System data [36,37]. Nevertheless, vertical translation
errors can be substantial, potentially reaching up to 8 m in the absence of established
ground control points [ 38,39]. This intrinsic unpredictability contributes to a cautious
approach among specialists, particularly regarding the exclusive reliance on SfM-MVS for
critical vertical measurements or the verification of fabricated components.
The integration of LIDAR for precise geometric measurements and photogrammetry
for high-fidelity visual textures is increasingly recognized as an effective hybrid approach
across diverse construction monitoring applications [2,6,35]. Within this integrated frame-
work, LIDAR instrumentation provides precise spatial measurements, forming a founda-
tional geometric representation, while photogrammetric methods contribute rich, detailed
visual information, thereby augmenting the geometric data with high-resolution texture
and appearance (Figure 6).

A significant challenge for experts employing SfM-MVS pipelines is the high compu-
tational intensity of the data processing workflow. Unlike LIDAR, which directly acquires

3D point coordinates via time-of-flight laser ranging, SfM first estimates camera poses and
a sparse point cloud through feature matching across images, while subsequent MVS den-
sification derives depth maps algorithmically from pixel disparities (parallax) in numerous
overlapping views [25,26,40]. This inherent computational demand is considerable [40–42].
Achieving high-fidelity densification in specialized software, such as Metashape or PIX4D,
consequently mandates substantial Graphics Processing Unit resources and can incur
processing durations extending from several hours to multiple days for extensive datasets.
3.1.3. 360-Degree Cameras: The Velocity of Documentation
360◦ cameras have emerged as a leading tool for efficient construction progress moni-
toring, particularly valued for their rapid panoramic image capture from single stations
despite introducing higher noise and variability in 3D models compared to terrestrial laser
scanning [43,44].
In this chapter, 360◦ camera devices are considered as data-capturing devices for 360◦
SfM-MVS reconstruction, where 360◦ videos or photos are used for the structuring of sparse
or dense point clouds using SfM-MVS protocols. Besides these, 360◦ cameras can also be
used for photographic documentation without any complex processing; they are mainly
used for immediate processing of images or within SLAM-based mapping. These, however,
are not in the scope of this review.
Although these cameras do not offer the metrological precision characteristic of LI-
DAR systems, their rapid data acquisition capabilities, operational simplicity, and cost-
effectiveness render them exceptionally suitable for the frequent documentation require-
ments prevalent in general contracting. This technology has fundamentally redefined
expectations regarding “site visibility,” transitioning industry practices from infrequent
photographic documentation to extensive, immersive virtual site inspections.
The primary driver for adopting 360 ◦ cameras is their substantially reduced data
acquisition time compared to conventional multi-view photogrammetry, which requires
numerous overlapping images from multiple viewpoints, and terrestrial laser scanning,
which necessitates multiple stationary positions to ensure comprehensive coverage and
mitigate occlusions [30,32,43,44]. Empirical investigations demonstrate that 360-camera
solutions can expedite site documentation by a factor of five relative to other common
methods and achieve a tenfold increase in speed over manual photographic processes
(Figure 6).
This efficiency stems from the device’s capacity to acquire full 360◦ panoramic imagery
from a single vantage point, thereby substantially minimizing the requisite number of
acquisitions for comprehensive site coverage [ 44]. Despite their operational simplicity
and elevated data density, these cameras frequently yield 3D models exhibiting elevated
noise levels and geometric variability relative to terrestrial laser scanning and multi-view
photogrammetry [43]. Nevertheless, recent innovations—including AI-augmented 360 ◦
cameras leveraging wall texture classification, image segmentation, and feature-extraction
algorithms—have yielded enhanced accuracy and reliability for automated construction
progress monitoring [23].
3.2. User Expectations
In the context of progress monitoring, quality control, and productivity assessment,
the expectations from the digitalization of construction processes often diverge significantly
from the observed realities, particularly due to challenges in data acquisition, processing,
and integration for actionable insights. Contemporary expert practitioners increasingly
prioritize integrated ecosystems that facilitate a seamless continuum of data capture, analyt-
ical processing, and actionable output, moving beyond the demand for isolated, standalone
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
Buildings 2026, 16, 1448 10 of 22
tools. Investigations into user expectations consistently underscore pivotal requirements
that extend beyond mere hardware specifications, with a pronounced emphasis on interop-
erability, user experience, and process automation [45–47].
3.2.1. The “Single Source of Truth” Expectation
Users are fatigued by data silos where information is trapped in proprietary formats or
disconnected platforms. The expectation is that reality capture data (point clouds/meshes)
must integrate directly into the Common Data Environment (CDE) to enable interoperabil-
ity [48–50] and clash detection [49,50].
In this framework, the single source of truth is not merely a collaborative goal but
a systemic architecture built upon the ISO 19650-2 [ 51] framework for a Common Data
Environment (CDE). It utilizes a data-centric model where reality capture data and BIM
objects are linked via unique identifiers (GUIDs), ensuring data is stored exactly once to
prevent redundancies. Governance is enforced through automated status gateways that
manage the transition of data from “captured” to “verified,” providing an immutable
provenance that manual, document-centric methods lack. This architecture ensures that
“firm” information is always presentable to stakeholders, as every reported metric is directly
traceable to a verified dataset within the CDE.
3.2.2. Quick Access to Information and Proactive Quality Reporting
Construction projects are burdened by complex activity interactions that often lead
to delays and increased costs. Consequently, there is a growing expectation for artificial
intelligence to automate the labor-intensive data processing workflows, thereby enhancing
productivity [47] and enabling experts to focus on advanced analytics and strategic decision-
making [46,47]. This automation would mitigate the time-consuming and labor-intensive
nature of manual data analysis, which often involves organizing and localizing raw vi-
sual data [8], but the requested information often comes with a lag, impeding real-time
adjustments and proactive interventions necessary for efficient project management [49].
3.2.3. Ease of Use and Precision of the Information
The primary challenge lies in the current inability to seamlessly and automatically
translate complex, dynamic site conditions into structured, actionable data for immedi-
ate assessment of progress and quality, leading to reactive instead of proactive project
management [52,53].
Many studies have focused on applying novel technologies like laser scanning, com-
puter vision, and radio frequency identification to achieve real-time automated monitoring
and control, yet significant challenges persist in their practical application within construc-
tion projects.
4. Technological Advancements to Data Capture and Analysis
Rather than suggesting the replacement of existing hardware, this section evaluates
the specific processing requirements of currently deployed sensors to determine how they
must be reconfigured within a near-real-time control loop. When it comes to tracking the
construction process in the digital surroundings (recorded construction), progress moni-
toring depends exclusively on documented evidence of completed work, whereas quality
control during the construction process relies on the assessors’ ingenuity in exploiting
available technologies.
The present study focuses on advancements in monitoring:
• Structural element completeness—automated verification that physical components
(e.g., columns, beams, or masonry walls) have been completed as per design.
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
Buildings 2026, 16, 1448 11 of 22
• Material placement verification—confirming that specific materials are correctly posi-
tioned according to design specifications.
• Construction accuracy and alignment—ensuring that the physical structure matches
the geometric design.
• Surface quality assessment—the inspection of the “as-built” finish, such as checking
for cracks, flatness, or curing quality.
• Progress quantification metrics—assigning measurable units, such as cubic meters
of concrete or square meters of masonry finished, to the raw recorded construction
site data.
• Real-time defect detection—detection of construction processes that may cause de-
fects (i.e., wrong placement of formwork) or detection of faults in the construction
process (i.e., missing connection elements in the wall formwork or missing supporting
elements in ceiling formwork).
• Productivity metrics—tracking the efficiency of labor and machinery in real time,
providing data-driven insights into the velocity of construction activities.
This section presents an exhaustive review of the state-of-the-art technological ad-
vancements engineered to dismantle these bottlenecks. The analysis is bifurcated into
two primary domains: (1) technologies that accelerate the physical collection of field
data, moving towards autonomous, ubiquitous, and continuous capture workflows; and
(2) technologies that accelerate the cognitive processing of this data, leveraging advanced
artificial intelligence (AI), neural rendering, and edge computing to transform raw sensor
inputs into actionable semantic understanding in near real time. By examining these do-
mains, we establish the technological mandate for a modernized, data-driven construction
control loop.
4.1. Technologies to Speed Up Data Collection
4.1.1. Autonomous Mobile Robotics (AMR) and Quadruped Systems
Mobile ground robots, particularly quadruped platforms such as Boston Dynamics
Spot and ANYbotics ANYmal, have emerged as promising solutions for autonomous,
repeatable reality capture on active construction sites. These platforms offer several ad-
vantages over traditional manual inspection: they can navigate complex terrain, operate
in hazardous environments, carry multiple sensor payloads, and perform scheduled au-
tonomous missions without direct human supervision.
Yang et al. [52] proposed a cyber-physical systems framework for construction progress
monitoring, employing mobile robots as primary data collectors. It integrates robotic
platforms into ecosystems, combining reality capture, cloud processing, and automated
analytics. This work established principles for robot-enabled continuous monitoring.
Halder et al. [54] developed a quadruped robot system for real-time remote construc-
tion progress monitoring with AR features. It streams live 360 ◦ images to operators for
AR-based as-built vs. DT comparisons. This advances near-real-time inspections, but
high-resolution data strains processing bandwidth. Building on this foundation, Halder
et al. [54] developed a robotic cyber-physical system for automated reality capture and
visualization. It integrates quadruped robots, cloud processing, and AR interfaces. Their
2024 study advances autonomous mission planning and data pipelines, enabling regular
scans with minimal human input. Challenges persist in dynamic obstacle avoidance and
coverage optimization.
Zhao and Cheah [55] created a BIM-enabled robotic system for indoor construction
progress monitoring. It uses BIM models to generate waypoint navigation paths for
repeatable, schedule-aligned scans. Integrating SLAM with BIM navigation advances prac-
tical robotic monitoring. Chen [56] examined rapid robotic scan-to-BIM workflows using
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
Buildings 2026, 16, 1448 12 of 22
LIDAR-equipped mobile platforms and AI. Robotic scanning with automated semantic
parsing reduces manual processing time by 60–70% compared to traditional terrestrial laser
scanning, though accuracy trade-offs require management.
The core advantage of robotic inspection lies in the transition from manual, discre-
tionary data collection to automated, repeatable missions. The recent literature quantifies
these benefits: quadruped robots reduce repetitive site inspection workloads by 25–90%,
depending on mission complexity and autonomy level [57].
4.1.2. Unmanned Aerial Systems (UASs) and Beyond Visual Line of Sight (BVLOS)
Unmanned Aerial Systems (UASs) have evolved from experimental platforms to in-
dispensable tools for construction site surveying, earthworks volumetrics, and structural
inspections. State-of-the-art developments have advanced beyond manual piloting to fully
autonomous “drone-in-a-box” systems and beyond visual line-of-sight operations, substan-
tially enhancing the frequency, coverage, and scalability of aerial data acquisition [58,59].
Huang and Jiang [59] reviewed UAS applications in construction management from
2016 to 2021, analyzing over 150 papers to identify trends, uses, and gaps. They found
progress monitoring and inspection to be the most advanced UAS applications, with
benefits in coverage speed, safety, and data quality.
Ibrahim et al. [60] optimized UAS reality capture plans to improve photogrammet-

accounts for coverage redundancy, viewing angles, ground sampling distance, and line-of-

3D defect localization at centimeter-level accuracy for access-free maintenance planning.
Samsami [58] systematically reviewed automated construction inspection and progress
monitoring across 200+ papers, covering capabilities, domains, and barriers. UASs perform
best on large outdoor projects but face challenges indoors due to GPS limitations and
collision risks.
UAS photogrammetry enables rapid horizontal site coverage. A single flight can map
hundreds of acres, yielding high-resolution orthomosaics and digital surface models for
comprehensive progress views. This excels for earthwork volume tracking, supplanting
slow, hazardous manual surveys. Here and in other tasks, “drone-in-a-box” systems
boost autonomy via weatherproof stations with automated battery charging/swapping.
Nevertheless, regulatory limitations may be restricted here.
Advancements like onboard Edge AI help cut cloud-upload latency for gigabyte-scale
imagery. Therefore, flight-time inference detects objects/defects, transmitting compact
insights (e.g., site logistics maps and safety alerts) instantly for real-time decisions.

4.2. Real-Time Construction Quality Reporting
Acquiring comprehensive, high-fidelity data constitutes only half the challenge in
digital construction monitoring. The second primary bottleneck entails the expeditious
processing, analysis, and interpretation of this multimodal data to yield actionable insights
that inform decision-making. Unprocessed point clouds, imagery, and sensor streams must
be converted into quantifiable progress metrics, quality assessments, geometric deviation
reports, and predictive alerts. This section reviews technologies that expedite data analysis
and afford near-real-time comprehension of on-site conditions.
4.2.1. From Photogrammetry to Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian
Splatting (3DGS)
3D reconstruction from 2D construction site imagery has relied on the SfM-MVS
pipeline, which generates sparse point clouds via feature matching followed by dense point
clouds or meshes via multi-view disparity estimation. SfM-MVS remains computationally
intensive, often requiring hours or days to process large-scale datasets. Moreover, it strug-
gles with featureless regions or reflective/transparent surfaces prevalent in construction
environments, yielding reconstruction artifacts and holes [69,70]. A significant paradigm
shift is underway with neural rendering techniques.
Neural Radiance Fields introduced a paradigm shift by representing scenes via mul-
tilayer perceptrons that parameterize a continuous 5D radiance field—spatial position
and viewing direction—rather than discrete meshes or point clouds. NeRFs excel at
synthesizing photorealistic novel views via volume rendering and capturing complex
view-dependent effects (e.g., specular reflections, transparency) that elude traditional pho-
togrammetry. However, the original NeRF was prohibitively slow to train and render.
Recent optimizations, such as Instant Neural Graphics Primitives and NeRFacto, have
reduced training times from hours to seconds or minutes, making them viable for rapid
site visualization [69–71].
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS): The most recent advancement in reality capture is
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). Unlike NeRFs, which require querying a neural network
for every pixel during rendering, 3DGS represents the scene as a collection of millions
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
Buildings 2026, 16, 1448 14 of 22
of 3D Gaussians (anisotropic ellipsoids) that carry color and opacity information and are
rasterized directly to the screen [72,73].
4.2.2. AI and Machine Learning for Construction Monitoring
AI and ML are the transformative advancements in automating the analysis of multi-
modal construction site data. Deep learning models, such as convolutional neural networks,
facilitate the automated detection, classification, and quantification of construction elements
and activities using image analysis and point cloud segmentation [74]. These approaches
process data orders of magnitude faster than manual methods while attaining equivalent
or superior accuracy, thereby enabling near-real-time progress monitoring and quality
assessment [58].
Modern systems employ state-of-the-art deep learning architectures for visual and 3D
data, including object detection networks for identifying construction elements, equipment,
and workers in images and video [ 74], semantic segmentation models for pixel-wise or
point-wise classification of images and point clouds in rapid scan-to-BIM workflows [74],
instance segmentation for delineating individual object instances to support accurate
counting and measurement [58], and transformer-based models that demonstrate improved
performance on complex scenes with occlusion and clutter, advancing beyond traditional
CNNs [58].
Key approaches include end-to-end automated pipelines that integrate data capture,
AI processing, and reporting into unified cyber-physical systems, minimizing manual
intervention. Pioneering examples include Golparvar-Fard et al.’s [60] framework, which
fuses visual/sensor data via deep learning for element detection/classification and auto-
mated schedule-aligned reporting; Pour Rahimian et al.’s [75] gamified BIM-ML system
for on-demand monitoring via imagery/point cloud analysis matched to BIM objects; and
Abreu et al.’s [74] BIM-AI Digital Twins with real-time object detection and point cloud
segmentation for continuous updates. These pipelines automatically ingest new data,
run inference, update the DT, and generate stakeholder reports, as validated across 200+
studies [58].
T ransfer learning and domain adaptation address data scarcity and poor generalization—
primary barriers beyond detection tasks [58]—by pre-training on massive general-domain
datasets, then fine-tuning with limited construction data, cutting training requirements
and elevating performance across sites.
Multimodal learning combines imagery, point clouds, BIM models, and IoT sensors
in unified deep learning models, improving robustness, contextual understanding, and
accuracy in complex environments [58,67,68]. It leverages complementary strengths, such
as visual textures from imagery and precise metrics from sensors (e.g., concrete maturity),
as in sensor fusion for cross-validated DT [67,68].
Human-in-the-loop and active learning overcome data scarcity, generalization issues,
and interpretability by engaging experts for AI output validation. Feedback improves
models, with active learning prioritizing uncertain cases [58]. For instance, Pour Rahimian
et al.’s [75] system elevated accuracy from 78% to 94% via stakeholder input, with analogous
refinements in Golparvar-Fard et al. [60] and Abreu et al. [74].
4.2.3. Edge AI and On-Device Processing
Three aspects are identified as the key ones: latency elimination and bandwidth
efficiency, on-device photogrammetry and SLAM, and hybrid edge-cloud architectures.
By executing optimized inference models (Edge AI) directly on local edge hardware,
the framework reduces latency for safety-critical tasks like worker helmet detection to
approximately 30–50 milliseconds per frame. This allows the information on the critical
https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16071448
Buildings 2026, 16, 1448 15 of 22
issue to be transferred almost instantaneously, bypassing round-trip data transmission
delays [58]. This empowers instantaneous safety interventions critical to construction
sites, such as real-time detection of worker falls, PPE non-compliance, or crane proximity
hazards using edge-enabled cameras, which can immediately activate alarms and avert
accidents [58,74]. Moreover, by preprocessing and filtering data at the source—uploading
only compressed metadata, alerts, or key events rather than full raw video feeds—edge
AI achieves dramatic bandwidth reductions (often >90%) essential for unreliable, low-
connectivity environments prevalent in remote or urban construction settings [58].
Hardware advancements in mobile SoCs and AI accelerators enable the execution
of lightweight photogrammetry and visual SLAM algorithms directly on edge devices
such as smartphones, drones, and site cameras [58]. Although on-device outputs typically
yield lower resolution and density compared to cloud-based counterparts, they deliver
critical real-time capabilities: immediate verification of scan coverage, preliminary 3D
reconstructions for basic progress monitoring, and on-site quality checks before operators
depart—averting costly revisits and enabling proactive adjustments in dynamic construc-
tion environments [58–74].
Hybrid edge-cloud architectures distribute computing tasks across hierarchical tiers—
on-device, edge servers, and cloud data centers—to optimize latency, bandwidth, pri-
vacy, and scalability [ 58]. Time-critical inferences, such as real-time safety monitoring
(e.g., worker falls, PPE non-compliance, or equipment hazards) and preliminary progress
monitoring, execute locally to enable instantaneous responses and eliminate transmis-
sion delays [58,74]. Conversely, resource-intensive tasks like detailed 3D reconstructions,
multimodal data fusion, or long-term analytics are offloaded to the cloud for superior
computational power and storage [58,67,68].
Emerging developments in hybrid frameworks, federated learning for decentralized
model training without raw data sharing, and microservices for modular, scalable de-
ployments have propelled responsive construction monitoring systems, as evidenced in
integrated pipelines fusing edge AI with cloud BIM updates across diverse sites [58,74,75].
4.2.4. BIM Integration and Digital Twins
Building Information Modeling furnishes structured semantic, geometric, and spa-
tiotemporal context—encompassing as-planned models with element hierarchies, precise
geometries, and schedule linkages—that converts heterogeneous raw construction data
streams into coherent, actionable project intelligence [ 58]. Digital Twins extend BIM by
instantiating dynamic, perpetually synchronized virtual replicas of physical construc-
tion states, assimilating multimodal real-time data from imagery, point clouds, sensors,
and IoT [ 58,74,75]. Contemporary research outlines key advancements, including au-
tomated Scan-to-BIM workflows via point cloud segmentation and procedural model-
ing [58,74,76], BIM-guided data acquisition with optimized robotic paths [58,60,74], and
extensible DT platforms enabling predictive analytics, discrepancy detection, and scenario
simulation [58,75].
A primary innovation lies in automated Scan-to-BIM workflows [74,76], which inte-
grate point cloud processing from laser scans [76], AI-based semantic segmentation [75],
and procedural geometric modeling [ 74] to generate accurate as-built BIM models [ 58].
Studies confirm these reduce manual effort by 50–70% versus traditional methods [ 58],
enabling seamless updates to the DT as in Pour Rahimian et al.’s BIM-ML system [75] and
Abreu et al.’s segmentation pipelines [74].
Building on this, BIM-guided data collection leverages BIM models to automatically
generate optimized capture missions for robots, drones, and scanners, minimizing cov-
erage gaps, redundant scans, and occlusions through path optimization and viewpoint

planning [58,60,74]. By providing semantic contexts—such as element locations, visibility
constraints, and required inspection points—BIM improves capture planning, automates
data interpretation and registration to as-planned models, and supports seamless DT

Furthermore, semantic data linking to BIM enables automated mapping of detected

5. Discussion and Challenges: AutoGreenTraC Research Framework
While the literature demonstrates that individual data acquisition technologies—
specifically UAS photogrammetry, LIDAR, and 360◦ vision—have reached technological
maturity, their isolated application fails to address the construction industry’s urgent need
for real-time optimization. The review exposed the critical gap: latency between data
acquisition and the generation of actionable insight. Bridging this gap is not merely a
technical convenience but a prerequisite for the AutoGreenTraC project goals.
The literature review reveals three fundamental bottlenecks that align directly with the
AutoGreenTraC project’s core mission of accelerating information transfer from physical
construction sites to digital environments:
• Data acquisition speed vs. accuracy trade-offs.
• Computational latency lacking near-real-time feedback.
• Integration gaps and a single source of truth deficit.
Building on the literature synthesis, a conceptual framework for AutoGreenTraC
was structured around three interconnected pillars that directly respond to identified
technological gaps:
• Autonomous data acquisition.

to ground-based approaches [ 60]. IoT sensor networks complement these by enabling

6. Conclusions
The construction sector continues to grapple with substantial cost overruns and sched-
ule slippages, primarily attributable to the delays and inefficiencies inherent in conventional
manual methods and nascent digital approaches to progress monitoring and quality assur-
ance. This study bridges this pivotal shortfall through an exhaustive review of cutting-edge
vision-based sensing modalities—namely, Light Detection and Ranging, SfM-MVS, and
panoramic (360-degree) imaging—to formulate a robust theoretical foundation for near-
real-time automated project monitoring.
The review illuminated a fundamental tension between the metrological exactitude
demanded for stringent quality reporting and the elevated data throughput essential for
real-time progress monitoring. LIDAR still holds supremacy in geometric precision, but
the overwhelming data volumes, vulnerability to occlusions, and excessively prolonged
processing times render it impractical for frequent site surveillance. On the other hand,
360-degree cameras furnish the crucial speed and cost-effectiveness essential for routine
captures, albeit sacrificing the precision demanded for high-stakes applications. SfM-
MVS delivers a compelling balance of photorealistic quality and geometric reliability yet
persists as hindered by exorbitant computational demands and persistent inaccuracies in
vertical measurements.
