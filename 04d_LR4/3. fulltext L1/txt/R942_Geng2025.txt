Integrating multi-source data for life-cycle 
risk assessment of bridge networks: a system 
digital twin framework
Ziheng Geng1, Chao Zhang2, Yishuo Jiang3, Dora Pugliese1 and Minghui Cheng1,4* 
Abstract 
Bridges are critical infrastructure assets that face a variety of stressors throughout their service life, requiring a life-
cycle approach to assess their risk profile. Recent advancements in sensing and monitoring technologies provide 
a powerful data foundation to improve the accuracy of life-cycle risk assessment (LCRA). However, existing works 
that incorporate data for probabilistic risk assessment typically focus on individual bridges and rely on single-source 
data, limiting their scope and applicability. To this end, a system digital twin (SDT) framework based on Bayesian 
network (BN) is proposed to integrate multi-source data for LCRA of bridge networks. Specifically, the SDT can capture 
correlations and interdependencies across various scales, including within individual components (e.g., multiple 
failure modes), between components within a system (e.g., bridges along a route), and across interconnected systems 
(e.g., bridge and hydraulic systems). It integrates data from various sources including bridge inspections, traffic moni-
toring facilities, and water watch stations. A coastal bridge network in Miami-Dade County, FL, is used as an illustrative 
example to demonstrate how the SDT integrates multi-source data for risk assessment. Additionally, several future 
scenarios are hypothesized to showcase the applicability and flexibility of the proposed framework in supporting risk 
management for infrastructure systems.
Keywords System digital twin, Life-cycle risk assessment, Infrastructure management, System reliability, Bayesian 
network
Introduction
Bridges are critical assets within infrastructure systems, 
playing a pivotal role in ensuring the functionality and 
connectivity of modern communities and cities. How -
ever, these assets are vulnerable, facing a variety of stress-
ors throughout their service life. Factors such as natural 
hazards, aging materials, and excessive loading can accel-
erate the deterioration of bridges and undermine their 
reliability under both normal and extreme condi -
tions [1, 2]. This underscores the necessity for a life-
cycle approach to risk assessment in bridge networks. 
Life-cycle risk assessment (LCRA) provides a holistic 
approach for assessing the evolving risks associated with 
a structure/system over its lifespan [3, 4]. This approach 
involves identifying potential hazards, estimating their 
probability of occurrence, and assessing their potential 
consequences. With the risk profile, LCRA facilitates 
informed decision-making in planning inspection, main -
tenance, repair actions to ensure long-term sustainabil -
ity and resilience [5, 6]. In light of increasing impacts of 
climate change, adopting such a holistic approach is also 
essential to enhance the resilience of bridge networks, 
enabling them to withstand and recover from disruptions 
while maintaining their functionality [7].

Recent breakthroughs in sensing and monitoring 
technologies have significantly improved the acquisi -
tion of multi-source data reflecting bridge conditions, 
thereby opening new avenues for enhancing LCRA of 
bridge networks. For example, non-destructive testing 
(NDE) techniques have been powerful tools for detect -
ing material-level flaws in bridges. Common NDE meth -
ods, such as ground penetrating radar [8, 9], close-range 
photogrammetry [10], infrared thermography [8, 11], 
and terrestrial laser scanning [12] have demonstrated 
efficacy in detecting defects, rebar corrosion, and crack -
ing. Additionally, structural health monitoring (SHM) 
has emerged as an advanced approach for comprehensive 
assessment of structural condition. SHM systems utilize 
diverse sensors to capture physical measurements asso -
ciated with loads (e.g., bridge weigh-in-motion for traffic 
loads [13]), environmental stressors (e.g., acoustic sensor 
for scour detection [14]), and bridge responses (e.g., opti-
cal-fiber sensors for strain [15, 16] and vibration moni -
toring [17]). Collectively, these innovations produce an 
extensive and comprehensive dataset that is expected to 
be used by LCRA models to improve prediction accuracy 
and reduce model uncertainty.
With the ability to leverage diverse data sources in 
the real world, digital twin (DT) technology is increas -
ingly recognized as a transformative tool for life-cycle 
management of infrastructure systems. Specifically, DTs 
are virtual representations of physical assets or systems, 
continuously synchronized with their physical counter -
parts through bidirectional information exchange [18]. 
The distinguishing features of DTs lie in their ability to 
(a) integrate observations from physical asset to dynami -
cally update the state of digital asset and (b) leverage the 
updated digital state to inform decision-making for the 
operation, maintenance, and management of physical 
assets. However, the development of DTs for LCRA of 
bridge networks faces two critical challenges. First, bridge 
networks are complex systems comprising multiple inter-
connected sub-systems, such as bridges, transportation 
networks, and rivers. These sub-systems exhibit correla -
tions and interdependencies across various scales, both 
within individual sub-systems (e.g., multiple structural 
components of a single bridge and multiple bridges along 
a route) and across different sub-systems (e.g., bridges 
subject to river scour). Therefore, a systems approach is 
required to effectively capture these interrelationships. 
Second, risk assessment of bridge networks requires the 
integration of heterogeneous data sources. For example, 
bridge inspections and river measurements inform the 
failure probability of bridges, while traffic volume data 
aids in assessing the consequences of bridge failures. 
These datasets typically differ in format and update fre -
quency, underscoring the need for a data assimilation 
approach to seamlessly integrate multi-source data into 
the DT.
To address these research challenges, this paper devel -
ops a scalable computational framework for LCRA of 
bridge networks. The framework enables the develop -
ment of a system digital twin (SDT) at a large scale (e.g., 
city- and county-scales), the integration of data from 
diverse sources and different systems, and the considera -
tion of complex statistical correlations among the ran -
dom variables and data sources within the same system 
and across different systems. A case study in Miami-Dade 
County, FL, is conducted to demonstrate the proposed 
framework. Specifically, data from diverse sources 
including bridge inspections, traffic monitoring facilities, 
and water watch stations are utilized to update the risk 
profile of the bridge network. To the best knowledge of 
the authors, this case study is the first DT that reaches 
the county scale, interlinks three systems for probabil -
istic modeling and risk updating, and incorporates the 
statistical correlations. Additionally, three scenarios 
are presented to illustrate the flexibility of the proposed 
framework, showcasing the capacity of SDT to adapt to 
real-world changes.
Literature review
Life‑cycle risk assessment of bridge networks
Extensive efforts have been dedicated to applying LCRA 
to spatially distributed bridge networks within trans -
portation systems. Risk analysis typically involves two 
components: (a) evaluating the failure probability of indi-
vidual bridges over time, and (b) estimating the direct 
and indirect consequences of bridge failures. For failure 
probability analysis, previous studies have developed 
time-variant reliability analysis frameworks for various 
failure-inducing factors, including floods [19, 20], colli -
sions [21, 22], traffic loads [23, 24], material deterioration 
[25, 26], earthquakes [27, 28], tsunamis [1, 29], storm 
surges [30, 31], and climate change [7, 32]. Recognizing 
that adjacent bridges are exposed to similar environ -
mental stressors and traffic patterns, event-based simu -
lations [33, 34] and random field modeling [35, 36] have 
been employed to model the spatial correlation of bridge 
failures. For failure consequence assessment, estimat -
ing indirect consequences, i.e., the cost associated with 
reduced functionality across the bridge network, remains 
a challenging task due to the need to simulate the varia -
tion in traffic flow resulting from bridge failures. Exist -
ing studies have advanced traffic assignment analyses 
by incorporating user decision-making behaviors [6], 
emphasizing extreme risk scenarios [4], and enhancing 
computational efficiency [37, 38].
Additionally, with the rapid advancement of machine 
learning, surrogate modeling techniques such as

However, a critical limitation of the abovementioned

discrepancies between model predictions and real-

Digital twin for bridge asset management
Recent studies have explored the implementation of 
DTs in bridges asset management. For example, DTs 
have been developed using building information mod -
eling (BIM) and augmented with inspection data such 
as 3D scanning [ 44], digital image correlation (DIC) 
[45], and weigh-in-motion (WIM) [ 46]. Extending 
these advancements, modeling enabling technologies, 
including geometry, measurements, simulations, and 
other data-driven techniques, have been integrated 
to develop cyber-physics DTs [ 47– 49]. Additionally, 
several studies have focused on the dynamic decision-
making feedback loops within DTs, employing proba -
bilistic graphic models as the computational cores and 
utilizing Bayesian inference to update the digital state 
and inform predictive maintenance decision-making 
[50– 52].
Despite significant advancements, existing DTs for 
bridge asset management suffer from two limitations. 
First, existing works predominately focus on developing 
DTs for individual bridges. However, bridges are inte -
gral components of spatially distributed transportation 
networks, where the failure of a single bridge can trig -
ger cascading effects, such as traffic detours and delays. 
These indirect impacts frequently surpass direct con -
sequences by several orders of magnitude [ 53], under -
scoring the necessity for modeling bridge networks as 
complex, interdependent systems. Second, current DTs 
with probabilistic modeling capabilities primarily rely 
on single-source data for risk updating. This reliance 
on siloed data source constrains the capacity of DTs 
to capture the dynamic evolution of real-world condi -
tions, thereby undermining their predictive accuracy. 
Given the diversity and heterogeneity of real-world 
data, a comprehensive approach is required to syner -
gistically integrate data from multiple sources for risk 
updating.

System digital twin framework for bridge network 
risk assessment
Herein, a SDT framework based on BN is developed to 
integrate data from multiple sources for bridge network 
risk assessment. BNs have emerged as a pivotal enabler 
for implementing DT due to their capabilities in: (a) mod-
eling complex correlations and interdependencies within 
infrastructure systems, (b) integrating diverse real-world 
data sources through Bayesian updating, and (c) perform-
ing probabilistic inference and prediction on variables of 
interest. The development of a BN-based SDT involves 
several critical steps: (a) defining the graph structure, i.e., 
BN structure, to capture the complex correlations within 
the system, (b) eliciting conditional probability tables 
(CPTs) to quantify dependencies between variables, and 
(c) specifying data nodes and performance nodes to ena -
ble automated Bayesian inference, as shown in Fig. 2. This 
section begins with an introduction of the concepts of 
BN, followed by a detailed explanation of how the graph 
structure is established for both bridge-level and net -
work-level risk assessment. Lastly, it elaborates the clas -
sification of nodes and provides a streamlined approach 
for implementing probabilistic inference.
Concepts of Bayesian network
BN is a probabilistic graphic model that encodes the 
dependencies and causal relationships among variables 
[61]. It is structured as a directed acyclic graph (DAG), 
where each node denotes a random variable and each 
directed edge signifies a statistical dependency between 
two variables. A BN can be formulated as a joint prob -
ability distribution multiplied by the marginal probability 
distributions and conditional probability distributions. 
Taking an example of the BN with four nodes in Fig.  3, 
nodes A and B are root nodes, described by marginal 
probability distributions P(A) and P(B), respectively. 
Node C, as a child of nodes A and B, is described by a 
conditional probability distribution P(C | A, B) . Similarly, 
node D, as a child of node B, is described by a conditional 
probability distribution P(D | B) . Using these marginal 
and conditional probability distributions, the joint prob -
ability distribution of the BN can be described by:
This joint probability distribution underpins the 
BN, supporting probabilistic inference across complex 
dependencies within the model.

structure, which specifies the dependencies or causal

Graph structure of system digital twins for bridge‑level risk 
assessment
The graph structure of SDTs for individual bridge risk 
assessment is illustrated in Fig.  4. As outlined in Sect. 3, 
the failure of bridges includes two modes: superstructure 
failure and scour failure. The probability of superstruc -
ture failure is assessed using superstructure condition 
rating, thus an edge is added from the superstructure 
condition rating node to the failure probability node. In 
case of scour failure, the failure probability depends on 
both the substructure condition and the hydraulic fac -
tors, thus three edges are added from the substructure 
condition rating node, river flow node, and river stage 
node to the failure probability node. One significant 
consequence of bridge failure is vehicle detour, thus an 
edge is added from the AADT node to the failure conse -
quence node. The risk of bridge failure can be obtained 
by multiplying the failure probability with its associated 
consequences, thus two edges are added from the failure 
probability node and the failure consequence node to the 
failure risk node.
Additionally, a clustered graphical structure is uti -
lized to capture statistical correlations among variables. 
Typically, such correlations are represented by drawing 
vectors from a Gaussian random field. However, exact 
modeling of random fields requires edge connections 
among each pair of correlation variables, resulting in a 
densely connected graph structure. This intensive con -
nectivity significantly escalates computational burden, as 
the memory usage of BN increases exponentially with the 
number of edges [67]. Herein, an approximation method 
is employed to enable computationally tractable mod -
eling of statistically correlated random variables [68]. 
Specifically, correlated variables are linked to a common 
source node, symbolizing their mutual origin in a shared 
random field. This configuration is exemplified in Fig.  4, 
where river flow node and river stage node are connected 
to the common source node. To further improve the 
modeling accuracy, error nodes are connected to each 
random variable, as illustrated by the flow error node and 
stage error node in Fig.  4. More details about the math -
ematical derivation and validation of modeling correlated 
random variables can be found in Bensi et al. [68].
Graph structure of system digital twins for network‑level 
risk assessment
The construction of graph structure of SDTs for network-
level risk assessment necessitates the incorporation of 
spatial geometry of various components across the net -
work. This implies that whether the river flow, river stage, 
and AADT nodes can be linked to bridge failure prob -
ability and failure consequence nodes is dictated by their 
spatial relationship. Specifically, the linkage of river flow 
and stage nodes to bridge failure probability node needs 
to satisfy: (a) the river monitored by the water watch 
station flows through the bridge, and (b) the distance 
between the station and the bridge is less than a prede -
fined threshold. Similarly, the linkage of AADT nodes 
to bridge failure consequence node requires that: (a) the 
bridge lies along the route monitored by the STCS, and 
(b) the distance between the STCS and the bridge falls 
below a given threshold. Additionally, beyond the statisti-
cal correlation between river flow and stage for individual 
river gages, the graph structure also captures the interde -
pendencies of flow and stage among adjacent river gages. 
When these river gages measure the same river and their 
distance is below a certain threshold, the flow and stage 
nodes associated with these gages are modeled using 
a clustered graphical structure, i.e., sharing a common 
source node.

Probabilistic inference usingof system digital twins
With the constructed graph structure, a dataset needs to 
be created to fit the marginal or CPTs for each node of 
the BN. This dataset can originate from field measure -
ments or simulations. Before being used to fit the CPTs, 
the dataset should be discretized, transforming continu -
ous data into discrete states. Various discretization strat -
egies can be applied depending on the characteristics 
of the dataset, for example, equal-width and equal-fre -
quency binning allow for uniform segmentation of data, 
while quantile-based discretization is typically used to 
emphasize specific data ranges [70]. Herein, Monte Carlo 
(MC) simulation is utilized to generate the dataset. For 
discretization, equal-width and quantile-based strategies 
are applied selectively to handle various variables within 
the dataset. More details about the MC simulation and 
the discretization processes will be elaborated in the sec -
tion of case study.
An important property of BNs is their ability to per -
form inference on variables of interest when data are 
fully or partially available. To facilitate automated infer -
ence over time, BN nodes are classified into data nodes, 
analysis nodes, and performance nodes based on their 
functions, as shown in Fig.  6. Data nodes include vari -
ables such as superstructure and substructure condition 
ratings, river flow, river stage, and AADT. These variables 
can be acquired at different frequencies from diverse 
monitoring devices in real-world conditions. Analysis 
nodes include common source, flow error, stage error, 
failure probability, and failure consequence, which are 
intermediate variables involved in the Bayesian inference 
process. Performance node refers to the failure risk at the 
individual bridge level and network risk across the bridge 
network, serving as key indicators of the component/
network performance. At each inference, data nodes are 
updated based on the available data sources and Bayesian 
inference is performed to update the probability distribu-
tion of performance nodes. Please note that an illustra -
tive example of a BN for monitoring the life-cycle risk of 
a bridge network is available in a previous study by the

Case study: A bridge network in Miami‑Dade 
County
Graph structure of system digital twins
The bridge network in Miami-Dade County, FL, is used 
as a case study to illustrate the practical application of 
the proposed SDT framework. The graph structure is 
constructed using three databases: the bridge database, 
traffic database, and river database. The bridge database 
is derived from the NBI database [72]. The features uti -
lized to construct graph structure include the geographic 
coordinates of each bridge, the intersecting rivers, and 
the routes on which the bridges are located. Figure  7 
shows a plan view of bridge locations within Miami-Dade 
County. The traffic database is provided by the Florida 
Department of Transportation (FDOT) [73], containing 
details on geographic coordinates of each portable traf -
fic monitoring site and associated roadway numbers. The 
river database is acquired from the U.S. Geological Sur -
vey (USGS) National Water Resources Dashboard [74], 
providing the geographic coordinates of each river gage 
and monitored stream names. Note that a preprocessing

Then, the AADT nodes and river nodes are evaluated 
to determine whether they satisfy the requirements 
for linkage to the bridge nodes. Specifically, the river 
flow and stage nodes can be linked to the bridge fail -
ure probability node if the river intersects the bridge 
and the distance between the river gage and the bridge 
is less than 5 km. For the AADT nodes, the 5 nearest 
STCSs monitoring the roadway where the bridge is 
located are selected to connect to the bridge failure 
consequence node. Additionally, data from two river 
gages are considered statistically correlated if they 
measure the same river and the distance between them 
is less than 2 km. These river flow and stage nodes are 
modeled using a shared common source node. Follow -
ing the above steps, the graph structure for monitoring 
the life-cycle risk of the bridge network in Miami-
Dade County is constructed, as shown in Fig.  8. The 
graph integrates 1,034 bridges, 1,261 STCSs, and 10 
river gages within Miami-Dade County, containing a 
total of 6,478 nodes and 10,584 edges.
Conditional probability tables fitting
MC simulation is conducted to generate a dataset to fit 
the CPTs of the BN. The procedure begins with deter -
mining data ranges for certain random variables. Spe -
cifically, the superstructure and substructure condition

ratings are converted to MSs ranging from 1 to 7, assum -
ing a uniform distribution. For river flow and stage, data 
ranges are determined according to historical river gage 
measurements obtained from the USGS website [74]. 
Figure  9 shows data from three representative river 
gages in Miami-Dade County. It shows that despite the 
presence of outliers, river flow and stage typically fluctu -
ate within ± 30% of their mean values and are normally 
distributed. These findings are used to define random 
variables of river flow and stage in the MC simulation. 
Similarly, data ranges of AADT are determined using his-
torical records from the FDOT open data hub [73]. The 
analysis of six representative STCSs indicates that AADT 
varies within a range of 0.8 to 1.8 times the initial values, 
as shown in Fig.  10. This variability is incorporated into 
the definition of random variables for AADT in the MC 
simulation.
To calculate the failure probability of bridges, sam -
ples are uniformly drawn from the superstructure and 
substructure condition ratings, as well as river flow and 
stage. In parallel, AADT samples are uniformly drawn 
from 5 nearby STCSs to calculate the failure consequence 
of bridges. Herein, the uncertainty factor associated with 
the spatial interpolation of AADT is assumed to follow a 
triangular distribution with a mode of 1.0, a lower bound 
of 0.6, and an upper bound of 1.4. Other parameters rel -
evant to the calculation of failure consequence are taken 
from Cheng and Frangopol [75], summarized in Table  1. 
The failure risk is calculated as the product of failure 
probability and failure consequence. Please note that the 
data for common source node, flow error node, and stage 
error node are derived using Cholesky factorization of 
river flow and stage data [68].
The dataset must be discretized before being utilized to 
learn the CPTs of the BN. Specifically, the superstructure 
and substructure conditions of bridges are represented by 
MSs, which are inherently discrete variables. For contin -
uous variables such as common source, river flow, river 
stage, flow error, stage error, and AADT, an equal-width 
discretization is employed, dividing the range of each 
variable into 6 uniform intervals. In contrast, variables 
relevant to the calculation of risk, including bridge fail -
ure probability, bridge failure consequence, bridge failure 
risk, and network risk, are discretized using a quantile-
based strategy. This strategy leverages 25th, 50th, 75th, 
85th, and 95th percentiles to capture critical variation in 
data distribution and enable a more granular representa -
tion of high-risk scenarios. With the discretized dataset, 
parameter learning is conducted to fit the CPTs of the 
BN.

Flexibility of the proposed system digital twin 
framework
One of the strengths of the proposed SDT framework lies 
in its flexibility in accommodating the dynamic evolution 
of urban environments through the graphical structure of 
BN. As urban areas expand and transform, infrastructure 
systems undergo significant changes, including the con -
struction of new bridges, the closure of aging structures, 
and heightened exposure to climate-related challenges, 
such as the increasing frequency and intensity of extreme 
weather events. These evolving conditions require the 
risk assessment framework to be flexible to adapt to 
such changes. The proposed framework addresses this 
need through its modular structure, where the CPTs 
are organized in an isolated and localized manner. This 
structure allows specific components within the BN to 
be independently modified or updated without recon -
figuring the entire network. Consequently, the proposed 
framework has the potential to rapidly adapt to the com -
plex changes in the real world, thus enhancing its utility 
for decision-makers. Herein, three scenarios are selected 
to demonstrate the flexibility of the proposed framework, 
as illustrated in Fig. 13.

Limitations and future work
Although the proposed framework demonstrates fea -
sibility and applicability in assessing life-cycle risk for 
bridge networks, it suffers from certain limitations. First, 
a significant challenge is the storage and computational 
efficiency associated with large-scale BNs. For example, 
the BN in this study comprises 6,478 nodes and 10,584 
edges, occupying 97,484.8 MB memory to store its CPTs. 
This presents a substantial computational burden. As 
the size and complexity of the network grow, the expo -
nential increase in memory requirements can severely 
hinder the practical implementations of the proposed 
framework. (Cheng M, Geng Z, Gao HO (2024) Perfor -
mance-oriented system digital twin: A Bayesian net -
work-based  framework to customize sub-digital twins 
for faster Bayesian inference. Struct Infrastruct E (Under 
review)) proposed a custom BN approach to address this 
challenge. It can extract a trunk network from the origi -
nal large-scale BN based on available data sources and 
given performance measures. By reducing the size of the 
network, it can significantly lower the computational 
overhead, facilitating efficient Bayesian inference. Build -
ing on these advancements, future work should focus 
on seamlessly integrating custom BN with the proposed 
framework to develop scalable solutions that can han -
dle the computational demands of increasingly large and 
complex networks.
Second, due to the limitations in available resources, 
the current framework has not yet been validated. Future 
work should focus on assessing the performance and 
quality of the SDT to ensure its applicability in practical 
engineering scenarios. Specifically, the validation can be 
implemented with the aid of field and laboratory testing. 
The proposed framework can identify a list of vulner -
able bridges within the bridge network. For these iden -
tified bridges, field coring can be conducted to extract 
core samples from their structural components. These 
samples can then be tested in the laboratory to analyze 
their properties such as microstructural characteris -
tics, apparent electrical resistivity, compressive strength, 
chloride ingress, and carbonation depths. These labora -
tory analyses provide quantitative metrics that reflect the 
physical condition of bridges. To validate the framework, 
the same procedures should be applied to bridges classi -
fied as low risk by the SDT. By comparing the laboratory 
results from vulnerable and low-risk bridges, valuable 
insights into the predictive accuracy and robustness of 
the framework can be derived.
Third, the proposed framework primarily focuses on 
risk assessment of bridge networks. It integrates the 
probability of bridge failure with the associated failure 
consequences to identify the risk within the bridge net -
work. However, it does not include the system’s response 
to address these threats. Future work should extend the 
framework to incorporate restoration and resilience. 
Resilience is a performance indicator that evaluates the 
system’s capacity to withstand, adapt to, and recover 
from disruptions [54, 78, 79]. It highlights the ability of 
a system to recover to its previous functionality after a 
disruption. Restoration models, which define the recov -
ery pattern of the system, are essential tools for quanti -
fying resilience [80]. Incorporating these elements will 
enhance the utility of the framework in infrastructure 
management, better assisting in decision-making regard -
ing inspections and maintenance of infrastructure.
Summary and conclusions
This paper develops a system digital twin (SDT) frame -
work based on Bayesian network (BN) to monitor the 
life-cycle risk of bridge networks. A prototype graph 
structure of SDTs is first developed at the bridge level 
to capture the correlations within individual compo -
nents (e.g., multiple failure modes of bridges) and to 
integrate data from various sources. The prototype is 
then extended to the network level by embedding spatial 
geometry of cities or counties. The resulting network-
level graph structure can capture interdependencies 
both within a single system (e, g., bridges along a route 
and river gages monitoring the same river) and across 
interconnected systems (e.g., bridge, transportation, and 
hydraulic systems). With the graph structure, Monte 
Carlo simulation is conducted to generate a dataset to 
fit the conditional probability tables (CPTs) of the BN. 
To streamline the Bayesian inference over time, the BN 
nodes are classified into three types: data nodes, analysis 
nodes, and performance nodes. This classification facili -
tates automated Bayesian inference using multi-source 
data with different update frequencies. A real-world 
bridge network in Miami-Dade County, FL, is used as a 
case study to illustrate the feasibility and applicability of 
the proposed framework for life-cycle risk assessment. 
The following conclusions are drawn:
The graph structure is constructed using three 
databases: the bridge database, traffic database, and 
river database. By embedding the spatial geometry 
of the county, a graph structure with 6,478 nodes 
and 10,584 edges is created, integrating 1,034 
Page 18 of 20Geng et al. J Infrastruct Preserv Resil (2025) 6:9 
bridges, 1,261 STCSs, and 10 river gages within 
Miami-Dade County. This demonstrates the scal -
ability of the proposed SDT framework.
The graph structure utilizes a clustered graphi -
cal structure to capture the statistically correlated 
variables, including river flow and stage data from 
both individual and adjacent river gages. These cor -
related river flow and stage nodes are connected to 
a common source node, demonstrating the capacity 
of the SDT framework to model the complex cor -
relations at the county scale.
A future scenario is hypothesized to showcase how 
the SDT integrates diverse data sources for risk 
inference. The bridge conditions are assumed to 
deteriorate over time, whereas traffic volumes and 
river stage/flow are assumed to increase gradually. 
The results reveal a general upward trend in net -
work risk, punctuated by localized fluctuations. 
This demonstrates the capability of the proposed 
framework in updating the risk of bridge network 
using available multiple-source data.
Three representative scenarios are selected: the 
construction of new bridges, the closure of aging 
bridges, and data out of range. The proposed frame -
work effectively addresses these changes by modify -
ing localized CPTs without reconfiguring the entire 
network. The updated BN retains its integrity and 
functionality for risk assessment across all scenar -
ios. The results show the flexibility of the proposed 
framework in accommodating the dynamic evolu -
tion of urban bridge networks.
