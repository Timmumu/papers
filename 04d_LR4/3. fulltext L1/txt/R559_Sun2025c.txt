Abstract: The rapid expansion of civil infrastructure in China underscores the critical
need for advanced solutions to ensure the structural health of aging bridges. This study
introduces a novel data and knowledge-driven digital twin modeling (DK-DTM) frame-
work designed to enhance the safe and efficient operation and maintenance (O&M) of
bridges. Such a system should be capable of (1) monitoring structural dynamics in real time,
(2) capturing spatiotemporal details and changes (e.g., defects and deformations), (3) analyz-
ing structure deterioration patterns, (4) predicting structure failure risks, and (5) generating
optimal maintenance and repair actions for ensuring structural safety. Previous studies
have developed advanced sensing techniques and robust artificial intelligence algorithms
for capturing and analyzing bridge health conditions. However, most existing techniques
and algorithms heavily rely on high-quality data, which are difficult to obtain during
bridge O&M. This raises the critical question of how to incorporate expert knowledge
together with data-driven tools to establish a trustworthy DT for bridge O&M. This study
presents the DK-DTM framework, which uniquely integrates multi-source data collection,
spatiotemporal modeling, and expert knowledge reasoning. By combining these compo-
nents, the framework supports smart structural health assessments of bridges, enabling
comprehensive monitoring, prediction, and decision-making for efficient maintenance. The
spatial and temporal models provide real-time data, while the expert knowledge model
functions as an automated evaluation tool for structural health assessment. The results
demonstrate that the proposed DK-DTM framework significantly enhances the accuracy
and efficiency of O&M processes for aging bridges, addressing key gaps in existing digital
twin methodologies.
Keywords: digital twin model; structural health assessment; expert knowledge
1. Introduction
Since the 1970s, the number of bridges in China has expanded rapidly. Accord-
ing to the 2023 Statistical Bulletin of Transportation Industry Development, there were
1,079,300 highway bridges nationwide by the end of 2023. The rapid increase in the number
of bridges, combined with higher vehicle density and load demands, has created significant
challenges in ensuring their safe and efficient operation and maintenance (O&M) [1]. Aging
bridges face structural issues such as cracking, water seepage, and damage to expansion
joints, requiring advanced solutions for long-term monitoring and maintenance. In the
United States alone, approximately USD 20.5 billion is needed annually to address the

escalating deterioration of bridges [2]. Therefore, a smart bridge management system is
necessary for ensuring safe and efficient O&M of aging bridges.
Traditional visual inspections of bridges are labor-intensive and rely heavily on sub-
jective judgments, often leading to inconsistent and error-prone results. This highlights
the need for automated solutions that can improve both the accuracy and the efficiency of
bridge health monitoring. Recent developments in computer vision techniques and image
processing algorithms show potential in bridge inspection and structural health monitoring
(SHM) of bridges [3]. Despite their widespread applications, computer vision techniques
and image processing algorithms still face challenges, including noise interference, limited
sensitivity, the complexity of setting appropriate alarm thresholds, and sensor reliability.
Especially when dealing with complex bridge structures, these factors may result in missed
detections, false positives, or system malfunctions, thereby affecting the overall effective-
ness and reliability of the bridge health monitoring system [ 4]. It is therefore necessary
to establish a smart O&M system that can monitor structural dynamics and capture spa-
tiotemporal details for analyzing structure deterioration patterns and assessing structure
failure risks.
A trustworthy BDT enables seamless integration between physical and virtual envi-
ronments by providing an accurate digital representation of physical structures, utilizing
real-time data, and facilitating closed-loop interactions between the virtual and physical
bridges [5]. The integration of virtual and physical systems overcomes limitations in time,
space, cost, and safety, enabling comprehensive real-time assessments and predictive main-
tenance for bridge structures. Therefore, DT technology can be applied in the field of bridge
SHM, significantly improving its operational efficiency. In fact, DT technology has been
extensively applied in various fields, including manufacturing, aerospace, transportation,
healthcare, and energy. DT technology has gained substantial attention and recognition
from academic institutions, government agencies, and industry experts both domestically
and internationally [6].
The current digital twin modeling (DTM) is typically constructed using collected data
to construct simulation models. However, relying solely on data-driven DT models makes
it difficult to comprehensively monitor and provide early warnings for the health status
of complex bridge structures. This study proposed a DK-DTM framework for support-
ing smart structural health assessments of bridges. The proposed framework contains
(1) data collection and processing of bridge health condition data from multiple sources and
(2) spatiotemporal modeling of BDTs. The proposed DK-DTM framework uniquely in-
tegrates spatiotemporal modeling with expert knowledge reasoning to provide dynamic
early warnings for potential structural issues. This comprehensive approach ensures ac-
curate monitoring, predictive analysis, and enhanced decision-making for bridge O&M.
As a result, the proposed framework significantly enhances the efficiency and safety of
bridge O&M.
The organization of the paper is as follows. Section 2 provides a systematic review for
synthesizing previous studies on SHM and DT and identifying knowledge gaps. Section 3

image data, 3D point cloud data, sensor data, and expert knowledge. Section 5 presents the
multi-source heterogeneous data fusion model, which integrates spatiotemporal and expert
knowledge models to enable a comprehensive assessment of structural health. Section 6
provides a discussion of the proposed model’s strengths and limitations compared with
traditional approaches. Finally, Section 7 concludes the paper by summarizing the main
contributions and proposing directions for future research.

2. Literature Review
2.1. Structural Health Monitoring
Over the past few decades, driven by advancements in sensing technology, data
processing, and artificial intelligence (AI), bridge structural health monitoring (SHM)
technology has experienced significant advancements. In the past, SHM mainly relied
on manual inspections and non-destructive testing (NDT) techniques, such as acoustic
emission, X-ray diffraction, and interferometry [7]. However, these methods have certain
limitations, particularly in addressing complex bridge structures, as well as in real-time
monitoring and accuracy [8].
Recent developments have underscored the potential of big data and AI for transform-
ing SHM, particularly in terms of interpreting vast amounts of data to facilitate effective
condition assessment [9]. The advent of digital technologies has facilitated a transition
to more sophisticated, automated SHM systems. Long-span bridges worldwide, such as
the Tsing Ma Bridge in Hong Kong and the Akashi Kaikyo Bridge in Japan, have been
equipped with long-term monitoring systems, enabling continuous data collection and
damage detection [10]. These systems utilize networks of sensors, including strain gauges,
fiber optics, and wireless sensors, to monitor key parameters such as vibration, strain,
and temperature [11]. The integration of robotics, including ground, wall-climbing, and
flying robots, has further enhanced these monitoring capabilities by enabling access to
hard-to-reach areas and increasing the efficiency of data collection [ 12]. The Internet of
Things (IoT) has also contributed to the transition from traditional wire-based systems
to wireless, real-time SHM, enhancing data accessibility and system efficiency [ 13]. For
instance, Serlenga et al. (2021) demonstrated the importance of integrating diverse sensing
techniques, such as seismic and electromagnetic methods, to monitor the Gravina Bridge in
Matera, Italy [14]. Their study showcased how multi-source data fusion enables a holistic
analysis of structural behavior, improving the reliability of SHM systems while addressing
challenges associated with merging heterogeneous data types. While significant progress
has been made, several challenges persist in SHM, particularly regarding data interpreta-
tion, model accuracy, and system reliability. Traditional data-driven methods, although
widely used, often struggle to accurately capture the complexity of bridge behavior under
varying environmental and operational conditions [15]. Heterogeneous data from diverse
sensor types further complicate SHM processes, requiring advanced data fusion techniques
to improve model accuracy and system reliability [ 9]. In this context, the evaluation of
safety formats for non-linear finite element analysis offers valuable insights. For instance,
Tur, Viktar V . et al. (2020) critically analyzed widely used methods such as the partial factor
method, global resistance methods, and probabilistic approaches, highlighting significant
uncertainties and statistically vague formulations in current structural codes [16]. These
findings emphasize the need for advanced methodologies to address variability in struc-
tural responses and improve the reliability of safety evaluations, particularly for aging
bridge infrastructure. Numerical modeling approaches, including non-linear analysis meth-
ods, further contribute to the field by addressing uncertainties in structural performance
assessment. As noted by De Matteis et al. (2023), non-linear analysis frameworks enable
accurate simulations of bridge behavior, providing robust methodologies for assessing
safety under complex conditions [17].
The integration of big data and AI technologies into SHM offers significant potential
to address these gaps. Machine learning algorithms, particularly those used for vibration-
and image-based SHM, have exhibited considerable potential in damage detection, pattern
recognition, and predictive analysis [18]. However, many of these systems are still in the
developmental phase, and challenges remain, such as managing heterogeneous data from
different sensor types and improving the reliability of real-time predictions [19].

In light of these challenges, the DK-DTM presents a viable solution. By incorporat-
ing expert knowledge into the rule engines and data interaction mechanisms, this model

of real-time data with expert knowledge in DT technology enables a more holistic mon-
itoring framework, significantly improving the efficiency and safety of bridge operation
and maintenance.
2.2. Digital Twin T echnique
The concept of the DT, first proposed by Michael Grieves, was initially applied in
product lifecycle management (PLM) [20]. NASA pioneered the application of DT tech-
nology in the aerospace field in 2010. As technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT),
artificial intelligence (AI), and big data advance, DT technology has gradually expanded
into more industries [21].
DT technology has become widespread across sectors such as manufacturing, health-
care, aerospace, and construction infrastructure [22]. In manufacturing, the adoption of DTs
has been key in facilitating cyber-physical integration, optimizing production efficiency,
and enabling predictive maintenance through real-time data analysis [23]. Leveraging data-
driven approaches, DTs have transformed smart manufacturing, allowing for enhanced
decision-making and streamlined operations across the product lifecycle [24]. In healthcare,
it supports personalized treatment and public safety management [20]. In construction and
infrastructure, especially in bridge SHM, DT technology facilitates real-time bridge moni-
toring and predictive maintenance [25]. Digital twins rely on technologies such as IoT, big
data analytics, cloud computing, and AI [26]. Modern DTM not only encompasses physical
and virtual entities but also integrates data, services, and networks, enhancing interaction
between the physical and virtual [27]. Furthermore, machine learning algorithms and big
data analytics optimize system performance and predict future trends and failures [ 28].
DTM is used throughout the infrastructure’s lifecycle, offering a dynamic and holistic
view of its performance and condition. Studies such as that by Liu et al. (2020) demon-
strate the potential of DT-based SHM systems for large infrastructure, highlighting the
importance of real-time data integration in bridge management [29]. In bridge monitoring,
technologies like machine vision, edge computing, and big data fusion enhance real-time
monitoring and data processing efficiency for bridges. Machine vision and multi-source
data fusion technologies enable effective monitoring of bridge traffic loads and structural
health, facilitating timely detection of potential issues and preventive maintenance [30]. It
enables multi-dimensional monitoring of bridge structures, allowing timely, data-driven
maintenance decisions [31].
Despite significant advances in many fields, DT technology still faces numerous
challenges in practical applications. Constructing and maintaining DTM requires extensive
data support, with high costs for data collection, processing, and storage, particularly in the
monitoring of large infrastructure such as bridges [28]. Moreover, as scenarios grow more
complex, creating a multi-dimensional, multi-scenario DTM presents substantial technical
and resource challenges [27]. For infrastructure such as bridges, achieving multi-source
data fusion and processing, along with building a high-precision, automated DTM, remains
a central focus of current research.
3. A Data and Knowledge-Driven Digital Twin Framework for Smart
Bridge O&M
This study proposed a BDT model driven by both data and expert knowledge to
address the limitations of traditional models concerning accuracy and intelligence. The
model comprises three main components: the spatial model, temporal model, and expert
Appl. Sci. 2025, 15, 231 5 of 25
knowledge model. These models work in unison to facilitate real-time health monitoring
and generate early warnings for bridges, such as alerts for structural damage, deformation,
or other critical anomalies.
The spatial and temporal models utilize high-resolution cameras, light detection and
ranging (LiDAR), and various sensors to collect multi-source data from the bridge structure,
including 2D image data, 3D point cloud data, and sensor time series data. The spatial
model primarily describes the bridge’s spatial characteristics through the modeling of 2D
image and 3D point cloud data, providing an accurate spatial representation of the bridge
structure. The temporal model focuses on the bridge’s physical characteristics, capturing
changes in internal physical states and long-term developmental trends under load.
The expert knowledge model consolidates insights from expert interviews, workshops,
industry standards, and historical case analyses. This aggregated knowledge is then
standardized and structured into an expert knowledge base, which integrates extensive
expertise in bridge maintenance and inspection to offer theoretical support and practical
guidance for evaluating and assessing bridge conditions. The spatial and temporal models
together form the data-driven module, which is responsible for collecting and processing
real-time information and developmental trends of the bridge structure. These data are then
transmitted to the expert knowledge-driven evaluation module. The evaluation module
automatically analyzes and assesses the data by incorporating knowledge from the expert
knowledge base, providing a comprehensive assessment of the bridge’s condition and
predicting its health status. Thus, the DTM, combining data-driven and knowledge-driven
approaches, forms a powerful bridge health monitoring and early warning system. The

especially valuable for large-scale bridges, where traditional methods struggle to achieve
comprehensive coverage of all areas. Moreover, this method ensures that the collected
images are of high enough resolution to detect even fine surface cracks. The use of UAVs

4.2.1. The 3D Point Cloud Data Collection
The acquisition of high-precision three-dimensional (3D) geometric data is critical for
bridge structural assessments, and LiDAR technology is widely employed for this purpose.
LiDAR sensors emit laser pulses and calculate distances to surfaces by measuring the
time of pulse returns, producing high-density point cloud data. Depending on the size
Appl. Sci. 2025, 15, 231 10 of 25
and characteristics of the bridge, two primary methods are utilized for data acquisition:
UAV-mounted LiDAR systems and stationary rack-mounted laser scanners.
For large-span bridges, such as suspension or cable-stayed bridges, UAV-mounted
LiDAR systems are deployed to capture detailed geometric data. These systems enable
comprehensive multi-angle scans, particularly for hard-to-access areas, including the
underside of decks and tall piers [ 31]. Flight paths are carefully designed using pre-
assigned coordinates to ensure complete coverage of critical structural components, such
as piers, decks, and girders. The flight altitude and scan frequency are calibrated to meet
the resolution requirements of complex structures. Additionally, UAV-mounted systems
are equipped with GPS and inertial measurement units (IMUs), which provide accurate
positioning and orientation data to ensure spatial alignment of the collected point cloud.
For smaller or medium-sized bridges, particularly those located in urban environ-
ments, stationary 3D laser scanners mounted on racks are typically employed. These
scanners are strategically placed around the bridge to capture precise geometric details
without requiring UAVs. Rack-mounted systems are particularly advantageous in settings
where UAV operation may be constrained by regulatory or environmental factors, such as
in densely populated urban areas.
The data collection process generates extensive high-density point cloud datasets
that capture key geometric features of the bridge, including cracks, surface irregularities,
and localized damage. To produce a unified 3D model, point cloud data from different
perspectives are aligned using advanced registration algorithms, ensuring spatial consis-
tency and completeness. This processed point cloud data form the foundation for subse-
quent applications, including geometric modeling, deformation analysis, and structural
health monitoring.

4.3.1. Sensor Deployment and Data Acquisition
In BDT modeling, an effective sensor network is essential for acquiring high-quality
data that accurately reflect the condition of the bridge [ 43]. Each type of sensor plays a
distinct role in capturing specific aspects of the structure’s behavior, and their placement
must be carefully planned to ensure comprehensive monitoring.
Accelerometers are used to capture the dynamic responses of the bridge, particularly
vibrations induced by traffic loads, wind, and environmental forces [ 44]. These sensors
are typically installed on the main girders, deck, and critical load-bearing elements. Their
placement is designed to monitor vibrations across various sections of the structure, pro-
viding a comprehensive view of the bridge’s dynamic behavior. The data collected by
accelerometers are essential for identifying modal parameters, such as natural frequencies
and mode shapes, which help assess the structural integrity and detect abnormal vibrations
that may indicate damage or degradation.
Strain gauges are installed at key structural locations, such as piers, abutments, and
support beams, where significant stress is expected. These sensors continuously monitor
the strain in the bridge components, allowing engineers to measure how the structure
responds to loads over time. The data collected reflect the bridge’s deformation under
varying loads and provide insights into localized stress concentrations, which are critical for
identifying potential fatigue and material failure. Strain data are fundamental for long-term
health assessments, helping to detect early signs of structural weakening.
Temperature sensors are distributed across the bridge to monitor environmental and
material temperatures, particularly in areas prone to significant thermal expansion and
contraction [45]. These sensors are often placed on the deck, beams, and supports where
temperature-induced stress may occur. The data collected are essential for adjusting
other sensor measurements, such as strain and displacement, as temperature variations
directly affect these readings. Accurate temperature data are crucial for distinguishing
between temperature-driven structural changes and true material deformations, ensuring
the reliability of time series analysis.
Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) sensors are placed on the bridge’s key
components, such as the deck and towers, to measure displacements and monitor large-
scale deformations [ 46]. These sensors are particularly useful for tracking movements
due to settling, shifting foundations, or long-term environmental effects. The GNSS data
enable the monitoring of slow, cumulative deformations that can impact the stability of the
bridge over time. By providing precise measurements of large-scale shifts, GNSS sensors
are essential for evaluating overall structural stability.
The data collected from these sensors are time-stamped and synchronized to form
comprehensive time series datasets. These datasets undergo further preprocessing to
denoise and standardize the data for analysis. Once preprocessed, the data are suitable for
advanced time series modeling, such as long short-term memory (LSTM) analysis, which is
used to predict structural changes and potential failure points based on historical trends, as
shown in Figure 7.

4.3.3. Sensor Data Processing Using Long Short-Term Memory
LSTM networks are uniquely equipped to handle sequential data, making them ideal
for analyzing time series data, where both short-term variations and long-term patterns are
critical for making accurate predictions and issuing early warnings of structural issues [49].
LSTM models are an advanced variant of recurrent neural networks (RNNs), designed
to address the challenge of retaining relevant information over long sequences of data [50].
Unlike standard RNNs, LSTM networks incorporate memory units that help them retain
relevant information over extended time periods, enabling them to better capture patterns
in time series data. The memory is regulated by several gating mechanisms, which allow
the model to control the flow of information: deciding what to remember, what to forget,
and what to output at each time step.

cell state Ct—based on the previous state and the current input xt. The process is governed
by the following equations.

ration. When unexpected variations, such as sudden increases in stress or displacement,
are detected, the LSTM model generates an alert, prompting immediate investigation and
further analysis.
