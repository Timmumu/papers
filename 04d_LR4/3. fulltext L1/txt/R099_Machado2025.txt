ABSTRACT
The creation of structured informational models improves understanding of tunnel behavior throughout its 
service life and enables faster detection of defects, which is critical for hazard assessment and infrastructure 
safety. Recent studies highlight the importance of automation and 3D reconstruction in overcoming the limi -
tations of manual tunnel inspection and data management. Technologies such as Point Cloud Data (PCD) and 
advanced modeling techniques have enhanced inspection processes and model generation. However, significant 
challenges persist in the development of Digital Twins (DT), particularly in the integration of heterogeneous data 
sources. This paper presents a methodology that combines parametric modeling technics with field data to 
develop an automated procedural modeling framework for tunnel inspection Digital Twins that integrates het -
erogeneous data and optimizes lifecycle management. To support this goal, the framework also aims to: (i) 
automate modeling workflows; (ii) enable the creation of a structured databank; (iii) improve inspection accu -
racy; and (iv) introduce a new index, the Mesh Decimation Quality (MDQ), to evaluate mesh quality during the 
decimation and digitization process. The approach was validated through a case study of the Coastal Tunnel in 
Brazil, for which an automated digital twin was created using PCD from the Faro Focus 3D X330 laser scanner. 
The MDQ index was applied to evaluate mesh resolution, ensuring model accuracy and controlling computa -
tional costs. Metadata from the physical asset was integrated using procedural code scripts. A proposed digital 
inspection approach, utilizing a procedural inspection grid, successfully identified defects and classified areas, 
demonstrating the method efficiency and accuracy. This framework highlights the feasibility of digitalizing 
tunnel inspections and integrating real-world data for improved infrastructure management, with potential for 
future expansion.
1. Introduction
Tunnels undergo structural degradation over time due environ -
mental conditions, aging, lining excessive loads, and uncertainties in 
geotechnical properties ( Baji et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2017; Montero 
et al., 2015 ). Deterioration in the tunnel lining undermines its integrity 
and may increase the risk of tunnel lining to eventually collapse ( Jiang 
et al., 2019 ). The inspection of these structures is a crucial process to 
comprehend how the impact of these factors impairs the structure and to 
manage maintenance.
Conventional inspection methodologies exhibit known disadvan -
tages, including lower time efficiency and challenges in precisely 
quantifying and locate defects ( Koch et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2022 ). To 
improve the inspection practice, several manuals and standards have a 
specific classification system which classifies the tunnel condition with 
different approaches and level of deterioration ( Poncetti et al., 2025 ) 
(Table A.1).
In general, the inspection practices and the condition classification of 
tunnels rely on visual inspections, with the documentation done in 
spreadsheets in the field, creating high subjectivity and dependency on 
the inspector ’ s experience and attention during the defect and condition 
documentation. Although there are some advances by the creation of 
manuals and the standardization of the tunnel inspection practice, some 
improvements are still necessary, specially by adopting the

Tunnels are critical infrastructures that significantly contribute to 
the economic and social well-being of populations and play a vital role in 
the development of transportation networks ( Petroutsatou et al., 2021 ). 
Regular inspections are essential to monitor the progression of structural 
pathologies over time, enabling the evaluation of both structural 
integrity and serviceability ( Sandroni & Labiouse, 2011 ). If left unde -
tected, such deteriorations may lead to severe structural degradation, 
potentially shortening the tunnel service life, disrupting traffic flow, and 
compromising user safety. These failures often result in economic losses 
and require costly remediation measures, while also increasing the risk 
of failure ( McKibbins et al., 2009 ).
To reduce the problems in the current tunnel inspection procedures, 
some researches intend to improve the practices by suggesting novel 
methods for structural condition evaluation ( Ai & Yuan, 2018 ), opti -
mizing the inspection frequency ( Ai et al., 2020 ), or applying technology 
to the inspection practices, such as using Non-destructive methods 
( Llanca et al., 2017 ) and robots ( Menendez et al., 2018 ) to improve the 
data acquisition, and using Digital Twins (DT) for a better documenta -
tion and visualization of the inspection activity.
A DT can be defined as a continuously evolving digital representation 
of the historical and real-time behavior of a physical asset or process 
( Dan et al., 2022; Glaessgen & Stargel, 2012 ). Constructed using real 
measurements and layers of field data acquisition, digital twins offer 
significant potential for infrastructure management ( Parrott and War -
shaw, 2017 ). However, realizing this potential entails challenges, 
particularly in managing data heterogeneity. Integrating and synchro -
nizing data from diverse sources is a complex and time-intensive task, 
which, as noted by Q. Lu et al. (2020) increases the risk of the digital 
twin becoming outdated. Additionally, while continuous inspection and 
monitoring practices generate larger datasets that enhance accuracy, 
they also demand more advanced data management systems. In the 
context of tunnel inspection, monitoring, and evaluation, digital twins 
can significantly improve inspection practices and overall infrastructure 
management.
An iterative virtual model for generating a tunnel DT able to interact 
with inspection and monitoring database enhances the tunnel evalua -
tion and documentation. This interactive Digital Twin is possible by 
integrating Point Cloud Data (PCD), parametric modeling, and proce -
dural modeling techniques. PCD enables precise tridimensional repre -
sentations, as demonstrated in studies such as Zhang et al. (2014) and R. 
Lu & Brilakis (2019) . Furthermore, Sun (2011) presents image acquisi -
tion for detecting deformations and defects in circular shield tunnels 
using mobile laser scanning, focusing on automated DT creation with 
high detailed geometry and damage integration. To evaluate the 
geometrical and structural integrity of tunnels, Loprencipe et al. (2018)
combined laser scanning, thermographic imaging, and ground- 
penetrating radar (GPR) with implementation scripts developed specif -
ically for clearance verification. Parametric modeling provides a robust 
approach for generating flexible geometric models, emphasizing the 
sequence of mathematical operations and the model construction pro -
cess, focusing on associative construction rather than on the final 
outcome ( Mafipour et al., 2023; Schultz et al., 2017 ).
While current inspection practices using laser scanning, photo -
grammetry, and manual reporting provide valuable data, they often 
suffer from fragmented workflows, limited automation, and high 
subjectivity in defect interpretation and quantification ( Insa-Iglesias 
et al., 2021; Stentoumis et al., 2015; Xue et al., 2021 ). Additionally, 
these approaches frequently lack seamless integration between data 
acquisition and model generation, hindering updates and reuse of 
models throughout the asset lifecycle. This disconnect significantly 
limits the implementation of Digital Twin methodologies, which depend 
on continuous synchronization between physical infrastructure and its 
digital counterpart, particularly in complex environments such as the 
irregular geometry of the Coastal Tunnel.
Understanding that three-dimensional reconstruction and the auto -
mation of structural defect identification and quantification are essential 
for enhancing inspection quality and meeting infrastructure needs, this 
paper focuses on implementing parametric modeling and field data 
extraction to automate modeling processes, improve inspection accu -
racy, and create a structured databank for the lifecycle management of 
tunnel digital twins. The objective of this study is to develop an auto -
mated procedural modeling framework for tunnel inspection DT, aiming 
to integrate heterogeneous data sources and support lifecycle manage -
ment through improved digital inspection and classification capabilities.
The workflow integrates field data obtained from detailed inspection 
reports and structural scans as inputs for a Grasshopper 1.0.0007 script. 
It involves surface creation, adaptive subdivision into an inspection grid, 
and automated classification of areas based on digital inspection data. 
Metadata containing relevant information is then assigned to areas of 
interest.
The methodology was validated through a real-world application in 
the Coastal Tunnel in Brazil. This approach enabled virtual inspections 
and established a virtualization environment that seamlessly integrates 
real data with detailed modeling. It allows the full execution of the in -
spection and classification campaign digitally within the scanned area, 
enabling updates and refinements based on acquired data and developed 
programming. By reducing subjectivity and incorporating Industry 4.0 
methodologies, this work represents a significant advancement in tunnel 
management practices.

tween the model and the infrastructure. Automation, a detailed geom -

allows creating a realistic 3D model able to adapt to the data input and 
visualization to being adaptable to different applications of the DT in the

Parametric modeling can be an important tool for integration and for 
creating a virtualization environment for the structure. For example, 
Sung et al. (2020) utilize visual programming and parametric modeling 
to quickly calculate the layout of a construction project in the field of 
civil engineering. Erharter et al. (2023) use this tool for parametric 
modeling and BIM model generation for the geology of Tunnel Angath. 
Utilizing advanced modeling techniques in the case study, such as 
parametric programming, allowed visualizing the irregular excavation 
geometry due to the modeling flexibility, meeting the demand for 
updating current methodologies for representing such structures.
The process uses mathematical standardizations with adjustable 
parameters to create geometry, which can be modified to enhance the 
accuracy or suitability of the model to field data or design adjustments 
( Fig. 1 ). The Grasshopper 1.0.0007 parametric modeling tool operates as 
a graphical interface for algorithm editing. It is a plug-in for the three- 
dimensional modeling software Rhinoceros 7 3D, enabling to create 
parametric 3D models and to generate complex geometries iteratively 
and flexibly. The visual algorithms consist of interconnected compo -
nents; each one performing a specific transformation or operation to 
adjust the components and modify the resulting geometry in an auto -
mated and flexible manner.
Fig. 1 illustrates the process of parametric modeling using graphical 
algorithms in the Grasshopper language. This approach is based on the 
interconnection of input data through a series of modeling operators. In 
the provided example, the parametric graphical operators are associated 
in parallel; the scale of the grid structure on the tunnel surface is 
increased by 20 % from the designated point and the division is also 
increased. The resulting new mesh, after implementing this process 
change, is made available at two different outputs, enabling a sequence 
of programming modifications and operator associations.
Thus, with minor adjustments, the entire model can be updated. This 
type of programming demands significant processing power, as more 
complex modeling structures require a complete recompilation of the 
framework when changes are made to the initial systems. Consequently, 
programming optimization becomes essential. Optimization adjust -
ments can be implemented without the need for an extensive series of 
associations. While this may result in some loss of control over the 
structure properties, it can significantly reduce computational costs.
3. Methodology
The proposed methodology is divided into two parts. First, it explains 
the inspection procedure, which serves as the input for generating the 
data input for digital twin. Second, it details the proposed parametric 
implementation in Grasshopper code, clarifying each step involved in 
constructing the automation process. This is crucial because each 
parameter and stage can be adapted flexibly to meet specific demands.
3.1. Inspection data generation
The standardization of inspection practices has a global scope, with 
various companies responsible for infrastructure while administrative 
associations pursue an ideal methodology ( ADIF, 2021; CeTU, 2015; 
FHWA, 2015; Yuan et al., 2012 ). Commonly applied practice involves 
categorizing areas within the structure using defect-sensitive classifi -
cation. Response data plays a crucial role in guiding and enhancing the 
decision-making system. Machado & Futai (2024) present a methodol -
ogy to create a virtual representation for the inspection data ( Fig. 2 ).
The inspection process involves creating a grid that divides the 
tunnel surface into zones: the roof, the right wall, and the left wall. These 
zones are further divided into three longitudinal areas, each spanning 
10 m in the transversal direction. This grid is used to locate structural 
defects and classify them into four service states, in which State I rep -
resents an intact condition and State IV indicates severe damage. Met -
adata information is then inserted into specific zones based on field data 
(see Machado & Futai (2024) for more details). However, some chal -
lenges are associated with this methodology. Tunnels often have irreg -
ular geometries, making it difficult to define specific locations and 
accurately register field inspection notes. To address this, Machado & 
Futai (2024) proposed using a zone of influence for classification: if a 
State I defect is located between two State II zones, it will be registered 
as State II, since the low level of detail in the model prevents the precise 
localization of pathologies. Although this approach does not fully solve 
the problem, it enhances safety by adopting a conservative approxima -
tion. The advanced modernization brought by Industry 4.0 can help 
overcome these challenges by enabling digital inspections, refining the 
inspection grid, creating more detailed models, and automatically 
inserting metadata and classifications.
The modernization of inspection practices in tunnel infrastructures is 
based on generating more detailed data about the structure, thus 
creating a database to optimize the decision-making process, and man -
aging the structure lifecycle. The methodological framework was based 
on infrastructure inspection, laser scanning of underground in -
frastructures, and modeling associated with this data. These foundations 
aim to meet the requirements for the virtual twinning of the tunnel.

Mesh decimation is a technique used to optimize and simplify the 
underlying shapes of a model by reducing the number of vertices, edges, 
and, consequently, surfaces that compose the polygons. In modeling, 
mesh decimation involves simplifying the level of detail/development

4. Results and discussion
This section is devided into two main subsections. The first focuses 
on the validation process for the methodology, whereby a generic rail -
way tunnel PCD was used to test the developed parametric model script 
and to calibrate its parameters. The second subsection involves applying 
the full methodology, including the inspection campaign data and the 
Grasshopper framework, to a real highway tunnel in Brazil.
4.1. Validation process
The validation process develops the proposed methodology and 
calibrates the parameters for tunnel field data infrastructure. The pro -
cess involves conducting tests and adjustments in the graphical code 
programming using a railway tunnel point cloud. The implementation of 
procedural modeling to automate the reconstruction of tunnel models is 
infrequently addressed in the literature. Therefore, the proposed 
framework and tests provide valuable insights into the application of 
this approach to such infrastructure.
4.1.1. PCD processing and optimization
Point cloud cleaning and segmentation are crucial steps in the result 
processing of laser scanning or other 3D mapping technologies data. 
These steps aim to eliminate inconsistencies, noise, and unwanted re -
gions from the point cloud, resulting in more precise and usable data. 
Filtering based on density, distance, duplicates, and outlier removal are 
common techniques employed for this purpose ( Oytun & Atasoy, 2022 ).
Distance filtering with outlier elimination was performed by defining 
the Region of Interest (ROI), and external points were removed using the 
Crop function facilitated by the Crockroach plugin, which allows point 
information editing without the need of conversion into editable ele -
ments. Given the complexity of directly editing point files, the point 
cloud was trimmed using editing tools within Rhinoceros 7 without 
requiring conversion into editable elements.
Mesh decimation was performed to improve manageability by 
applying density filtering to the raw acquired point cloud file using the 
PC reduce function, implemented by the open-source Point Cloud 
Components (PCC) 1.0 plugin. A new quality index, Mesh Decimation 
Quality (MDQ), is proposed to evaluate mesh quality after the decima -

The Hausdorff Distance is a commonly used metric that measures the

larger deviations. Face Normal Deviation (FND) quantifies the angular

and decimated meshes. This metric is used to assess distortions in shape 
and surface curvature introduced by decimation; however, it requires 
accurate face-to-face correspondence, which is typically evaluated using 
the Overlayed Area Relation (OAR).

4.1.2. Mesh generation
The mesh generation process primarily depends on a PCD suitable for 
the developed methodology. Regions with redundant points or high 
density significantly increase processing time, as described in the pre -
vious section. However, improper cleaning, particularly concerning 
outliers, can result in incorrect or inadequate meshes in terms of LoD. To 
improve the adaptation of the process to the specific characteristics of 
the real tunnel case, a railway unlined simple tunnel point cloud was 
tested ( Fig. 8 ).
Fig. 8 illustrates the application of the methodology to a railway 
tunnel during validation tests prior to practical implementation. The 
mesh construction, guided by the point cloud, is achieved using Poisson 
Surface Reconstruction through the open-source plugin Cockroach 0.01, 
which provides point cloud commands and post-processing capabilities 
within the Grasshopper graphical programming environment. The 
connection between the Normals and CloudMesh functions generates 
the surface from the field data information ( Fig. 3 ).
The absence of guide points at the connection between the walls and 
the floor of the structure presents challenges for mesh generation in 
these specific areas ( Fig. 8 .a). However, it is possible to minimize errors 
by adjusting the properties of the parametric model, reducing decima -
tion, and using raw data or modifying mesh generation parameters, 
although these processes increase computational cost. Areas with low 
point cloud density or missing data due occlusion effect, but without 
significant changes in directional continuity are filled by approximation, 
representing a substantial gain in the methodology ( Fig. 8 .b).
The trimming process for the mesh differs between longitudinal and 
transverse cuts ( Fig. 9 .a). Longitudinal cuts use an adjustable cutting 
plane, with its extensions and inclination relative to the axis defined as 
critical for the structure inclination. The script generates a median line 
from the point cloud and extends it to create a cutting plane. The lateral 
extension, height, and angle of this plane can be calibrated. Transverse 
cuts are defined in the next stage using arcs from the inspection grid, 
generated based on the mesh. Only arcs delimiting the walls are selected 
for trimming, while the first and last arcs serve solely as boundaries 
reference for the procedure.
The trim longitudinal code is presented and adjusted using scrolls in 
the Grasshopper programming environment ( Fig. 9 .b). These adjust -
ments are made to ensure effective intersection between the point cloud 
and the created surface. The longitudinal mesh trim involves creating a 
horizontal plane, which uses point cloud data to define the components 
for adjusting the cutting process, including plane inclination and the 
length of the cutting tool. The transverse trim ( Fig. 9 .c) involves three 
stages: The first step creates the arcs using the generated mesh, with the 
user defining the extension and boundary of the set of arcs limits. The 
second step closes the arcs and generates the loft connecting them.
Procedural modeling provides adjustable tools to meet specific 
modeling preferences or requirements. The transverse and longitudinal 
cutting algorithms adjust automatically, generating a model with min -
imal manual tuning. The loft creation function connects the transverse 
arches, producing separate meshes defined by the arches to generate the 
grid areas. A significant advantage of this approach lies in the flexibility 
to create the layers that compose the process. Fig. 10 illustrates this 
adaptability in the subsequent process of splitting areas for the inspec -
tion grid, allowing it to adjust to different patterns and methodologies 
used by companies conducting integrity analyses.
4.1.3. Metadata information attribution for model database
In the final stage of the automated creation of the digital twin, the 
process of inserting metadata into the generated three-dimensional grid 
is divided into two steps. First, data from the structure inspection 
identify structural defects such as leaks, cracks, peeling, and others, and 
classify the grid areas ( Fig. 2 ). Next, a.csv file is created, containing 
numbers corresponding to each classified area of the grid. This file is 
then integrated into the algorithm, which separates the state of each 
area into layers and automatically applies colors ( Fig. 11 ).
Fig. 11 illustrates the sequence of area classification insertions into

The open-source EleFront 5.1.8 plugin was used for metadata

4.2. Coastal tunnel application
The automated construction for inspection digital twin for the 
structure was implemented in a real highway tunnel (Coastal Tunnel). 
The tunnel awaits subsequent road connection works before it can begin 
operations ( Fig. 13 ). Located near the shore coastline in the southeastern 
region of Brazil, the Coastal Tunnel plays crucial economic and social 
roles, respectively by facilitating the transportation of materials and for 
connecting important tourist destinations in the country.
The decision about the specific tunnel for the framework application 
is primarily due to the irregular geometry resulting from the shotcrete 
lining of the design. The conventional virtualization process would rely 
on numerous approximations, neglecting potential construction issues 
or overbreak zones. Using PCD to feed the procedural programming 
results in a more detailed indoor mapping and can connect structural 
information to the model, creating a metadata-connected replica and 
database for the infrastructure.
The proposed data acquisition strategy and modeling framework are 
designed to be adaptable and may require minor adjustments for 
application in tunnels with different geometries or environmental con -
ditions, particularly in cases where point cloud coverage is limited or 
occlusion areas are more prominent. Tunnel surface geometry is often 
influenced by the type of lining material. Based on the list of common 
tunnel linings provided by FHWA (2015) , it can be inferred that mate -
rials such as steel, precast concrete, or masonry tend to produce regular 
surfaces, while shotcrete, unlined, or timber-lined tunnels result in more 
irregular geometries and occlusion issues. Procedural modeling tech -
niques ( Fig. 8 .b) help address gaps in PCD coverage; however, scanning

4.2.1. Field data acquisition (Sensitive inspection and Scanning)
The PCD acquisition was performed using the Faro Focus 3D X330 
laser scanning equipment in setup configuration resolution 1/5 and 
quality 4x, for its long-distance measurement capabilities ( Fig. 14 ). The 
scanning was conducted at approximately 15 m from the portal, 
covering a sweep of about 60 m in a single reading. The wait time for 
acquiring the scan data and capturing colors through images is 
approximately 10 min, of which 6 min are allocated for creating the 
point cloud and 4 min for color capture. This acquisition process results 
in a point cloud density of approximately 26.5 million points.
The laser scanner captures point data with varying densities 
depending on the distance from the object and the angle of incidence. As 
the distance increases, both resolution and precision tend to decrease, 
which may affect the quality and accuracy of the field data acquisition. 
Another source of bias involves occlusion areas, where the scanner 
cannot capture data due to physical obstacles or complex geometries, 
resulting in gaps in the point cloud, as illustrated in Fig. 8 .b.
The Faro Focus 3D X330 scanner has been previously validated in 
unlined tunnel environments for modeling purposes ( Cacciari and Futai, 
2017 ). This validation supports the reliability of the acquired data in this 
study, despite the natural limitations of the scanner, such as resolution 
variability and occlusion effects.
A sensitive inspection was conducted using a generic three- 
dimensional model created in Rhinoceros 7 for classification and 
direct acquisition of digital data. A tablet was employed to access the 
main hardware via internet and to acquire data directly into the main 
storage ( Fig. 15 ). The data on the geometry was directly recorded using 
the Rhinoceros 7 3D user text attribute function. The data was 
conventionally recorded in a 2D grid for a certain level of redundancy in 
the tunnel inspection process. The limitations of internet connectivity in 
a tunnel can be mitigated by combining Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite 
internet and Long Range (LoRa) technologies.

4.2.2. Parametric modeling with field data for the Coastal Tunnel
A single tunnel scan was employed as a systematic sampling 
approach. This decision aimed to evaluate the extent of mesh generation 
from a single scan and to calibrate the resolution of the data acquisition 
process. The selected PCD was collected near the tunnel portal, a region 
characterized by significant brightness variation to test color capture. 
Additionally, this area benefits from a stable internet connection and 
contains the highest concentration of diverse defect types (e.g., cracking, 
spalling, leakage, and construction-related issues).
The PCD was compiled and exported to Rhinoceros 7. The process of 
removing outliers and cleaning from the defined ROI is carried out by 
cutting using the Volvox 0.3.0.0 Grasshopper plugin. The point cloud 
enters as a set of points, and separates these points in individual value 
results in high processing demand; each of the millions of points has its 
coordinates. Therefore, for removing the region of interest from RAW 
data, the use of specific PCD cutting tools is recommended. In subse -
quent steps, localized geometry adjustments can be made using the 
surface conversion methodology presented in Fig. 4 .
The filter process started with a dataset of over 26 million points 
acquired from raw TLS field data. The reduction methodology was 
applied to optimize modeling while ensuring no loss of resolution during 
surface creation, as shown in Fig. 7 . Therefore, automatic reconstruction 
programming was applied to the treated point cloud, constructing the 
structure with procedural modeling. In a subsequent process, the 
transverse cutting arcs were selected for transversal trim.
Fig. 16 shows the properties of the final adopted mesh after the 
decimation process. In Fig. 16 .a, the significant reduction in mesh res -
olution is evident, aimed at improving workability and at reducing 
computational costs. Fig. 16 .b displays the final mesh and the calculated 
indexes for the process. The MDQ increased, while the AD decreased 
significantly, and the OAR remained relatively stable. The mesh fits well 
for creating the inspection digital grid, validating the proposed MDQ 
index.
4.2.3. Digital inspection and parametrical implementation
A key aspect of digital twin construction is the Fit Line (Fline) 
function ( Fig. 8 .b), which generates a guide axis based on point cloud 
averages. This function depends on PCD point density, and on the 
experimental design, Fline required three-dimensional position adjust -
ments using the 3D Rotate function. The same adjustment was applied to 
plane cut inclination for longitudinal cutting and to guide arc creation, 
which defines grid classification areas. In the Coastal Tunnel, TLS- 
generated point clouds ensure uniform data distribution, enabling 
Fline to construct an accurate axis for the methodology.
The methodology developed combines procedural modeling pro -
gramming with colored point cloud data to classify structure degrada -
tion levels virtually. It utilizes the ROI of the point cloud extracted by 
scanning and generates grid areas based on it, facilitating accurate 
classification according to company standards ( Fig. 17 ). The process 
greatly relies on the structure luminosity since the TLS captures data 
from images.
Fig. 17 presents a result from the next phase of programming, which 
involves extracting the tunnel assessment grid curve. This methodology 
enables the full execution of the inspection and classification campaign 
digitally within the scanned area, allowing updates and refinements 
based on acquired data and developed programming ( Fig. 17 .b).

min), and digital detection time (around 8 min), which included grid 
adjustment, mesh generation, and defect analysis. Overall, the digital 
inspection required less than half the time of the traditional method, 
while offering improved accuracy in defect identification and 
localization.

4.2.5. Advancements beyond existing methods
The proposed framework introduces significant innovations 
compared to conventional inspection practices and previously reported 
Digital Twin (DT) approaches. It applies procedural modeling for the 
first time to tunnel DT lifecycle management, allowing flexible and 
parametric generation of inspection models and grids that adapt to 
diverse tunnel geometries. This enhances reconstruction accuracy, re -
duces occlusion effects, and significantly decreases processing time, 
supporting consistent updates throughout the asset lifespan.

5. Conclusion
The methodology developed for constructing a digital twin in tun -
nels, using parametric and procedural modeling, has proven effective in 
advancing the storage of virtualized information for managing and 
optimizing decision-making in infrastructure. The integration of new 
technologies and Industry 4.0 developments into assets of significant 
economic and social importance helps mitigate economic losses while

enhancing safety and longevity.
However, the methodology has limitations dependent on external 
factors or requiring adjustments during pre-processing. For instance, 
internet access is necessary to connect the inspection process to the main 
server. Point cloud density depends on scanning equipment and site 
characteristics, which can affect accuracy. Additionally, three- 
dimensional rotation adjustments may be required during pre- 
processing in the Fline feature, although these are relatively simple 
due to the flexibility of procedural modeling.
The MDQ index is introduced to ensure mesh workability and qual -
ity. This parameter is practical for guiding the meshing process: an 
’ acceptable ’ MDQ range indicates that the mesh resolution is sufficient 
to generate the tunnel mesh and grid without losing critical geometric 
information, while also optimizing computational cost. In contrast, a 
’ poor ’ MDQ may lead to inaccuracies in the model, potentially distorting 
the spatial allocation of inspection locations. These thresholds are 
crucial in ensuring that mesh quality meets the precision requirements 
necessary for reliable decision-making in digital tunnel inspection and 
modeling scenarios.
A digital twin demands a highly detailed model based on precise data 
from the real structure to minimize approximations. It is crucial for the 
twin to be linked to real asset information, which should be processed 
and integrated into the model efficiently. The digital replication of the 
Coastal Tunnel was performed using point cloud data from the Faro 
Focus 3D X330 TLS, and the proposed MDQ index proved effective for 
creating the mesh grid. Mesh construction was handled using Cockroach 
programming, leveraging the flexibility of procedural modeling to 
ensure a high LOD. Metadata integration from the real structure was 
automated using EleFront 5.1.8, and Grasshopper 1.0.0007 scripts with 
complementary open-source plugins facilitated efficient automation, 
detailing, and connection between real and virtual assets.
The framework developed for the Coastal Tunnel provides a digital 
inspection with a databank integrated into the tunnel model. The results 
meet digital twin requirements, delivering a virtualized, automated 
model connected to the asset for efficient management, identifying 
specific defects, improving the overall structure knowledge and main -
tenance plan optimization. There is potential for expanding the twin 
with additional layers of information, which will be explored in future 
research.
The results demonstrate the creation of automated geometric models 
from PCD data, virtualization of the inspection process, and metadata 
integration into the digital twin model. The framework offers flexibility 
and adaptability for various infrastructures and demands, enhancing 
tunnel lifecycle management and decision-making using Industry 4.0 
technologies.
