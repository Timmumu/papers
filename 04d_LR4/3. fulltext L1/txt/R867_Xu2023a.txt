ABSTRACT 
Structural health monitoring (SHM) is a critical component of ensuring the safety of buildings, yet the effective 
management of sensors and their corresponding monitoring data is complex. Thus, a digital twin health moni -
toring information model for buildings was proposed to simultaneously locate sensors in space and monitor data. 
We described the requirements of this process and designed a conceptual model by combining a building in -
formation model (BIM) and a real-scene 3D model. The key steps in the fusion process include reconstructing the 
BIM into sub-BIMs considering building component types and geometric transformations, 3D spatial position 
registration with a rigid-body transformation method, and semantic information mapping. After the fusion 
process, monitoring data from sensors are stored in a database management system and integrated with the fused 
model. A digital twin model of the Nanjing Museum Old Hall was constructed as a case study, which evaluated 
the fusion method ’ s feasibility and usability. 
1. Introduction 
Ensuring the safety of buildings is a critical component of urban 
disaster prevention. In addition to external factors, such as natural di -
sasters, the safety of buildings can also be affected by internal factors 
such as material performance degradation and component creeps [39] . 
However, structural health is dynamic, and when the health status of a 
building varies greatly from the design expectations, accidents may 
result. For example, the Jiaxin Hotel in Quanzhou, China unexpectedly 
collapsed in March 2020, causing 29 deaths and 42 injuries during its 
reconstruction
1
. In another incident, 53 people died when an old 
building in Changsha City was suddenly damaged and collapsed in April 
2022.
2 
To prevent further loss of life owing to lapses in building safety, 
ongoing monitoring is required. Thus, structural health monitoring 
(SHM) technology has been widely used to collect and analyze data to 
diagnose structural health status using non-destructive sensors arranged 
inside the structure [22] . 
Early studies and applications of SHM in civil engineering focused on 
projects related to water conservation, such as bridges and dams [18] . 
However, with the increase in the construction of large or structurally 
complex buildings, SHM technology has been gradually introduced into 
the field of building engineering. Multiple aspects of SHM technology 
have been investigated, such as high-performance sensors, data collec -
tion, automatic monitoring systems, and methods of identifying struc -
tural damage [4,15,36] . 
However, accurately evaluating and predicting a building ’ s health 
state based on SHM data is challenging owing to the diversity in the 
structural forms of building engineering, the complexity of the internal 
structural components (e.g., beams, plates, and columns), and the sub -
stantial variation in the states of these two factors [31] . To address this 
issue, numerous studies have focused on improving the methods of SHM 
data analysis. For example, to prevent the impact of anomalous moni -
toring data, Zhang et al. [38] described a simple and efficient approach 
that incorporates complementary information obtained from multi-view 
local binary patterns and the random forest algorithm to distinguish 
data anomalies. Additionally, Jayawickrem et al. (2022) reviewed the

In addition to the data-processing methods, data models are an 
important component of addressing the complexity of building safety. 
For example, the structural mechanics model supports the analysis of 
structural health using the finite-element method. In this study, a new 
building SHM information model based on digital twin technology was 
proposed to extend and complement the structural mechanics model. 
The objectives of this study were to: (1) manage the monitoring sensors 
located on the internal components of a building and their monitoring 
data in space and (2) analyze the external influences on the building. 
Subsequently, the association of the monitoring data of different struc -
tural components as a whole in real time helps restore the dynamic 
health state of the building. Furthermore, the external environment is 
another crucial factor that can cause damage to buildings. Thus, the 
second objective of our study was to analyze the external influences on 
the building. The model offers a foundation for monitoring data man -
agement, analysis, and the dynamic representation of the structural 
health state. 
Digital twin technology refers to the process of mapping an object in 
the real world to create a highly accurate digital representation and 
reflecting the information collected in the real world onto the digital 
model to make predictions based on the simulation, analysis, and visu -
alization of the natural world [27]. A digital twin maps the attributes, 
structures, states, performances, and functions of physical entities and 
systems within a virtual world [6]. Grigor et al. [14] addressed the 
methodological development of structural simulation, analysis, and 
control models for historic buildings using the concept of digital twin 
technology and demonstrated that developing a digital twin model for a 
historical building allows for more extensive usage of SHM data. Addi -
tionally, these models facilitate a greater understanding of the real-time 
structural behavior of complex buildings to ensure general structural 
safety with minimal technical interventions. Jiang et al. [17] introduced 
the concept of a digital twin within the field of bridge engineering and 
established a general, non-deterministic, digital twin-driven multiscale 
fatigue life prediction framework for steel bridges, which enhanced the 
understanding of the fatigue evolution mechanism throughout the 
bridge lifecycle. 
The direct exploration of establishing a digital twin model for 
building an SHM system is limited and requires further investigation. 
However, studies on the potential applications of the digital twin 
concept in evaluating smart cities may provide a point of reference. 
Batty [3] analyzed the construction methods of digital twin cities, 
including that of high- and low-frequency cities. Similarly, White et al. 
[30] analyzed the modeling and construction of a digital twin smart city 
and found that it is possible to create an accurate digital twin by 
combining a large BIM with the big data generated by the Internet of 
Things (IoT) sensors. Further, Jaume et al. [16] conceptualized the use 
of digital twins in urban management and defined the basic structure of 
a city’s digital twin. Additionally, Du [12] reported that creating digital 
twin buildings may involve constructing physical information systems 
that use buildings as carriers and utilize BIMs, geographic information 
systems (GIS), the IoT, artificial intelligence, big data, and other tech -
nologies. In comparison, Zhu et al. [40] introduced digital twin tech -
nology in the construction of the Sichuan–Tibet railway and explored 
the methods for classification and coding of physical elements. 
As shown above, realizing a digital twin model of building health 
monitoring is important by combining a BIM and a real-scene 3D model. 
In particular, BIM technology can provide the distribution details of a 
building’s internal components (such as beams, plates, and columns) in 
a 3D form and integrate information from construction to operation to 
provide a foundation for collecting and managing a building’s health 
data from monitoring sensors. To this end, BIM technology has been 
introduced into the process of monitoring building health, including the 
visualization of monitoring information, comprehensive management of 
structure monitoring information, and establishment of a structural 
safety early warning system [25,23,19,29]. For example, Boddupalli 
et al. [5] adopted a BIM as a health monitoring computing and digital 
platform to organize and visualize health monitoring data and verify the 
advantages of BIM technology in these capacities. Further, Yang [34] 
and Deng et al. [11] explored the application of BIMs in SHM visuali -
zation, while Theiler and Smarsly [28] proposed a BIM-based health 
monitoring information description model by extending the industry 
foundation class (IFC) model. This model uses IFC as a formal basis to 
describe the information of the SHM system and promote the develop -
ment of the interoperability between the SHM systems. 
Although BIMs provide the internal location information of a struc -
ture for monitoring sensors, which is conducive to analyzing the re -
lationships among different structural components, health monitoring 
systems should also focus on the impact of a structure’s external envi -
ronment. For example, the impact of surrounding disturbances and 
seismic waves should be monitored. Therefore, research on the SHM of a 
building based on its spatial location is required. 
One such tool that may be utilized for this purpose is GIS, which is 
essential for urban information analysis [33], emphasizes the 
geographical and spatial characteristics of objects, and provides accu -
rate positioning and spatial analysis of buildings. An important devel -
opment in the current GIS field is the real-scene 3D model, which results 
from the oblique photogrammetry method, and possesses the charac -
teristics of high accuracy, fidelity, and measurability and has become an 
important technology in the construction and management of smart 
cities [10,35]. Recently, the Chinese government issued a notice to 
promote the construction of a 3D realistic geospatial scene in China, 
aiming to develop new 3D geospatial information products to describe 
and represent the real 3D spaces of industrial production, living envi -
ronments, and ecological development [8]. However, the real-scene 3D 
model represents the “skin,” or exterior, of the building without the 
interior component information, which inhibits its capacity to meet 
sophisticated needs such as indoor navigation, fire evacuation, and 
building construction. 
In summary, it is necessary to merge the complementary real-scene 
3D model and BIM to establish an accurate digital twin model of 
buildings for SHM. Chen et al. [9] explored feasible approaches for 
converting IFC models into 3D Tiles based on an open-source solution 
for visualizing BIM in Cesium. Following the open-source solution, we 
aim to merge a BIM and a real-scene 3D model to create a digital twin 
model for building SHM. The remainder of this paper is organized as 
follows: Section 2—the conceptual model of the digital twin, Section 
3—the proposed methodology for merging a BIM and a real-scene 3D 
model, Section 4—the application of the methodology to the Nanjing 
Museum, and Section 5—conclusions. 
2. Conceptual model of the digital twin 
2.1. Analysis of model requirements 
To monitor and manage a buildings’ health information in real-time, 
its digital twin model needs to meet three requirements, as shown in 
Fig. 1: (1) The ability to manage structural information such as the 
building’s size and geometric parameters, as well as semantic informa -
tion, such as the materials used for beams, plates, and columns and their 
prefabricated assembly. The structural information of buildings can be 
used for dynamic and static mechanical analysis of structures under the 
influence of the environment. (2) The ability to describe the external 
shape of the building in terms of its location, contour, and geometric 
shape. This information can be used to analyze the relationship between 
the building and its surroundings, such as the impact of foundation pit 
excavation on surrounding buildings during the construction period or 
the impact of surrounding disturbances on the building. (3) The ability

to manage the build-state monitoring information. In addition, the 
model should support managing dynamic monitoring information, such 
as stress, displacement, and vibration, from various sensors. These 
sensors collect real-time information on the external representation of 
the building structure and internal changes in the structure. 
Building structure information, external shape information, and 
sensor data can be considered the geometric and attribute information of 
a GIS, and existing GIS data models can be referenced. The BIM is 
convenient for managing building component information and is widely 
used in the construction industry. However, this model can also repre -
sent and manage building information during the planning, design, 
construction, and maintenance periods. Therefore, we selected the BIM 
to represent and manage the building structure information in this 
study. The real-scene 3D model provides accurate geographic location 
information, realistic effects, and accurate measurements. Therefore, we 
selected a real-scene 3D model to describe the geographic information of 
the building. Combining a real-scene 3D model with a BIM can meet the 
first two requirements outlined above and provide a foundation for 
locating sensors and analyzing the surrounding environment. For the 
third requirement, the building SHM data are essentially structured data 
that can be managed using a database. The monitoring data were then 
easily associated with the geometric data of the fused model as attribute 
data for the GIS model. Therefore, the fusion of a BIM and a real-scene 
3D model was the key to building a digital twin model in this study. 
2.2. Selection of data standards for the model fusion 
A basic model for converting a BIM into a real-scene 3D model is 
required to support fusion and combine BIMs and real-scene 3D models. 
For the GIS model, we selected a real-scene 3D model as the target/ 
foundation model for fusion because it simultaneously manages the 
geometry and dynamic monitoring data of a building. Thus, the data 
must first be transferred from the BIM to a real-scene 3D model to merge 
the two models. 
In recent years, researchers and engineers have developed many data 
standards for BIMs and real-scene 3D models. The optimal data standard 
that is a good starting point (standard of the source model) and endpoint 
(standard of the target model) for the fusion must be addressed. Several 
professional BIM software programs, and their corresponding model 
standards, are available including: the RVT extension of Autodesk ’ s 
Revit product files, the CATIA program used by Gehry Technology, and 
the PLN files created with Graphisoft ArchiCAD. Furthermore, the IFC 
model has become a widely used BIM standard [1] . This standard was 
formulated and managed by the International Alliance for Interopera -
bility and displays the characteristics of stability, openness, and integ -
rity. Therefore, the IFC standard was selected as the source model 
standard for the model fusion in this study. 
A real-scene 3D model is generally created using the oblique 
photogrammetry method to provide a near-real 3D model of physical 
objects. Many commonly used data standards have been produced for 
real-scene 3D modeling programs such as Astrium ’ s Street Factory, 
photoscans, and PIX4Dmapper. The commonly used model standards for 
real-scene 3D models include OpenSceneGraph (OSGB), object file 
(OBJ), 3D Tiles, and S3c. Among them, the 3D Tiles model is an open- 
source standard that is easily accessible to the public and provides 
good support for rendering the 3D Digital Earth [37] . Therefore, the 3D 
Tiles model was used as the target standard for the model fusion in this 
study. The basic structure of the 3D Tiles standard is shown in Fig. 2 . The 
tiles in the model store all 3D geographic information data. The batched 
3D Model (b3dm) standard tiles are suitable for mass 3D model loading, 
support rich texture and material information, and are widely used to 
represent buildings. The data in the “ tileset.json ” file describes the tiles 
and the storage of the spatial organization structure of the tiles. 
3. Merging a BIM with real-scene 3D model 
As mentioned in the previous section, the key to model fusion is 
transferring the BIM from the IFC standard to the real-scene 3D model in 
the 3D Tiles standard. The difficulty in fusion lies in transforming the 
BIM ’ s geometric, spatial, and semantic information. The fusion frame -
work is designed as shown in Fig. 3 . The BIM of buildings in the IFC 
standards must undergo a series of transformation processes, which 
mainly include four steps: (1) BIM reconstruction, (2) geometric infor -
mation conversion, (3) spatial position registration, and (4) semantic 
mapping of the construction information. During the BIM reconstruction 
step, the model must be split into sub-BIMs according to their compo -
nent types. Subsequently, their geometric and semantic information is 
obtained, which lays the foundation for geometric and semantic infor -
mation transformation. The geometric information conversion step

3.1. BIM reconstruction 
A BIM contains geometric and semantic information for several 
building components, such as the size of walls and the specifications for 
column materials. A BIM can easily lose semantic building information 
when directly converted into a model with a 3D Tiles standard. To avoid 
this, we first deconstruct the BIM and then reconstruct it into a set of 
BIMs with multiple sub-components. Each sub-component model uses 
the IFC standard to store geometric information, such as beam1.ifc, 
beam2.ifc, and column1.ifc. These sub-components store the corre -
sponding semantic attribute information in an attribute file with the 
same name. The attribute file is stored in JavaScript Object Notation 
(JSON) format, which is a lightweight data-exchange format suitable for 
transmitting and exchanging text data [7] . The BIM reconstruction 
process is illustrated in Fig. 4 . 
In this process, accurately splitting the BIM, forming sub- 
components, and accurately linking and saving the geometric and 
attribute information are crucial for reconstruction. Building compo -
nents were classified based on their type, including building columns, 
structural columns, walls (such as internal partitions and curtain walls), 
stair railings, ramps, doors, and windows. A few software packages can 
be used for reconstruction operations, such as a secondary development 
method based on Autodesk Revit. Considering software copyright, an 
open-source software BIM server was used to reconstruct the BIM. As a 
commonly used open-source software in the BIM field, the BIM server 
provides IFC file management, data parsing, and formatted output and 
can directly open the model in the RVT standard. With the support of the 
application programming interface (API) of the BIM server, we devel -
oped a program to implement a series of reconstruction processes, such 
as sub-component traversal, component type discrimination, semantic 
information reading, and component data export. 
The type of each sub-component is queried by traversing all sub- 
components in the BIM. The following types of sub-components are 
available: stair railings, doors, and windows. These sub-components are 
identified as the ones to be reconstructed; their attribute information 
will be explored further. The reading and parsing rules vary depending 
on the geometric characteristics of components, which are mainly 
divided into three categories: (1) A simple component — conventional 
read-and-parse statements of the BIM server are used to obtain the 
component geometric and semantic information, such as windows, 
doors, and columns. (2) Components with holes — in addition to reading 
and parsing the normal information of the component, nested hole in -
formation, such as walls, must be read and parsed. (3) Objects con -
taining composite components — in addition to reading and parsing the 
components ’ normal information, the composited components ’ sub- 
parts need to be read and parsed according to associative properties, 
such as stairs containing railings, stair runs, and floors.

3.2. Geometric information transformation 
The geometric information in the IFC standard cannot be directly 
converted into a 3D Tiles standard. Therefore, an indirect conversion 
method is adopted. First, the OBJ format is selected as the intermediate 
exchange format. This object file format was a standard 3D model 
developed by Alias Wavefront. This standard is recorded in text and is 
widely used to exchange 3D software models. Subsequently, the OBJ 
format was converted to the GL transmission format (glTF) in the real- 
scene 3D model. The glTF format was converted to the b3dm format. 
The tile file in the 3D Tiles was established to realize the geometric in -
formation conversion of BIM using the b3dm file. 
During the geometric transformation process, an open-source tool, 
IfcConvert ( https://ifcopenshell.org/ifcconvert.html ), was used to pro -
vide the conversion function from the IFC format to the OBJ format. To 
fully convert geometric information from the IFC format to the b3dm 
format, it is necessary to focus on the transformation from the OBJ to the 
b3dm format. The main part of the b3dm model that describes the 
geometric element information is in binary glTF format, which can 
reduce redundant data irrelevant to rendering in a 3D format and is a 3D 
file format suitable for OpenGL loading. With the development of 3D 
technology, various 3D standards have emerged, such as Blender, Maya, 
and Lightwave3D. However, glTF is committed to providing an ex -
change format for web 3D models. 
The data structure framework of the glTF format differs from that of 
the OBJ format. As shown in Fig. 5 , the conversion of vertex coordinates, 
external normal vectors, texture coordinates, texture images, and other 
information in the OBJ standard is required. The glTF bin file stores the 
basic geometric data; therefore, it is necessary to save building vertex 
coordinates, external normal vectors, and texture coordinates in the 
OBJ. Texture images of buildings were stored in formats including glTF, 
PNG, and JPG. In OBJ, triangular patches are drawn using a vertex index 
to form the geometry. With glTF, a mesh of primitive arrays was used to 
represent a single complex geometry. Each primitive array contains the 
corresponding vertex attributes and textures referenced in a bin file. 
Therefore, setting the vertex traversal mode is vital for ensuring that the 
grid can be converted correctly. 
Several glTF conversion tools have been developed, including the 
relatively sophisticated obj2gltf, an open-source library launched by 
Analytical Graphics, Inc. ( https://github.com/AnalyticalGraphicsInc 
/obj2gltf ). Based on the process shown in Fig. 5 , the OBJ standard was 
converted to glTF using the tool obj2gltf. 
3.3. 3D spatial position registration 
The 3D Tiles standard is a GIS model that typically has a spatial 
coordinate system, such as the WGS84 world coordinate system. The 
BIM coordinate system in the IFC is typically a local coordinate system 
that can describe the scale and size of a building. However, it is neces -
sary to register the spatial positions of the two models under 3D regis -
tration to correctly display the BIM in the GIS. 
The source model was the BIM and the real-scene 3D model was set 
as the target model. The iterative closure point (ICP) algorithm is widely 
used, particularly in the point-cloud field, when considering multiple 
algorithms for registering 3D spatial models. The implementation pro -
cess of the ICP algorithm involves repeatedly selecting the tie-point pairs 
in two point clouds and calculating the optimal rigid-body trans -
formation until the convergence accuracy requirements for correct 
registration are satisfied [32,20] . The ICP algorithm is suitable for 
registering multi-station ground point clouds and can achieve good re -
sults. The coordinate system of BIM differs considerably from that of a 
real-scene 3D model. However, the ICP algorithm has weaknesses in 
registering BIMs, such as the slow speed of iterative convergence and 
low accuracy. 
The BIM and real-scene 3D model have prominent rigid-body char -
acteristics, and both models have good geometric characteristics 
conducive to determining the point pair relationship. Therefore, accu -
rate rigid-body transformation parameters can be calculated according 
to the tie-point pairs of the building. Afterwards, their fusion trans -
formation can be realized. The singular value decomposition (SVD) 
method in rigid-body transformations has the advantages of easy 
implementation, simple calculation, high speed, and reliable accuracy 
[26,2] . Therefore, this method is suitable for the registration of BIMs and 
real-scene 3D models. 
The first step of the registration process is to choose tie points that 
mainly adhere to the following criteria: (1) Tie points should be selected 
from visible feature points located on the building surface. Since 
buildings in a real-scene 3D model usually have external surface infor -
mation alone, it is unsuitable to use the internal points of the building as 
tie points. Instead, points on the building surface should be selected, 
such as wall corners, window corners, and building-surface structure 
joints, to improve the accuracy of point acquisition. (2) Tie points should 
be evenly distributed. The positions of the selected tie points should be 
reasonably distributed according to the scale of the building to avoid 
excessive concentrations or linear arrangements, which would affect the 
results. (3) There should be at least three tie points. The SVD method 
requires at least three groups of initial data points to determine regis -
tration parameters. The number of tie points can be appropriately 
increased to improve the calculation ’ s accuracy. After the tie-point pairs 
are selected, the registration calculation is performed, as shown in Fig. 6 .

3.4. Mapping semantic information 
A BIM includes the semantic information of sub-components, such as 
the beam material and concrete strength. The semantic information of 
the sub-components was stored in the corresponding JSON files through 
the previous reconstruction steps. Semantic mapping aligns the JSON 
semantic information of multiple sub-components in the BIM to the se -
mantic attribute information in the real-scene 3D model. However, it 
associates the semantic information of each sub-component with its glTF 
geometric information individually. Thus, the converted b3dm file was 
obtained and integrated into 3D Tiles. This accomplished the fusion of 
the BIM and real-scene 3D model. 
As shown in Fig. 7 , the b3dm file comprises a file header and body. 
The file header records the file type, version, size, and other relevant 
information. The file body includes a feature table, batch table, and 
binary glTF, which record the number of features, feature attributes, and 
geometric data. Therefore, the essence of semantic mapping is to place 
the glTF and JSON data of the sub-components into the corresponding 
blocks of the b3dm file and maintain their original relationships. 
In the semantic mapping process, the JSON file is first generated 
based on the name of the glTF geometry file of each sub-component. For 
example, wall0.json has been indexed according to wall0.gltf, and they 
have a common ID. The glTF data of the sub-components, such as wall0. 
gltf, is binarized and written in the binary glTF data block of the b3dm 
file. Meanwhile, a semantic information file, such as wall0.json, corre -
sponding to the sub-component is written into the batch table data block 
of the b3dm file. The identification (ID)between walls wall0.gltf and 
wall0.json was converted to a batch ID to maintain its connection. 
Finally, a tool is developed to realize the semantic mapping based on the 
open source 3D Tiles tool sample generator published by Cesium ( https: 
//github.com/AnalyticalGraphicsInc/3D-tiles-tools ). 
4. Application and discussion 
4.1. Case study 
The Nanjing Museum is one of the most important historical and 
cultural landmark buildings and the first large comprehensive museum 
invested by the state of China. The museum ’ s Old Hall has a long history 
spanning more than 70 years. In 2008, the museum was lifted through 
raising columns 3.0 m after cutting on the first floor. In addition, 
isolation bearings were installed at the base of the building for seismic 
reinforcement. With an increase in service time, the stiffness of the 
rubber isolation bearing is prone to increase, and the horizontal defor -
mation capacity decreases. A deterioration in the mechanical properties 
of isolation bearings affects the safety of isolation structures under 
strong earthquakes. Portions of the Old Hall ’ s structural components 
may be damaged by nature or humans, which in turn will impact the 
safety of the entire structure. Therefore, it is necessary to monitor the 
structural health of the Old Hall. To this end, 60 sensors were deployed 
for vibration isolation on nine abutments (A – I), as shown in Fig. 8 and 
Table 1 . Illustrations of the sensors are displayed in Fig. 9 . 
We developed a digital twin model for the Old Hall SHM system, 
based on the combination of a BIM with a real-scene 3D model, and the 
integration of sensor monitoring data. Tilt photos were acquired using a 
DJI Matrix 600 electric multirotor unpiloted aerial vehicle (UAV) with a 
five-lens tilt camera. The parameters of the UAV platform and cameras 
used in the aerial survey are listed in Table 2 . During the aerial survey, 
the azimuth overlap was 80 % and the lateral overlap was 75 %. As a 
result, more than 1600 aerial photographs were obtained, and the data

4.2. Discussion 
Monitoring a building ’ s health status is complicated and establishing 
an effective building health-monitoring information model is important. 
By combining a BIM and a real-scene 3D model, we established an SHM 
information model based on digital twin technology. This information 
model supports the sensor arrangement, analysis of sensing data, and 
representation of a building ’ s structural and mechanical status. The BIM 
provides a foundation for sensor location and mechanical state analysis 
of building structures. In contrast, the real-scene 3D model supports 
analyzing external environmental influences. The fused model in -
tegrates internal and external representations of a building. We propose 
that the fusion method can be applied to other fields, such as smart city 
planning, decision-making, building operations, and building 
maintenance. 
The challenges of fusing BIMs and real-scene 3D models include 
different organizational methods regarding geometry, semantics, and 
space. The IFC standard for BIMs was selected as the source in the fusion 
method. The 3D Tiles standard for the real-scene 3D model was selected 
as the target. Both open-source standards are convenient for converting 
and supporting related model standards. Semantic information is 
extracted before geometric transformation and restored after conver -
sion. Therefore, the semantic information of a BIM can be retained in the 
fusion process, which is essential for applying the fused model and 
constructing the digital twin. Following open standards, geometric and 
semantic information fusion adopts open-source software: a BIM server 
and Cesium library. This will assist in the promotion and application of 
the fusion method. 
The spatial positional information of the building was fused using a 
3D registration method. Based on the tie-point pairs of the two models, 
we used the rigid-body transformation method to determine the trans -
formation relationship between the two coordinate systems. The trans -
formation matrix is decomposed into several basic operations, namely, 
rotation and translation, using the SVD method. This makes the complex 
transformation easy to perform and realize. 
Considering the Old Hall of the Nanjing Museum as a case study, a 
fusion model was developed. The merging process between the BIM and 
real-scene 3D model can be automatically accomplished using a pro -
gram, excluding the tie-point selection. This was performed manually in 
this study. The automatic transformation process consumes approxi -
mately 25 min on a standard laptop (CPU is an Intel Core i7-10510U at 
2.30 GHz). In this case, the programs for each merging step are separate. 
In the future, we can unify all the programs and select the tie points as 
input conditions to automate and accelerate the entire merging process. 
The fused model results show that the buildings ’ geometric graphics 
and semantic information are preserved after fusion. Furthermore, the 
spatial positions of the two models are consistent with the external and 
internal scenarios of the building display. Thus, the micro-internal de -
tails of the building can be shown in a 3D macro space, and a 3D in -
formation model with a realistic scene and rich information can be built.

Based on the fused model of the Old Hall, a building SHM system was 
developed, in which 60 sensors for vibration, temperature, stress, and 
strain were arranged. The monitoring data were collected in real-time 
and integrated with the fused model, which formed the digital twin 
model for building health. The application to the Old Hall verifies the 
effectiveness of the BIM and real-scene 3D model fusion method that 
establishes a building health monitoring information model for a digital 
twin of the building. 
Although we have achieved the integration of a BIM and a real-scene 
3D model in terms of geometry, semantics, spatialization, and expres -
sion standards, a few unresolved issues remain. To effectively speed up 
the display of a real-scene 3D model, the level of detail method is 
typically used to establish different detail levels of the model. However, 
the level of detail method of the BIM is not established in the current 
fusion process, which decreases its loading speed. Furthermore, the BIM 
of a building may include numerous detailed components and large 
volumes of data, which may not be required to complete the process. 
Thus, simplifying and reducing the BIM is a problem worthy of further 
exploration. In addition, effectively analyzing the SHM data collected by 
all the arranged sensors is a challenge. The digital twin model can 
connect monitoring data from a spatial angle that offers a synthetic 
perspective to analyze the data, which needs further exploration. 
5. Conclusion 
Building safety is an important aspect of urban safety and disaster 
reduction. We propose a digital-twin-oriented building health moni -
toring information model. The model fuses a BIM with a real-scene 3D 
model, achieves the integrated management of a building ’ s internal 
components and external environment, and combines a building ’ s 
spatial shape and state. The greatest challenge in establishing a digital 
model is the fusion of geometric, semantic, and spatial location infor -
mation between the BIM and real-scene 3D model. This work was 
completed with the support of multiple open-source standards and 
software, such as IFC, 3D Tiles, OBJ, and BIM server, which make the 
fusion method easier to use. The fusion process applied a registration 
method based on rigid-body transformation and a BIM semantic trans -
formation method based on JSON. 
The fused model integrates the monitoring data by providing a 
spatial description of the monitoring sensors. The monitoring data of the 
sensors distributed in the local part of the building can be connected in 
space, thus providing a foundation and support for analyzing the health 
status of the building from the perspective of the entire building. Thus, 
the response status of the building structure under the influence of the 
external environment can be dynamically represented, such as through 
the stress, strain, and vibration of the components. Considering Nanjing 
Museum ’ s Old Hall as an example, we established a digital twin model 
and developed a building safety monitoring system based on this model, 
which preliminary verified the accuracy of our digital twin model. 
However, dynamically analyzing the overall health status of buildings 
according to monitoring data obtained locally requires further investi -
gation. The proposed digital twin model can provide an overall 
perspective and an integrated data management platform for different 
monitoring data from sensors arranged in a building. Future studies 
should focus on examining the dynamic inversion and real-time pre -
sentation methods for the overall health status of buildings based on the 
digital twin model.
