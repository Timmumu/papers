ABSTRACT Earthquakes pose severe threats to urban safety. Traditional prediction methods fail to
fully quantify seismic uncertainties and address low-probability, high-impact ‘‘long-tail risks’’. This paper
proposes a partially validated probabilistic urban seismic damage prediction framework integrating large-
scale scenario ensembles and digital twins. The framework is conceptually designed with four layers: data
fusion, digital twin modeling, scenario ensemble simulation, and probabilistic assessment. Due to current
data and computational constraints, empirical validation is limited to two core modules—the PI-GAN
ground motion generation module and the probabilistic damage cloud module—using the 2021 Y angbi
Ms6.4 earthquake (single event, single region). The remaining two modules (infrastructure cascading failure
and social behavior modeling) are presented as conceptual designs requiring future validation. The validation
results show that the Physics-Informed Generative Adversarial Network (PI-GAN) generates ground motions
with ≤5% PGA error and ≤10% response spectrum error, while the probabilistic damage cloud module
achieves 77.7% overall classification accuracy (with 79.5% recall, 87.3% precision, and 83.2% F1-score)
and 89.3% structure-damage matching degree. The framework enables a shift from empirical judgment to
data-driven pre-simulation, providing a partially validated and technically feasible solution for improving the
accuracy and comprehensiveness of urban seismic risk assessment, while clearly acknowledging that full-
chain validation—particularly for extreme M≥7.0 scenarios and unvalidated modules—remains as future
work.

A. BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
The suddenness and devastating nature of seismic hazards
remain a severe challenge to urban public safety. With
the increasing complexity of modern urban systems, key
infrastructure such as buildings, transportation, and energy
within cities are highly coupled, leading to seismic-induced

B. DIGITAL TWIN AND PROBABILISTIC SEISMIC RISK
ASSESSMENT
As a core enabling technology for deep integration of
cyber-physical systems, digital twin technology provides an
unprecedented platform for refined and dynamic disaster sim-
ulation at the urban scale [4]. It has been applied to construct
seismic response models of buildings [5], visualize seismic
damage simulation results [6], and even evaluate the seismic
resilience of urban lifeline systems [7]. Recent advances
in probabilistic seismic risk assessment have increasingly
incorporated stochastic rupture simulations within PSHA
frameworks, aiming to better characterize source and path
uncertainties [8], [9]. However, existing DT-based earth-
quake risk frameworks often lack integration with large-scale,
physics-consistent scenario ensembles and empirical valida-
tion against real seismic events. How to combine large-scale
seismic scenario ensembles with digital twin technology
for probabilistic and forward-looking urban-scale seismic
damage prediction remains a challenge requiring in-depth
research [10].
C. SCOPE AND VALIDATION STATUS OF THIS STUDY
It is important to clarify at the outset that the proposed
framework is presented as a conceptual architecture with
partial empirical validation. Specifically, the current study
validates two of the four framework modules: (1) the
PI-GAN-based ground motion generation module, and
(2) the probabilistic damage cloud module. The infrastructure
cascading failure module and the Agent-Based Modeling
(ABM) social behavior module are described in their concep-
tual design but have not been empirically tested against real
earthquake data. The validation is further limited to a single
moderate-magnitude event (2021 Y angbi Ms6.4) and a single
village (Huaian Village, 112 buildings). Generalizability to
other tectonic settings, urban typologies, or large-magnitude
(M≥7.0) scenarios remains unvalidated and is a direction
for future research. All claims of framework performance in
this paper should be interpreted strictly within these defined
boundaries.
D. MAIN CONTRIBUTIONS AND PAPER ORGANIZATION
The main contributions of this study are threefold: (1) a sys-
tematic integration of high-fidelity digital twin modeling with
physics-informed generative AI (PI-GAN) for ground motion
synthesis—with validation limited to moderate-magnitude
scenarios; (2) a full-chain probabilistic simulation workflow
that couples seismic source generation, site response, struc-
tural analysis, and infrastructure cascading failure within a
unified digital twin environment—where the infrastructure
and social modules remain conceptual at this stage; and
(3) an empirical validation of the core modules (PI-GAN and
damage cloud) using a real earthquake case (2021 Y angbi
Ms6.4 earthquake), demonstrating the framework’s practical
applicability and accuracy within the validated scope. Its
core idea is to abandon the pursuit of predicting a ‘‘most
likely’’ future, but instead systematically explore thousands
of ‘‘possible’’ futures through computational experiments
and statistically analyze their risk distribution. This study will
elaborate on the framework’s architecture, key technologies,
and implementation process, validate its core modules with
empirical data, and discuss how it can provide revolutionary
decision support for urban planning, resilience improvement,
and precise emergency response—while clearly distinguish-
ing demonstrated capabilities from conceptual extensions.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows:
Section II presents the overall integration framework.
Section III details the core algorithms and technical imple-
mentations, explicitly distinguishing validated modules from
conceptual designs. Section IV provides an empirical anal-
ysis of key modules using the 2021 Y angbi earthquake.
Section V discusses the framework’s applications, limita-
tions, and future roadmap. Section VI concludes the paper
and outlines future work.
II. INTEGRATION FRAMEWORK OF LARGE-SCALE
SCENARIO ENSEMBLES AND DIGITAL TWINS
The proposed prediction framework is essentially a complex
system integrating advanced sensing, refined modeling, high-
performance simulation, and intelligent decision-making. Its
core lies in establishing a closed-loop, bidirectional inter-
action between the physical city and its digital counterpart,
where real-time or near-real-time data from the physical
world (e.g., IoT sensor readings, InSAR deformation) are
continuously ingested to update and calibrate the digital
models, while simulation outputs from the digital twin (e.g.,
predicted damage states, cascading failure paths) are fed back
to inform monitoring, planning, and emergency response
in the physical city. This dynamic interaction enables a
panoramic, probabilistic exploration of seismic risks via com-
putational experiments. Logically, the framework is divided
into four closely coupled layers, collectively forming a closed
loop from real-world sensing to decision support. The overall
architecture, which explicitly illustrates the data and inter-
action flows between the physical city and the digital twin,
is shown in Fig. 1. The figure highlights the bidirectional syn-
chronization loops (physical→virtual and virtual→physical)
that are characteristic of a true digital twin system, moving
beyond a static conceptual diagram.

B. URBAN DIGITAL TWIN MODELING LA YER
In this study, the urban seismic damage digital twin is
defined as a digital mapping entity based on multi-source
heterogeneous data from air-space-ground integrated sensing,
which constructs a virtual system with full-element mapping
and bidirectional dynamic interaction with the physical city.
It integrates four core sub-models including geological-site,
engineering structure, infrastructure, and social behavior, and
possesses the capabilities of seismic scenario simulation,
damage probability prediction, and decision support, serving
as the core carrier for probabilistic seismic disaster analysis
at the urban scale.
The core components of the digital twin in this
framework include three layers: 1) Data layer: realizing
multi-dimensional digital characterization of the physical city
based on the disaster knowledge graph; 2) Model layer: com-
posed of four coupled sub-models with physical mechanism

and computational simulation capabilities; 3) Interaction
layer: realizing dynamic update of the digital model by the
measured data of the physical city, and reverse guidance of
the simulation results of the digital model to the disaster
prevention and mitigation decision-making of the physical

The rationality of naming the framework as ‘‘Probabilistic
Prediction Framework for Urban Seismic Damage Based on
Digital Twin’’ is that this system is not a traditional static
3D urban modeling, but possesses the core characteristics of
digital twin including ‘‘data-driven, cyber-physical mapping,
bidirectional interaction, and simulation decision-making’’.
It realizes the closed-loop linkage between the physical city
and the virtual city through digital twin technology, breaks
through the limitations of deterministic and single-scenario
analysis of traditional seismic simulation models, and con-
forms to the technical positioning of digital twin in the deep
integration of cyber-physical systems.
Above the data layer lies the core model of the
framework—the urban digital twin modeling layer. The goal
of this layer is not to create a static 3D urban model, but to
construct a dynamic, multi-scale, multi-physics coupled vir-
tual urban system with physical mechanisms and computing
capabilities. The construction of urban digital twins is evolv-
ing from single physical models to multi-dimensional cou-
pling. The Physical-Social-Information-Time (PSIT) four-
dimensional coupling framework proposed by Zhao et al. [11]
provides important reference for multi-dimensional model-
ing, and the new paradigm of global, dynamic, and intelligent
data body construction explored by Tang et al. [12] also
offers theoretical support for model development. The sys-
tem is constructed following the principles of modularization
and integration, mainly including four sub-models. These
sub-models are coupled to form a dynamic, multi-physics
urban digital twin—geological-site model provides ground
motion input, engineering structure model simulates building
damage, infrastructure system model replicates cascading
failures, and social behavior model simulates evacuation—
with their coupling relationship shown in Fig. 3. Their
validation statuses are explicitly distinguished as follows.
1) Geological and Site Model (V alidation Status: Empir-
ically validated as part of the ground motion generation
workflow)
This model serves as the foundation for accurately sim-
ulating seismic wave propagation and amplification effects.
It constructs a regional 3D stratigraphic structure and active
fault model based on detailed drilling data, geophysical
exploration data, and InSAR-derived deformation fields.
To simulate site-specific ground motion amplification, the
model embeds constitutive relationships that reflect the
nonlinear dynamic behavior of soil (e.g., viscoelastic or
elastoplastic models) and can incorporate 1D/2D/3D wave
propagation methods (e.g., equivalent linear analysis as
implemented in SHAKE-type programs or more advanced
numerical methods) where computational resources allow.
This allows for the accurate calculation of attenuation,

reflection, and site amplification effects of seismic waves
with different spectral characteristics as they pass through
complex geological media. Consequently, it provides dif-
ferentiated ground motion inputs for upper-layer structures.
In practice, for the Y angbi case study, regional borehole data
and geophysical survey reports were used to construct the
near-surface soil profiles. The widely used equivalent linear
method, implemented in a manner analogous to programs
like SHAKE [15], was then applied to compute site-specific
amplification.
2) Engineering Structure Model (V alidation Status: Empir-
ically validated through the DNN surrogate model and
probabilistic damage cloud analysis for the Huaian Village
building inventory)
To achieve efficient and accurate simulation of the seismic
performance of massive buildings at the urban scale, this
study adopts a ‘‘hybrid modeling strategy combining phys-
ical mechanism models and AI surrogate models,’’ which
can draw on the application experience of hybrid numerical
methods in building seismic protection assessment [8], [13].
For particularly important structures such as landmark super
high-rise buildings and large lifeline projects, refined solid
models based on the finite element method are used to capture
details of nonlinear force transfer and damage evolution. For
the large number of ordinary buildings, deep neural network
surrogate models between their dynamic responses and input
ground motion parameters are trained. However, the empiri-
cal validation in this study (Section IV) uses a DNN surrogate
model trained exclusively on 112 buildings from Huaian Vil-
lage, representing only two structural types (Frame/Masonry
and Brick-Wood/Earth-Wood). This model is site-specific
and requires complete recalibration with local data before
being applied to any other urban setting. The DNN surrogate
model is NOT claimed to be generalizable across different

3) Infrastructure System Model (V alidation Status: Con-
ceptual design only — not empirically implemented or tested
in this study)
Urban resilience depends not only on the seismic capacity
of individual buildings but also on the functional connec-
tivity of lifeline systems. Based on complex system theory,
this framework models transportation networks, power grids,
water supply networks, gas pipelines, and communication
networks as interdependent complex networks, which can
refer to the experience of constructing digital twin models
for urban lifeline engineering systems under seismic disaster
scenarios [7]. The model describes not only their physical
topology but also defines the functional logic, traffic allo-
cation rules, and cascading failure propagation mechanisms
under disaster disturbances (such as node failure and link
interruption). Cascading failures are simulated using load
redistribution models with parameters (e.g., node capacity
Cj, initial load Lj, edge weight wij) derived from real-world
network data (e.g., traffic flow surveys, power grid ratings) or
engineering design standards, rather than simplistic assign-
ments based on nodal degree. Thus, it simulates the dynamic
process from local damage to systemic functional paralysis.
As stated in the Scope section, this module has not been
validated against real earthquake data and is included to
define the intended full scope of the framework.
4) Social Behavior Model (V alidation Status: Conceptual
design only — not empirically implemented or tested in this
study)
To assess the social impacts of earthquakes and the effec-
tiveness of emergency responses, the framework introduces
a social behavior model based on Agent-Based Modeling
(ABM). By defining autonomous decision-making agents
(such as residents, rescuers, and vehicles) and establish-
ing their behavioral rules in emergency situations based
on empirical studies from disaster sociology and evacua-
tion modeling (e.g., pre-movement delay times, route choice
under stress, herding behavior), including panic, confor-
mity, and route re-selection, the model emergently produces
complex social phenomena from the bottom-up. The model
parameters can be calibrated using data from past earthquake
evacuations or drill exercises where available. These phe-
nomena include macro evacuation traffic flows and rescue
resource scheduling, achieving a cross-dimensional coupled
simulation of engineering systems and social systems. This
module remains conceptual at the current stage and requires
future empirical calibration and validation.
C. LARGE-SCALE SCENARIO ENSEMBLE SIMULATION
AND DEDUCTION LA YER
This layer represents a key leap for the framework from
‘‘deterministic analysis’’ to ‘‘probabilistic prediction,’’ with
its core task being to conduct large-scale computational
experiments on the digital twin using high-performance com-
puting resources. The process begins with the generation of
scenario ensembles, whose scientific basis is probabilistic
seismic hazard analysis.
The concept of ‘‘large-scale scenario ensembles’’ shares
common ground with risk mapping methods such as
advanced intensity measures [15] and GIS-fuzzy multi-
criteria decision integration [16], but focuses more on
encompassing uncertainties in earthquake occurrence and
propagation through generating massive ground motion sce-
narios and performing systematic probabilistic calculations.
First, based on regional seismic tectonic environments and
historical seismic activity parameters, the Monte Carlo simu-
lation method is used to randomly generate tens of thousands
to hundreds of thousands of statistically reasonable potential
seismic sources (including parameters such as location, mag-
nitude, rupture plane geometry, and slip distribution). The
sampling of fault orientation parameters (strike, dip, rake)
incorporates constraints from regional tectonic settings rather
than assuming simplistic normal distributions. For example,
dip angles may be constrained by fault type (e.g., thrust
vs. strike-slip) and regional stress field data. Subsequently,
for each potential seismic source, the stochastic finite fault
method or ground motion models (GMMs) are used. The
selection and application of GMMs follow region-specific
models (e.g., those developed for Western China or the Next
Generation Attenuation models) and account for epistemic
uncertainties through logic trees or sampling of alternative
models, to synthesize multiple ground motion time histo-
ries considering uncertainties in seismic wave propagation
paths, attenuation relationships, and local site conditions. The
stochastic finite-fault simulations, when employed, include
key seismological parameters such as stress drop, rise time,
and rupture velocity, with values calibrated to regional seis-
micity (e.g., using data from the 2021 Y angbi earthquake
sequence for Y unnan). Finally, a large-scale ground motion
scenario ensemble covering from high-frequency small-to-
moderate earthquakes to low-frequency extremely rare large
earthquakes is formed.
After generating the scenario ensemble, the high-
performance parallel simulation phase commences. The
entire process—from seismic source parameter sampling

D. PROBABILISTIC ASSESSMENT AND DECISION
SUPPORT LA YER
The primary goal of simulation is to assist in decision-
making. This layer focuses on processing raw data from
extensive simulations and converting it into actionable infor-
mation that can directly support decision-making. The key
technical methods used are statistical ensemble analysis and
data visualization.
Assessment units in an urban setting encompass individual
buildings, city blocks, pipeline segments, and so forth. For
each of these units, the system conducts a comprehensive
analysis of its damage states across all simulated scenarios.
Damage levels are categorized into various bands, includ-
ing ‘‘basically intact,’’ ‘‘minor damage,’’ and ‘‘moderate
damage.’’ The definition of these limit states is linked to engi-
neering demand parameters (e.g., inter-story drift ratio, peak
floor acceleration) through fragility functions. These fragility
functions are either adopted from the literature for generic
structural types or developed specifically for the building
inventory in the study area. The system tallies the actual
frequency with which each unit falls into these damage levels
and utilizes these frequencies to compute the probability of
each damage grade.
Spatial visualization of these probability values gener-
ates intuitive probabilistic damage cloud maps. As shown in
Fig. 5, compared with traditional deterministic damage maps
(Fig. 5a) that only show single-scenario results, the proba-
bilistic damage cloud maps (Fig. 5b) quantify the uncertainty
of damage levels, clearly revealing the spatiotemporal distri-
bution of urban risk and helping delineate high-risk zones.
By conducting network robustness analysis and sensitiv-
ity analysis on all simulation results, systemically critical
vulnerable nodes can be identified across various earth-
quake scenarios. These nodes may fail repeatedly or trigger
large-scale cascading effects. The findings provide a basis
for targeted reinforcement and help focus urban resilience
enhancement efforts.
The framework also supports testing the robustness of
pre-defined emergency plans. Decision-makers can select
different strategies—such as emergency evacuation routes or
resource distribution plans—and input them as initial condi-
tions into the established simulation database. By evaluating
the average performance and stability of these strategies
across thousands of potential scenarios, highly robust optimal
strategies that perform effectively across the broadest range
of conditions can be identified.
This process enables a fundamental paradigm shift in
emergency decision-making. Specifically, as illustrated in

III. CORE METHODOLOGIES AND ALGORITHMIC

The framework‘s technical implementation is grounded in
a set of core methodologies that bridge seismology, struc-

3) GROUND MOTION GENERATION VIA
PHYSICS-INFORMED GAN (PI-GAN)
This section details the seismologically-grounded methodol-
ogy for generating the scenario ensemble, which is the core
innovation for uncertainty quantification.
i. Seismic Source Parameter Sampling
The sampling of earthquake sources integrates probabilis-
tic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) principles with regional
tectonic constraints [8], [9].
a. Magnitude: Sampled according to the Gutenberg-
Richter law, lgN(M) = a − bM (4), where the a and b
values are calibrated to the seismicity of the study region
(e.g., derived from the China Seismic Hazard Map).
b. Location: For areal sources, a 2D normal distribution is

1) ANALYSIS OBJECTIVES
The objective was to validate the physical consistency and
accuracy of ground motion time histories generated by the
PI-GAN model, specifically for a regionally-specific seismic
scenario (the Y angbi M s6.4 earthquake). The focus was on

2) METHODOLOGY
i. Model Training and Calibration:
The model was trained on a base dataset from the PEER
NGA-West2 database (700 records) and regionally calibrated
by incorporating 50 measured strong motion records from
the Y angbi earthquake. The 50 Y angbi strong-motion records
were split as follows: 35 records (70%) for training, 8 records
(16%) for validation (hyperparameter tuning and early stop-
ping), and 7 records (14%) for final testing. The test set
was held out until the final evaluation, and all reported per-
formance metrics (PGA error, response spectrum error) are
computed on the test set only. A composite loss function
(α = 1.0, β = 0.8, γ = 0.5) combining adversarial
loss, GMPE constraint loss, spectral constraint loss and
wave equation constraint loss was employed, as defined in
Equation (6). The Adam optimizer was used for training with
a learning rate of 0.0002 and a batch size of 32. Data pre-
processing adopted normalization to map the ground motion
acceleration values to the interval [−1,1]. Training proceeded
for 500 epochs until loss convergence (final loss value 0.08).
ii. Ground Motion Generation: Using key parameters from
the Y angbi earthquake (Ms6.4, soft site conditions with shear
wave velocity <360 m/s), the trained PI-GAN model was
used to generate 1000 ground motion time histories, with
a uniform sampling rate of 50 Hz and a truncated effective
duration of 60 seconds.
iii. Performance Evaluation:
a. Qualitative Comparison: The waveform morphology,
peak distribution, and attenuation trends of 5 randomly gener-
ated time histories were compared against a measured record.
b. Quantitative Analysis: The relative difference in PGA
between generated and measured time histories was calcu-
lated. Furthermore, the error between the average response
spectrum of the 1000 generated time histories and the mea-
sured response spectrum was computed.
