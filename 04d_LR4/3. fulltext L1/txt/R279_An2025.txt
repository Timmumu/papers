ABSTRACT
The application of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) automatic flight is increasingly popular for structural surface
inspection. To address the low level of automation and insufficient adaption of the flight path in response to
environmental obstacles, a method of automatic planning UAV inspection mission based on the Geometric Digital
Twin (GDT) model and Voxelized Obstacle Information (VOI) is proposed. First, a method for shifting the Field of
View (FOV) centroids in parallel is proposed to efficiently generate inspection waypoints. Second, a waypoints
adjustment method based on environmental VOI of 3D point clouds is proposed to address the safety issues.
Third, a method combining Genetic Algorithm (GA) with A* based on VOI is proposed for optimizing UAV flight
path to avoid real-world obstacles. The feasibility of the proposed methods was verified in both an office building
and a steel truss bridge. Compared to existing methods, the efficiency is significantly improved.
1. Introduction
Periodic structural surfaces inspection is crucial for ensuring the
structural safety of large-scale infrastructures such as buildings and
bridges. Traditionally, such inspection has been largely conducted
manually, which is time consuming and prone to errors. More recently,
due to the agility and efficiency of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in
capturing surface images, some studies have begun to apply UAV tech-
nology for structural surface inspection and shown promising results
[ 1 – 3 ]. This process typically involves orienting the UAV camera towards
the target structure where it captures inspection images of the visible
coverage area on the structural surface, which is defined as the Field of
View (FOV). During the process of using UAVs to fly around structures
and acquire inspection image data, three main control indicators are
emphasized [ 4 – 6 ]: (1) safety, to ensure that UAVs do not collide with
obstacles; (2) comprehensiveness, to acquire complete images of the
whole surface to avoid missing inspection; (3) efficiency, to avoid un-
necessary flight burdens. When using traditional manual UAV flight,
such multiple and possibly conflicting goals can be challenging to be
achieved simultaneously, especially for large-scale and complex struc-
tures [ 7 – 9 ]. To tackle this issue, some researchers have comprehensively
reviewed and studied the pre-planning of rational UAV flight missions to
enable automatic execution [ 10 – 12 ]. Compared to the manual control
method, it has been shown that pre-planning can significantly enhance
the safety and efficiency of the inspection [ 6 ]. For an effective pre-
planning of flight mission, the first step is to generate suitable struc-
tural inspection waypoints, which are the specified location coordinates
where a UAV hovers and acquires images.
Properly choosing of waypoints is crucial for the complete while
economical covering of the structural surface. Naturally, a reference
model to the real structure is necessary for determining the distribution
of the waypoints. Such a reference model can be classified into five
levels of detail (LoD) according to the Open Geospatial Consortium
(OGC) standard for City Geography Markup Language (GML) 2.0 [ 13 ]. A
building example is used to illustrate the different levels from LoD0 to
LoD4, as shown in Fig. 1 . The LoD0 model [ 14 – 16 ] represents the
structural projection on a 2D plane, which is typically acquired using
footprint extraction methods [ 17 , 18 ]. Such a simple model can be used
for the generation of waypoints that focus on the acquisition of rough
top view images. To generate waypoints that focus on the acquisition of
both rough facade and top view images, the LoD1 model [ 19 ], which is a
block model derived by extending the LoD0 model vertically, can be
used. Although in 3D, the LoD1 model lacks differentiated facades and
the roof structures [ 20 ], which can create difficulties for structures with

openings in the structure, such as doors and windows, the LoD2 model
can be further upgraded to LoD3 [ 21 ]. By further including details of the
structural interior, the LoD4 model can be created [ 6 ]. From their
characteristics it can be seen that it is challenging to use LoD0 and LoD1
models for effective structural surface inspection, while LoD2, LoD3 and
LoD4 can serve as better reference models. Previously, some studies
have generated waypoints based on models constructed by design
drawings defined under LoD3, such as manually constructed Building
Information Modeling (BIM) [ 22 – 25 ] and 3D geometric model [ 26 , 27 ].
While these models can serve as effective reference models can be
automatically generated through methods such as deep learning [ 28 ]
and progressive model-driven approach [ 29 ], their openings and inter-
nal details are not essential information for external surface inspection
waypoint generation. Moreover, the large amount of training data and
modeling complexity required do hinder the automatic construction of
the efficient reference models. Thus, it can be concluded that the LoD2
model is the most suitable choice for the task of external surface
inspection.
Some studies have utilized 3D point clouds to extract the geometric
shape of the structure [ 4 , 30 – 32 ], voxelized it or adapt observation
planes to the geometry of the scene for waypoints generation and iter-
atively optimizing the number and positions of waypoints based on data
quality [ 33 ]. The 3D point clouds can be produced by depth devices
[ 34 , 35 ] and multi-view reconstruction [ 36 ], and extensive research has
been conducted on the methods of point clouds generation [ 37 – 39 ] and
factors affecting the quality of point clouds [ 11 , 12 , 40 ]. Processing 3D
point clouds and voxelized models can effectively generate inspection
waypoints. However, due to the inclusion of significant non-structural
components and surrounding environments, it becomes challenging to
focus on the structural surface. Additionally, since these methods
involve a large amount of discrete data, they tend to be less accurate and
efficient compared to waypoints generation using LoD2 models.
Several studies have addressed waypoints generation based on
reference models. Tan et al. [ 22 ] proposed a “ three-steps ” region divi-
sion method. First, the polygonal surface of the building is divided into
several rectangular regions, with the corner point of each rectangular
region serving as initial point to create the first rectangle primitive.
These primitives are then translated until them cover all rectangular
regions on the surface, and the offset of each primitive ’ s center point is
extracted to generate inspection waypoints for the surface. Additionally,
by pre-dividing the plane into grids, a polygonal surface can be directly
divided in a “ two-steps ” process. Yang et al. [ 41 ] used UAVs to collect
overhead inspection images of debris fans, manually inputting the
boundary information of the debris fan into the pre-divided grid and
generating waypoints based on the grid ’ s center points, effectively
covering the irregularly shaped debris fan. Yu et al. [ 15 ] also conducted
research on the generation of overhead waypoints, using a four-
neighborhood growing method to determine the areas to be covered
and dividing the grid to generate waypoints. However, further research
is needed on these methods to adapt them to the “ two-steps ” division of
3D polygonal surfaces of GDT model, thereby improving the efficiency
of waypoints generation.

address potential safety issues. Such issues, which are often overlooked

3D point clouds ’ plane and boundary [ 4 , 31 , 32 ], the center points of

building, or too close to them. Furthermore, the flight paths planned in
these manners reduce flexibility and are less than optimal [ 42 ]. There-
fore, it is necessary to adjust the positions of the waypoints according to
the obstacle information around the structure to prevent unsafe or
inaccessible waypoints. Some studies have considered the environ-
mental obstacles in the process of flight path planning. For instance, Sun
et al. [ 43 ] used a semantic segmentation method to divide obstacle areas
in orthophotos, providing references for UAV flight path planning at the
2D level. Some other studies have considered manually constructed
obstacle models at the 3D level for path planning [ 44 , 45 ]. Tan et al. [ 46 ]
researched multi-UAV path planning issues using virtual obstacles that
simulate the environment. Obstacle information can also be expressed
through automatic methods. Phung et al. [ 4 ] expressed the obstacle
information using the maximum bounding boxes of 3D point clouds,
ensuring that waypoints and flight paths avoid obstacles. While these
methods can lower the flight risks to a certain degree by considering the
environment obstacles, manually constructed virtual obstacle models
may differ from actual conditions and be inefficient. Additionally, using
rough models such as bounding boxes to represent environmental ob-
stacles may result in the loss of valuable accessible areas, especially for
inspecting complex structures. Therefore, it is vital to further consider
more accurate obstacle information of the real-life environment when
using the parallel shift strategy.
Once the ultimate waypoints are determined, a flight path optimi-
zation shall be conducted to find the optimal route that sequentially
connects all generated unordered inspection waypoints. A typical unsafe
or inaccessible flight path is shown in Fig. 2 (b), in which parts of it pass
through obstacles or being too close to obstacles. It is necessary to
optimize the flight path to ensure UAV flight safety while also mini-
malize flight burden. These optimization challenges bear resemblance to
the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) and are commonly addressed
using heuristic algorithms, such as Genetic Algorithm (GA) [ 47 ], self-
adaptive genetic simulated annealing algorithm (AGASA) [ 2 ], and Ant
Colony Optimization (ACO) [ 15 ]. Research on these algorithms focuses
on improving their execution speed, convergence, and avoiding local
optima. These methods can enhance the accuracy and efficiency of path
optimization. However, the issues of obstacle avoidance and energy
consumption in UAV during practical engineering applications also
warrant further attention. Optimization of flight path that also tackles
safety issues is a constrained optimization problem, in which the con-
siderations of obstacles as constraints in heuristic algorithms are
incorporated. Koch et al. [ 48 ] used semantic maps overlaid on 3D model
to determine if there are restrictions on UAV flight along these paths,
and provided the results to heuristic algorithms to optimize the flight
path. In environments where obstacles cannot be completely avoided
using heuristic algorithms alone. To address this issue, some studies use
a combination of automatic path finding algorithms and heuristic al-
gorithms to ensure that the optimized flight paths avoid obstacles
[ 4 , 5 , 23 ]. Among these, Phung et al. [ 4 ] considered UAV energy con-
sumption related to flight direction when planning paths using such
methods. Furthermore, Tan et al. [ 23 ] not only planned UAV flight paths
but also devised routes for the UAV to reach designated locations for
battery replacement. However, it is vital to ensure that flight paths
maintain a certain safe distance from real-world environmental obsta-
cles and reduce costs associated with UAV turns due to obstacle avoid-
ance, which requires further research. In other words, it is crucial to
consider more real and accurate obstacle information and minimize
transitional waypoints for turns as much as possible to optimize the
flight path, which has not been sufficiently considered in the past
studies.
To tackle these issues, this paper proposes a novel method for
automatically planning UAV inspection flight missions for capturing
surface images based on Geometric Digital Twin (GDT) model and
Voxelized Obstacle Information (VOI). First, a LoD2-class GDT model,
which can be automatically and rapidly constructed [ 49 – 51 ], is inno-
vatively applied to the inspection waypoints generation. Based on the
accurate geometric shape of the GDT model, a preliminary waypoints
generation method that involves shifting FOV centroids in parallel is
proposed, enhancing the automation and efficiency of the waypoint
generation process. Second, a method utilizing more precise VOI for
inspection waypoints adjustment is proposed. This method employs
voxelization and regional division of the space containing the 3D point
clouds and GDT model to relocate unsafe or inaccessible waypoints to
safer areas. Finally, a heuristic path optimization method combining GA
with A* based on VOI is proposed to define the safe flight path, incor-
porating consideration of real-world environmental obstacles and
reducing the additional energy and time costs associated with automatic
routing through turn penalties. The method proposed in this paper
efficiently and effectively generates waypoints based on the GDT model
and ensures the safety of both the waypoints and the flight path con-
necting them through VOI. This comprehensive ‘point-to-line ’ approach
safeguards the safety of UAV in all aspects, ultimately acquiring effective
‘surface ’ imagery data. The main content of this paper is structured as
follows: the proposed method is presented in Section 2 . In Section 3 , the
method is applied to a building and a bridge to demonstrate the feasi-
bility of the proposed method. The final section presents the

2. Proposed UAV automatic inspection method using GDT model
and VOI
This paper proposes an automatic planning method for UAV in-
spection missions based on the GDT model and VOI, allowing for a rapid
and automatic deployment of UAV inspection flight mission, which can
achieve automatic and safe inspection of structure surfaces. The main
procedure of the method includes: (1) automatically constructing a GDT
model based on 3D point clouds; (2) automatically generating pre-
liminary inspection waypoints based on the GDT model; (3) adjusting
the preliminary inspection waypoints based on divided areas that
reference VOI; (4) optimizing the UAV flight path using the method
combining GA with A* based on VOI; (5) compiling UAV flight files to
realize automatic inspection. The technology roadmap is shown in
Fig. 3 .

2.2. Inspection waypoints generation based on FOV centroids ’ parallel
shift method
After the GDT model is generated, the FOVs on its are pre-divided
using the proposed grid mapping algorithm based on the camera ’ s
FOV. Subsequently, using the proposed parallel shift algorithm, the FOV
centroids on the GDT model surface are parallelly shifted along the
surface normal up to the working distance d of the camera, achieving the
generation of preliminary inspection waypoints, which also serve as the
camera ’ s center points. The images captured by the UAV when it reaches
these waypoints correspond to the subregions previously delineated on
the GDT model surface.

2.2.2. Grid mapping method of dividing FOVs for obtaining the centroids
After the parameters of the waypoints are determined and calcu-
lated, the FOVs can be divided on the surfaces of the model, allowing the
waypoints generation based on the FOVs ’ centroid. Previous studies
have proposed considering the model surface as a set and the FOVs as
subsets within the set, completing the division of the model surface ’ s
FOV by determining the subsets [ 4 ]. Covering regular rectangular planes
with the FOVs is relatively straightforward; however, many buildings
and bridges have surfaces that are irregular polygonal planes. Irregular
polygonal planes can also be used to fit curved surfaces and detail fea-
tures within structure. For such planes in 3D space, Tan et al. [ 22 ]
employed the “ three-step ” method described in Section 1 , while Yu et al.
[ 15 ] used the “ two-step ” method for dividing FOVs on polygonal planes
in 2D space. Inspired by these methods, a grid mapping algorithm (see
Fig. 9 ) is proposed to improve efficiency by achieve a “ two-steps ” divi-
sion of the FOVs on the polygon surface of the GDT model in the 3D
space. In this algorithm, an area larger than the model surface is pre-
divided into FOVs in the local coordinate system. The model surface is
then transformed from the global coordinate system to the local coor-
dinate system. Based on the polygonal surface of the model, FOVs that
fully cover the surface are automatically selected. This approach allows
for the segmentation of FOVs on irregular polygonal planes and facili-
tates automatic and efficient operations.

2.3. Inspection waypoints adjustment method based on VOI
As mentioned in Section 1 , because the bulk-generated waypoints do
not account for the impact of environmental obstacles, they may be in
unsafe or inaccessible positions, potentially lead to collisions. As sum-
marized in Section 1 , previous studies that generated waypoints based
on design models often overlooked the impact of real environmental
obstacles on UAV flight safety [ 5 , 22 – 24 ]; other studies represented
environmental obstacles with rough bounding boxes [ 4 ], which can still
miss some valuable accessible areas. On the other hand, 3D point clouds
used in this study, as reconstructions of real scenes, can accurately de-
pict the positions of environmental obstacles. Voxels, as subdivisions of
3D space [ 56 ], can clearly express the geometric information of envi-
ronmental obstacles. Therefore, after voxelizing the 3D point clouds
used to establish the GDT model as mentioned in Subsection 2.1 , the VOI
is obtained. A method for adjusting inspection waypoints utilizing more
precise VOI to refine waypoints adjustment is then proposed.
As shown in Fig. 11 , the process is as follows: firstly, based on the
maximum and minimum values of points in the 3D point clouds along
each coordinate axis, a cubic bounding box for the 3D point clouds is
constructed, and all surfaces of the bounding box except the bottom are
expanded outward by d
w
to create the working space of UAV, which
contains the waypoints. As shown in Fig. 12 , the larger the expansion
distance d
w
, the larger the working space of the UAV. With the same
voxel size, more voxels are needed to fill the working space, and the
computational cost for obtaining the VOI increases. Therefore, this
paper considers an expansion distance d
w
slightly greater than the
working distance d , which as mentioned in Subsection 2.2.1 , specifically
d
w
= d + 2 m, to balance computational efficiency with sufficient
adjustment space for the waypoints. Subsequently, the voxel size S is set,
and the working space is voxelized. The choice of S affects both
computational efficiency and VOI precision. For the inspection of
structures with smaller volumes, it is advisable to choose a smaller S , but
it should be kept within the range of 0.5 m to 1 m to ensure the precision
of the VOI. Based on the 3D point clouds and the GDT model, all voxels
within the working space are classified into three categories (details of
the voxel classification are as shown in Fig. 13 ): the first category is the
inaccessible voxels, which are considered unreachable for UAV if the
voxel contains points from the 3D point clouds or if the center point of
the voxel is inside the GDT model; the second category is the unsafe
voxels, which is considered having potential dangerous posed for UAV

Secondly, based on the VOI, risky waypoints located in inaccessible
voxels or unsafe voxels can be identified. Subsequently, these risky
waypoints are moved to the nearest safe voxel since each waypoint
corresponds to a unique FOV centroid, even if multiple risky waypoints
are adjusted to the same safe voxel, they still point towards their
respective FOV centroids. Therefore, this adjustment does not create
issues with image overlap. If multiple nearest safe voxels exist, the risky
waypoints are prioritized to move along the outward normal direction.
This adjustment strategy ensures that all waypoints are safe.
Finally, since the positions of some waypoints have been adjusted,
altering the status of the full cover waypoints generated in Section 2 , it is
crucial to ensure that the waypoints, after being moved, can still fully
cover the structural surfaces. Therefore, it is necessary to re-evaluate the
image coverage to ensure that no inspection areas are missed. The
process of calculating each camera ’ s FOV coverage structural surface
area is shown in Fig. 14 . First, the GDT model surfaces are subdivided
into dense discrete points using the grid mapping method proposed in
Subsection 2.2.2 with parameters contain the horizontal and vertical

accurately represent the coverage area of the FOV ensuring less than 1 %
coverage error but also maintains computational efficiency. Subse-
quently, the camera ’ s FOV for adjusted waypoints is re-calculated to
check whether any discrete points are not covered by the camera ’ s FOV
based on the adjusted waypoints, ensuring that the surface of the GDT
model has been fully covered by the UAV camera. It should be noted that
UAV camera typically have a small focal length, providing a wide field of
view and deep depth of field, which generally prevents blurring of the
structural surface images. The proposed waypoint adjustment method
can compensate the adverse effects of position changes on resolution
while ensuring safety. Additionally, adjusting the focal length can
further mitigate this impact. Whether discrete points are covered is
determined by the angle between the normal vector of the discrete
points and the view line, as well as whether there are any obstructions
along the view line reaching the point. Additionally, the discrete points
are consistent with the structure ’ s surface. Therefore, it is appropriate to
evaluate whether the adjusted waypoints fully cover the surface ’ s
discrete points using the accurate normal vectors provided by the GDT
model. If there are uncovered discrete points, cluster these points to
identify the uncovered areas. For each uncovered area, extract the
center of the area as an additional FOV centroid. Correspondingly, an
additional waypoint needs to be added; this waypoint should in the safe
area along the outward normal direction of the model surface. This
process continues until the UAV camera ’ s FOV covers all discrete points.
By adjusting the preliminary generated waypoints based on the
proposed VOI method, comprehensive coverage inspection of the
structure can be achieved, while ensuring the safety of the UAV during
inspection.

2.4. Flight path optimization method combining GA with A* based on VOI
The flight path is defined as a route that sequentially connects all
generated inspection waypoints. Due to the unordered nature of the
waypoints generated during the preceding processes, employing the
initial sequence to establish the flight path could result in collision risks
and significantly elevated energy consumption during the inspection
process. Consequently, optimizing the sequence of waypoint
connectivity to minimize the total travel distance and adding appro-
priate transitional waypoints to alter the unsafe or inaccessible flight
path between two waypoints becomes imperative. As mentioned in the
Section 1 , previous studies have not sufficiently considered the issue that
flight paths should maintain a safe distance from environmental obsta-
cles, and reduce the costs associated with UAV turns due to obstacle
avoidance. Therefore, a heuristic path optimization method combining
GA with A* based on VOI is proposed, which constrained by the VOI,
flight direction and turns costs.
As shown in Fig. 15 , the process is as follows: initially, based on the
GDT model, it is easy to determine whether a line connecting two
waypoints passes through the target structure. Therefore, using a GA
constrained by this condition can optimize the shortest flight path that
passes through all waypoints without crossing the target structure. Many
current path planning studies use GA [ 5 , 22 , 23 ], where GA treats flight
paths formed by different connection orders of waypoints as individuals
in nature. By crossing and mutating operations, GA changes the
connection order of the parent individual to generate new offspring with
different connection orders. The offspring with higher fitness are
selected to repeatedly generate new individuals. In this problem, fitness
refers to the distance of the current flight path, where a shorter flight
path distance corresponds to higher fitness of the individual. The
shortest flight path optimization problem constrained by flight direction
costs can be represented by the objective function Eq. (6) . When opti-
mizing this problem using GA, the objective function is negatively
correlated with fitness, where GA generates the individual with the

3. Field experimental validation
To verify the effectiveness of the proposed automatic planning
method for UAV inspection missions, it was applied to some represen-
tative real-life structures, i.e., an 8-storeyed office building [Fig. 17(a)]
and a 56 m-span steel truss bridge [Fig. 17(b)]. The UAV used to collect
multi-view images of the structures and execute the inspection missions
was a DJI Mavic3 Enterprise model, which has a maximum battery life of
46 min. It is equipped with an RTK module that enhances the positioning
capability of the UAV, and features a wide-angle camera sensor size of 4/
3 with 20 million effective pixels. The computer used for modeling and
running the algorithms was equipped with an Intel Core i7–10,700 CPU,
an Nvidia RTX 3080 12G GPU, and 32GB of RAM.
3.1. 3D point clouds reconstruction
The selected structures are reconstructed into 3D point clouds using
the multi-view 3D reconstruction method, with iTwin Capture utilized
for this 3D reconstruction. To simplify the GDT model construction and
omit some unnecessary details, a subset of multi-view images is used to
reconstruct a rough 3D point cloud (as shown in Fig. 18). In contrast, the
complete set of multi-view images is employed to generate a refined 3D
point cloud (as shown in Fig. 20) for accurate VOI generation. To ensure
the correct display of the 3D point clouds, the GCS is chosen for
exporting the reconstructed 3D point clouds. Subsequently, the gener-
ated inspection waypoints need to be imported into the flight control
system using the WGS-84 coordinate system. The conversion between
these two coordinate systems is facilitated by a MATLAB program
developed specially for this purpose. The reconstruction results for the
building and bridge 3D point clouds are shown in Fig. 6.
3.2. Inspection flight paths planning
The output 3D point clouds from 3D reconstruction are input into the
Polyfit framework [49] for GDT model construction. As shown in
Fig. 18, the models closely align with the 3D point clouds, accurately
representing the structural geometric attributes. Some representative
