Abstract
Purpose – This paper aims to develop a proactive construction safety management framework capable of 
predicting near-miss incidents by leveraging Digital Twin (DT) technology. The Digital Twin (DT) framework 
focuses on integrating environmental and human-related risk factors – particularly the trajectories and behaviors 
of workers – before accidents occur.
Design/methodology/approach – The paper proposes a computer vision-based digital twin framework that 
integrates real-time data on workers’ movements, postures, and interactions to construct a dynamic 3D model of 
the construction site. Deep learning algorithms are employed to predict human trajectories and behaviors, while 
collision detection techniques are used to identify potential near-miss scenarios. Field validation was conducted 
in three high-risk zones: forklift paths, floor openings, and material lifting areas.
Findings – The proposed framework achieved high accuracy in predicting near-miss events and provided timely 
early warnings, outperforming traditional 2D monitoring methods. The system’s capability to visualize worker 
interactions and spatial hazards in 3D significantly enhanced situational awareness and safety decision-making on site.
Practical implications – By transitioning from 2D to 3D monitoring and from post-event detection to real-time 
prediction, the proposed system enables early identification of near-miss risks during ongoing construction 
activities. Its integration of 3D visualization and digital twin technology provides intuitive, actionable insights, 
empowering safety managers to intervene proactively and enhance on-site safety performance.
Originality/value – This paper introduces an innovative approach that transitions safety management from passive 
detection to proactive prediction. By integrating digital twin technology with deep learning, it enables 3D visualization 
and prediction of near-miss incidents and supports real-time, data-driven safety interventions. The proposed system 
contributes to the advancement of intelligent, predictive safety management solutions in the construction industry, 
with a particular focus on forecasting near-miss events before they escalate into actual accidents.

Traditionally, construction safety management has relied on reactive measures, focusing 
on incident response rather than prevention (Zhang et al., 2025a). However, this approach 
has proven insufficient in addressing the root causes of accidents (Abdul Nabi and El-
adaway, 2022). In recent years, the industry has shifted toward proactive strategies, 
emphasizing the anticipation and mitigation of risks before incidents occur (Yang et al., 
2012; Zeng et al., 2023). Central to this proactive approach is the ability to predict accidents 
with high precision (Kunreuther et al., 2004), which not only helps in understanding 
accident trends but also in evaluating the effectiveness of safety management measures. 
Near-misses, defined as events that could have resulted in accidents but did not 
(Abdelhamid and Everett, 2000), serve as early warning signs of potential hazards. 
These incidents have been widely utilized in various industries, such as aviation, 
manufacturing, and healthcare, to enhance safety management. Near-miss incidents occur 
far more frequently than actual accidents, often by one or two orders of magnitude (Van Der 
Schaaf, 1995; Zhou and Irizarry, 2016). This abundance of data makes near-misses a 
valuable resource for statistical monitoring and trend analysis (Ancione and Milazzo, 
2025 ), offering opportunities to identify patterns and predict potential hazards. 
By leveraging near-miss data, safety management can transition from a reactive to a 
preventive framework, significantly reducing the likelihood of catastrophic outcomes.
However, current near-miss management practices are limited in their scope. Most 
approaches to near-miss management primarily focus on monitoring (Amaechi et al., 2024; 
Salem and Dragomir, 2023; Ullah et al., 2024a, b; Yan et al., 2025), enabling alerts when near-
misses occur, but they lack the capability to predict near-misses before they happen. Existing 
predictive approaches, including scenario analysis, regression models, grey systems, time-
series analysis, Markov chains, Bayesian networks, and neural networks, offer varying degrees 
of effectiveness for near-miss forecasting in construction (Das Chagas Moura et al., 2016; Doss 
et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016; Pljaki�c et al., 2019; Radaideh et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2016; Zheng 
and Liu, 2009). While these methods can provide predictions, they are largely based on 
historical data, using past patterns to infer future probabilities rather than dynamically 
simulating and predicting imminent 3D interactions between workers, machinery, and the 
environment as they evolve in real time. Consequently, they fail to provide the ability to actively 
defend against potential near-misses during the construction process. To address this gap and 
enable proactive safety measures, it is critical to predict the three-dimensional interactions 
between workers and the construction environment in real time.
Digital twin technology has emerged as a powerful tool for enabling 3D near-miss prediction 
and proactive safety management. A digital twin is a virtual replica of a physical construction 
environment, capable of real-time monitoring, simulation, and predictive analysis (Liu et al., 
2021, 2022; Ye et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2022). By integrating data from sensors, computer vision, 
and other sources, digital twins provide spatially accurate, dynamic representations of 
construction sites. This capability allows for the visualization of potential near-miss incidents 
in three dimensions, offering a comprehensive understanding of spatial relationships and hazards.
In this context, this study proposes a proactive safety management system that leverages 
digital twin technology and an AI prediction engine to anticipate near-miss incidents on

2. Related works
2.1 Near-miss management: from traditional to technological approaches and the
remaining challenges
The critical role of near-miss data in proactive construction safety management has been 
widely recognized, particularly given the persistently high-risk nature of the construction 
industry. Grounded in Heinrich’s accident pyramid theory (Heinrich, 1941), which was 
subsequently expanded by Bird, near-miss incidents are considered fundamental to 
occupational safety, serving as crucial early indicators of potential hazards. Bird’s extensive 
analysis of over 1.7 million accident reports revealed a striking ratio: for every fatality, there 
are approximately 600 near-miss incidents, predominantly triggered by unsafe acts (Bird and 
Germain, 1966). These near-miss events, often regarded as “unrealized accidents,” provide 
valuable opportunities for intervention. Proactive management of these incidents can 
substantially mitigate the likelihood of severe accidents, thereby preventing more serious 
outcomes (Haas et al., 2020).
Despite their significance, traditional approaches to near-miss data collection and 
analysis are often labor-intensi ve and heavily reliant on manual processes, which 
inherently limit their efficiency and scalability . This limitation has spurred the 
development of IT-based solutions designed to enhance near-miss detection and 
management. Recent advancements include the introduction of automated systems and 
sensor-based tools for real-time data collection and incident tracking. For example, Aria 
et al. (2014) successfully tested wearable sensor-ba sed technologies for detecting near-
miss falls, while Yang et al. (2014, 2015, 2016) demonstrated the potential of automated 
systems utilizing inertial measurement technology to identify hazardous movements. 
Zhang et al. (2019) further developed innovative models integrating mobile technologies 
and artificial neural networks to identify fall-related near misses. Teizer and Cheng (2015) 
proposed proximity detection models that employ automated location tracking to assess 
worker proximity to equipment and site layout hazards. More recently, Wu et al. (2010) 
introduced real-time RFID-based tracking systems, and Chung et al. (2023) explored 
IoT-based monitoring solutions for real-time near-miss detection. Nasernejad et al. (2021)

Significant progress has also been made in the prediction of near-miss incidents. For

Additionally, Madsen et al. (2024) utilized near-miss data, artificial intelligence, and 
machine learning to predict maritime incidents, demonstrating the utility of recent near-miss 
counts in predicting future serious casualties at the waterway level. However, these predictive 
efforts primarily focus on analyzing and forecasting near-miss data without addressing the 
dynamic processes leading to near-miss events, failing to address the dynamic, three-
dimensional interactions that drive most construction site hazards. This limitation hinders the 
ability to anticipate near-misses during their occurrence, thereby preventing truly proactive 
safety management.
In summary, while significant advancements have been made in near-miss detection, data 
collection, and prediction, critical challenges remain in the construction sector. Traditional 
methods are limited by their reliance on manual processes, and although IT-driven solutions 
have improved efficiency, most current approaches are still two-dimensional (operating in 
planar (x, y) coordinates, lacking vertical (z-axis) analysis, thus missing hazards like overhead 
crane interactions or falls from height), failing to fully capture the complex, three-dimensional 
dynamics of construction sites. The chaotic and dynamic nature of construction environments 
further complicates near-miss prediction, as constant site changes and intricate worker-
equipment interactions are difficult to model accurately. Moreover, existing predictive models, 
while innovative, often focus on post-hoc analysis rather than real-time process prediction. 
To address these limitations, there is a pressing need for the development of three-dimensional 
(integrating x, y, z axes to model 3D positions, trajectories, and postures, capturing multi-
directional interactions like workers near floor openings or under suspended loads), granular 
near-miss prediction methods that can monitor and forecast incidents in real-time. Such 
advancements would enable truly proactive safety management, ultimately reducing the risk 
of severe accidents and enhancing overall safety in the construction industry.
2.2 The capacity of digital twin in proactive safety management
Digital twin (DT) technology, which comprises a physical entity, a digital counterpart, and an 
integrated data connection, has emerged as a transformative tool for enhancing the safety and 
efficiency of complex physical systems (Castilla et al., 2024; Feng et al., 2025). Initially 
conceptualized during NASA’s Apollo program and later formalized by Michael Grieves, DT 
technology replicates the lifecycle of a physical system in a digital environment, enabling real-
time simulation and predictive analytics (Grieves and Vickers, 2017). By continuously 
synchronizing with sensor and environmental data, digital twins can dynamically model the 
current state of physical assets, thereby identifying potential failures before they occur.
The application of DT technology has expanded significantly beyond equipment 
monitoring, encompassing sectors such as manufacturing, logistics, energy, and 
construction (Martinez et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024). For instance, Rauscher et al. (2021) 
demonstrated its utility in nuclear reactor maintenance by developing a simulation tool that 
reduces downtime and enhances decision-making. In logistics and robotics, Wu et al. (2023) 
and Hu (2022) employed DT frameworks, including the Internet of Everything (IoE) and 
mutual information algorithms, to optimize operations and ensure the continuity of complex 
systems. In the construction sector, DT has been primarily utilized to create accurate 3D digital

replicas of infrastructure, facilitating progress tracking, asset management, and digital 
fabrication. For example, Long et al. (2024) emphasized how high-fidelity DT models can

Recently, DT technology has gained traction for predictive applications across various

a natural gas boiler, while Jiang et al. (2021) proposed a DT-driven framework for fatigue life

prediction. Katsidoniotaki et al. (2022) developed a DT for predicting extreme loads on a wave 
energy conversion system, showcasing its innovative potential in renewable energy. 
Additionally, Wang et al. (2023) established a DT model for gas pipeline leakage detection 
based on pressure signals, and Liu et al. (2023) presented an intelligent DT prediction and

edge computing. In the construction domain, Jiao et al. (2024) proposed a DT framework 
utilizing cloud-edge cooperation to predict worker trajectories, balancing computational 
efficiency with data privacy concerns.
Despite these advancements, the application of DT technology in construction sites faces 
significant challenges (Lu and Antwi-Afari, 2024; Luo et al., 2024). The chaotic and dynamic 
nature of construction environments, characterized by constantly changing workers, materials, 
and machinery, complicates the creation of accurate virtual representations. Furthermore, the 
predictive capabilities of DT systems in proactive safety management remain in their infancy, 
with most systems still limited to monitoring functions (Chung et al., 2023; Teizer and Cheng, 
2015; Wu et al., 2010; Yan et al., 2025). These systems primarily focus on real-time data 
collection and incident tracking rather than forecasting potential risks before they occur, 
failing to enable proactive safety interventions in dynamic construction environments. To fully 
realize the potential of DT in proactive safety intervention, challenges related to dynamic 
modeling, sensor data integration, and the incorporation of machine learning algorithms must 
be addressed. Overcoming these barriers will enable construction teams not only to monitor 
real-time conditions but also to predict potential risks and hazardous events, thereby 
transforming safety management from a reactive to a truly proactive approach.
3. Methodology
3.1 Framework of digital twin system
To enable real-time prediction of near-miss incidents during construction processes, it is 
essential to model and evaluate the interactions between construction workers and their 
surrounding environment (e.g. machinery, materials, and structures) for safety risk 
identification. This study leverages digital twin technology to dynamically reconstruct 
construction sites in a virtual environment, facilitating real-time simulation of interactions 
among on-site elements. For construction workers specifically, their motion trajectories and 
behavioral patterns are predicted in the digital twin domain by analyzing human keypoints 
extracted from pose estimation algorithms. This approach enables proactive detection of near-
miss events at their early stages. Moreover, the digital twin platform visualizes predicted near-
miss scenarios, including spatial relationships and temporal sequences, providing construction 
managers with actionable insights. Such visualization enhances data-driven decision-making, 
supports timely interventions, and strengthens proactive safety management in construction.

To accurately reconstruct construction site elements within the digital twin environment, 
different reconstruction strategies are applied based on the nature of the elements. Elements 
that remain relatively unchanged over short periods, such as the main construction structure 
and stored materials, are categorized as static elements and updated periodically. In contrast, 
elements that undergo continuous changes, such as moving workers, construction machinery, 
and actively used materials, are classified as dynamic elements and updated in real time.
The workflow of the digital twin system (as shown in Figure 2) is as follows: Initially, static 
data from the construction site is collected using drones, while real-time dynamic information – 
including dynamic construction materials, machinery, and personnel – is captured through 
binocular cameras deployed on-site. These data are then transmitted to the data processing 
module for analysis. Through algorithms for object recognition, object tracking, binocular stereo 
matching, and human pose estimation, the system extracts the positional information of both 
static and dynamic elements, as well as the keypoint information of construction personnel.
Subsequently, this information is fed into the digital twin platform to create a digital twin 
model of the construction site within a virtual environment. This model not only reflects the 
real-time status of each construction worker but also predicts their movement trajectories and 
postures based on historical data using transformer-based algorithms. The system can forecast 
near-miss incidents and issue early warnings within the digital twin domain. Moreover, the 
visualization capabilities of the digital twin platform provide a more intuitive basis and 
assurance for safety management on the construction site.
The digital twin (DT) system developed in this study is applicable to construction scenarios 
that feature high spatial complexity, dynamic environmental changes, and stringent safety 
requirements. Its applicability is guided by the following three principles:
(1) Presence of complex three-dimensional environments

3.2 Physical layer description
The physical layer forms the foundational data acquisition component of the digital twin 
system, enabling precise replication of the construction environment. To address the dynamic 
and multifaceted nature of construction sites, this system integrates unmanned aerial vehicles 
(UAVs) and binocular cameras for robust and comprehensive data collection. UAVs capture 
static features, such as structural elements and material storage zones, while binocular cameras 
provide continuous monitoring of dynamic entities, including workers, machinery, and 
moving materials.
The successful implementation of the physical layer relies on environmental and technical 
conditions. The system performs optimally under stable weather, adequate lighting, and 
carefully planned sensor deployment. For example, UAV operations require clear skies and 
minimal wind to ensure stable flight and high-resolution imagery. Similarly, binocular 
cameras achieve the best results when placed at elevated positions with wide, unobstructed 
views, and when deployed during daylight hours with consistent lighting. The subsequent 
parts elaborate on the deployment strategies and operational considerations for these sensing 
technologies.

UAVs play a critical role in periodically mapping static elements of the construction site, 
ensuring an accurate and current depiction of project progress. Several key factors guide their 
deployment and data acquisition:
(1) Data Collection Frequency: UAV scanning intervals should synchronize with the 
construction timeline. A weekly scan is typically sufficient for tracking structural 
updates, though additional flights may be warranted during pivotal phases, such as 
concrete pouring or major installations.
(2) Flight Altitude and Path Optimization: Flight altitudes, generally set between 50 and 
100 meters, must strike a balance between coverage breadth and image resolution. 
Automated path planning is essential to achieve comprehensive site coverage and 
minimize data gaps from inadequate overlap.
(3) Lighting and Weather Constraints: To ensure consistent image quality and avoid 
issues from shadows or fluctuating light, UAV operations should occur during stable 
lighting periods (e.g. 9–11 AM or 2–4 PM). Flights should also be halted during 
adverse weather – such as high winds, rain, or snow – to maintain stability and 
safeguard data integrity.
Binocular cameras facilitate real-time observation of dynamic construction activities by 
tracking the movements of personnel, equipment, and materials. Effective deployment hinges 
on considerations of visibility, occlusion risks, and light conditions:
(1) Effective camera placement is critical for optimal site coverage. Mounting binocular 
cameras on tower cranes or elevated structures provides a wide, unobstructed vantage 
point across the construction site. Strategic positioning in high-activity zones – such as 
areas for formwork installation, steel reinforcement, or concrete pouring – enables 
targeted observation of critical operations. Cameras should also be installed near 
heavy machinery, including excavators, cranes, and concrete mixers, to monitor 
worker-equipment interactions, with placement adjusted to avoid disrupting 
workflow.
(2) Deployment Environment: To optimize performance, binocular cameras should be 
deployed in environments with favorable lighting conditions. Operations are effective 
during periods of consistent, ample illumination – such as mid-morning (e.g. 9–11 
AM) or early afternoon (e.g. 2–4 PM) – to ensure high-quality image capture without 
reliance on supplemental enhancements. Deployment should be planned to avoid 
areas or times with poor natural light, minimizing the risk of degraded visibility or data 
inconsistency.
By combining regularly updated drone-collected static data with real-time dynamic data from 
binocular cameras, the physical module creates a comprehensive and up-to-date 
representation of the construction site. This robust data foundation is transmitted to the data 
processing module, where it undergoes further analysis and integration into the digital twin
platform.

3.3 Digital twin implementation
This section specifically details the implementation plan of the digital twin system for the 
construction site. It covers the mapping plan from the physical world to the digital twin virtual 
world to conduct a complete digital reconstruction of the construction site. Additionally, it 
presents the prediction scheme for the trajectories and postures of reconstructed construction 
personnel, the determination method for near-misses on the construction site, and the 
realization of near-misses event prediction. The entire digital twin system was developed using 
Unreal Engine 5 (UE5), a powerful real-time 3D engine known for its high-fidelity 
visualization capabilities and extensibility for simulation-based applications. The official 
documentation of UE5 is available at: https://docs.unrealengine.com/5.0/en-US/.
3.3.1 Physical state information mapping. To achieve more accurate reconstruction of 
multi-source information on the construction site, we adopt distinct reconstruction strategies 
for static and dynamic elements.
For static data collected by drones, after information extraction in the data processing 
module, as shown in Figure 3, the data is used to construct a static Building Information Model 
(BIM). This BIM model is then imported into the digital twin system developed in Unreal 
Engine (UE) for reconstruction and is updated regularly in accordance with the construction
progress.
For dynamic data collected by binocular cameras deployed on the construction site, the data
processing module extracts real-time updated IDs of dynamic construction personnel, 
machinery, and materials, along with their coordinates (x, y, z) in the world coordinate system 
with the camera as the origin. Initially, the YOLOv5 algorithm (Jiang et al., 2022) processes 
the left image to identify and classify element types, distinguishing construction workers, 
materials, and machinery. Concurrently, the left and right images undergo Semi-Global Block 
Matching (SGBM) (Hirschmuller, 2008) to derive depth information, facilitating accurate 
three-dimensional spatial reconstruction. Subsequently, the DeepSORT algorithm (Wojke 
et al., 2017) is utilized to assign unique IDs to each detected object, ensuring consistent 
tracking across the dynamic construction environment. Additionally, for the identified

elements within the digital twin platform, accurately replicating both static and dynamic 
components. However, capturing the nuanced interactions between construction personnel 
and their surroundings with greater fidelity demands more than static models can offer. 
Positional tracking alone falls short; reconstructing personnel postures to reflect real-world 
movements is critical for a faithful digital representation. This entails driving digital avatars 
with motion data synchronized to onsite worker actions, a process that builds on existing 
tracking capabilities but introduces new complexities.

the Euler rotation angles of the joints and bones of the human body based on the positions of

the Euler rotation angles of the bones. The IK solution is crucial for achieving more accurate 
posture reconstruction of construction personnel, thereby enhancing the realism and 
effectiveness of the digital twin system for the construction site.
Inverse Kinematics (IK) solution is a technique widely used in computer graphics and 
robotics to determine the joint angles of a kinematic chain given the desired end-effector 
position. The basic idea behind IK is to minimize an error function that measures the difference 
between the desired end-effector position and the actual position achieved by the kinematic 
chain. This is typically done using iterative optimization algorithms.
In our work, we implement an Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver based on PyTorch for solving 
skeletal systems. The Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-G oldfarb-Shanno (LBFGS) 
optimization algorithm (Zhang et al., 2025b) is employed to iteratively minimize the error 
between the target keypoints of the skeletal model and the detected keypoints. The IK solver 
adjusts the joint angles, represented as Euler angles, within the skeletal hierarchy, ensuring 
accurate alignment between the bones and the target keypoints, thereby achieving precise 
posture reconstruction.

3.3.2 Prediction of the trajectory and posture. In this section, we apply the algorithm
proposed by Mahdavian et al. (2023). This algorithm enables us to predict both the trajectory 
and posture of human subjects based on keypoints extracted using MediaPipe. As human 
motion occurs, the positions of keypoints change in a predictable, time-dependent manner – 
for example, the rhythmic swinging of arms and legs while walking or the stride pattern during 
running. These temporal dependencies capture the underlying motion patterns across 
successive time frames, enabling the model to understand how keypoints are likely to shift in 
the near future based on past trends. By leveraging the temporal dependencies within the 
observed keypoints, the method allows for efficient and accurate forecasts of human motion, 
capturing both position and limb configuration in real-time.

3.4 Digital twin visualization of near-misses
The digital twin system is fully deployed on the Unreal Engine (UE) platform, providing a 
dynamic, real-time visualization of the construction site’s virtual twin alongside the live 
conditions at the physical site. Through this digital twin, both predictive and historical near-
miss data are visualized, offering site managers and safety officers an immediate and 
comprehensive view of potential and actual incidents.
The system continuously monitors the positions and postures of workers and other dynamic 
elements within the virtual environment, forecasting potential near-misses based on real-time 
data. When a near-miss event is predicted, a visual alert is generated within the digital twin, 
immediately notifying on-site and remote staff of potential hazards. This real-time alerting 
system enables proactive intervention, reducing the likelihood of incidents by providing 
sufficient time for preventive actions.
Beyond immediate alerts, the digital twin logs and aggregates near-miss events, 
capturing details such as location, timestamp, and frequency. These records are visualized 
through interactive dashboards on the UE platform, enabling detailed safety analyses and 
long-term risk assessments. By mapping the frequency and spatial distribution of near-
misses, the system reveals recurring hazards and operational patterns that may warrant 
intervention. This data-driven insight empowers managers to prioritize resources, refine 
safety protocols, and implement targeted measures, fostering a safer and more efficient 
construction environment.
4. Pilot study
Our proposed digital twin-based framework is designed to enhance proactive safety 
management on construction sites by focusing on predicting near-miss incidents through 
the forecasting of worker trajectories and postures. This innovative system integrates both 
static and dynamic data sources to construct an accurate digital representation of the 
construction environment. This comprehensive digital model allows for effective monitoring 
and prediction of potential hazards, thereby significantly improving the safety measures 
implemented on-site.
4.1 Implementation details
To enable real-time tracking of dynamic elements on the construction site, binocular cameras 
are strategically positioned at key vantage points across the area. These cameras continuously 
capture the spatial movements of workers, machinery, and other dynamic objects, generating a 
live stream of positional data critical for safety monitoring. The coordinates of each identified 
dynamic element are referenced to the origin of the respective binocular camera, establishing a 
localized frame of reference that anchors the virtual representation within the digital twin
system.
Accurate reconstruction of these elements in the digital twin environment depends on
precise knowledge of the cameras’ spatial configuration. The current position and orientation
of each binocular camera are meticulously documented. High-precision Real-Time Kinematic
(RTK) GPS receivers and laser-based total stations determine the exact 3D position (x, y, z) of
each camera relative to the site’s coordinate system. Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs)
integrated with the cameras capture their angular orientation (pitch, yaw, roll) with sub-degree

4.2 Algorithm training
The training of our human motion prediction model utilized the widely recognized 
Human3.6M dataset (Ionescu et al., 2014). This dataset comprises the 3D joint positions of 
seven actors engaged in 15 distinct activities, such as walking, sitting, and smoking. While it 
has traditionally served as a benchmark for human pose estimation, we adapt it for human 
trajectory prediction as well. For our trajectory predictions, we specifically extracted the hip 
trajectories of each actor, while for pose prediction, we focused on the relative positions of all 
other joints with respect to the stationary hip. In conventional practices, the frame rate of this 
dataset is often reduced from 50 Hz to 25 Hz; however, we opted for a frame rate of 10 Hz, 
which is more suitable for construction applications.
The model was developed using PyTorch as the primary deep learning framework, trained 
over 250 epochs with the AdamWoptimizer, an initial learning rate of 10 � 4 , and a batch size of 
16. Awarm-up phase was implemented during the initial 10,000 steps, gradually increasing the 
learning rate from zero to 10 � 4 , which contributed to enhanced training stability.
