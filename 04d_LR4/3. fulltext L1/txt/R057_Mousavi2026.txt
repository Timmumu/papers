ABSTRACT
Bridge infrastructures are inevitably exposed to a wide range of deterioration mechanisms and external damages 
over their service life, which can gradually impair structural integrity, compromise functionality, and increase 
safety risks. These challenges, coupled with the growing complexity of infrastructure systems and limited 
financial resources, highlight the need for implementing intelligent and proactive asset management solutions. 
To address these needs, this paper presents a Reinforcement Learning-based Decision Support System (RL-DSS) 
framework for bridge maintenance optimization, leveraging a Multi-Objective Deep Centralized Multi-Agent 
Actor-Critic (MO-DCMAC) algorithm that is designed for application within bridge Digital Twin environments. 
The proposed RL-DSS implements a three-objective MO-DCMAC framework that models maintenance cost, 
structural failure risk, and serviceability as separate objectives within a vector-valued reward formulation, while 
employing a fixed linear utility function for policy optimization. The proposed framework was evaluated through 
three real-world bridge case studies, each representing different service conditions, to demonstrate its capability 
in providing long-term maintenance planning. Its performance was benchmarked against multiple established 
rule-based policies commonly used in practice. The results revealed that the MO-DCMAC agent achieved the 
highest overall utility score, demonstrating superiority by generating more refined and practical maintenance 
policies. By actively penalizing high-frequency site visits and strategically balancing intervention costs with risk 
mitigation, the RL agent prioritizes fewer but more impactful repair actions. The proposed RL-DSS reflects an 
adaptive, informed, and cost-effective decision-making process that captures the complexity of real-world 
maintenance scenarios, surpassing the limitations of traditional rule-based approaches.
1. Introduction
Sustainable transportation systems are widely recognized as critical 
foundations for the development of resilient and sustainable societies, 
and are closely aligned with the principles of smart cities ( Moslem et al., 
2024 ; Saha et al., 2024 ). Achieving these sustainability objectives relies 
heavily on the implementation of effective infrastructure asset man -
agement systems, which enable decision-makers to apply, data-driven, 
timely and predictive maintenance strategies supported by advanced 
intelligent control and autonomous management approaches ( G ¨orçün 
et al., 2025 ; Sood, 2024 ; Tang et al., 2025 ). In this context, bridges 
serve as fundamental components of transportation infrastructure and 
the broader built environment. However, their structural condition de -
teriorates over time due to various factors, including environmental 
exposure, traffic-induced loading, material degradation and aging. 
Without appropriate monitoring and management, these factors can 
lead to reduced structural capacity and serviceability, which may 
necessitate major rehabilitation, repair, or even demolition, resulting in 
increased costs, prolonged traffic disruptions, and elevated safety risks 
( Wu et al., 2021 ). Therefore, considerable attention has been directed

Over the years, a range of maintenance approaches has been devel -
oped to enhance infrastructure longevity and reliability. Proactive 
maintenance strategies aim to prevent deterioration through timely and 
planned interventions. One common approach is time-based mainte -
nance, which involves performing scheduled repairs at predetermined 
intervals, regardless of the structure's actual condition. Although 
straightforward to implement, time-based methods often lead to in -
efficiencies by triggering unnecessary maintenance actions ( de Jonge 
et al., 2017 ). In contrast, condition-based maintenance, which relies 
on observed or predicted structural states derived from inspection data 
or analytical models, tends to be more efficient than time-based main -
tenance due to its reliance on inspection information and tailoring in -
terventions to the asset's actual condition and performance needs ( Zonta 
et al., 2020 ).
However, the rapid expansion of transportation infrastructure and 
advances in technology have considerably reshaped the landscape of 
bridge management. Preventive maintenance, particularly for bridges 
with different structural forms, could be a challenge for engineers, 
especially in the absence of a structured, data-informed decision-making 
frameworks ( Brighenti et al., 2024 ; Rashidi et al., 2016a ). This is mainly 
because strategic bridge management and maintenance extends beyond 
routine preventive maintenance needs; it requires prioritizing long-term 
safety, operational functionality, and economic sustainability 
( Frangopol and Kim, 2022 ). Given the substantial financial and envi -
ronmental implications associated with replacing deteriorating bridge 
structures, extending the service life of existing bridges has become a 
more cost-effective and sustainable alternative.
In recent decades, decision-making in bridge asset management has 
become progressively more complex, largely due to structural com -
plexities and the integration of advanced digital and communication 
technologies. These advancements have expanded the range of potential 
management solutions, complicating the identification of optimal stra -
tegies for asset managers. As a result, a number of specialized Bridge 
Management Systems (BMSs), such as PONTIS ( Thompson et al., 1998 ), 
DANBRO( Andersen, 1990 ) and BRIDGIT ( Hawk and Small, 1998 ), have 
been developed to support systematic maintenance planning and asset 
preservation. These systems integrate data inputs, regulatory standards, 
and specialized software tools to facilitate tasks such as maintenance 
planning, prioritization of interventions, and lifecycle cost optimization 
( Brighenti et al., 2024 ; Rashidi, 2013 ).
Emerging technologies including Bridge Information Modelling 
(BrIM), Digital Twins (DTs) and Deep Learning (DL) are further 
advancing the BMS capabilities. By enabling the digital representation of 
assets and integrating real-time monitoring data with predictive models, 
these technologies allow for reliable assessment of structural health and 
more informed decisions regarding the timing and scope of maintenance 
activities ( Mousavi et al., 2025a , 2025b ). Consequently, transportation 
authorities around the world are increasingly adopting these systems to 
address the growing complexity of infrastructure management and 
improve the efficiency and effectiveness of bridge maintenance practices 
( Shahrivar et al., 2025 ).
Despite significant advancements in development of bridge DTs, 
application of Decision Support Systems (DSS) into bridge management 
systems has been rarely considered in bridge DTs ( Mousavi et al., 2024 ). 
Whereas they can provide powerful capabilities for enhancing mainte -
nance efficiency, improving decision-making quality, and reducing the 
time and cost associated with planning interventions. Therefore, when 
effectively integrated, they could serve as the core of a DT, synthesizing 
the data gleaned from monitoring, predictive modelling, simulation, and 
lifecycle analysis into actionable insights. Nonetheless, the current 
implementations of bridge DTs often lack robust bidirectional strategies 
capable of translating analytical outputs into actionable maintenance 
interventions.
Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a promising method -
ology for enhancing prescriptive maintenance strategies ( Saifullah et al., 
2024 ). In the context of infrastructure management, RL formulates the 
maintenance planning problem as a sequential decision-making prob -
lem, where each decision influences both immediate outcomes and 
future system performance. RL algorithms learn optimal strategies 
through iterative interactions with the environment, guided by reward 
signals that quantify the effectiveness of decisions. The primary goal is 
to maximize cumulative rewards over time, reflecting long-term per -
formance and cost efficiency ( Sutton and Barto, 1998 ). The ability of RL 
to accommodate complex infrastructure portfolios, support long-term 
optimization over extended planning horizons, and operate effectively 
under uncertainty highlights its potential to transform infrastructure 
maintenance and asset management practices. While RL offers a theo -
retically sound framework for intelligent maintenance planning under 
uncertainty, existing methodologies are often limited by the absence of 
effective algorithms for deriving optimal policies. A critical review of the 
literature reveals persistent limitations in achieving key operational 
objectives, as most existing RL-based approaches predominantly focus 
on single objective optimization ( Heng et al., 2024 ). In practice, how -
ever, maintenance optimization problems inherently involve multiple, 
and often conflicting, objectives that must be considered simulta -
neously. These limitations emphasize the necessity for dedicated 
research efforts that bridge the gap between theoretical RL constructs 
and their practical implementation in real-world asset management 
systems.
This study introduces an advanced RL-based Decision Support Sys -
tem (RL-DSS) for bridge maintenance optimization using Multi- 
Objective Deep Centralized Multi-Agent Actor-Critic (MO-DCMAC). 
The framework employs MO-DCMAC to simultaneously handle un -
certainties and incorporating multiple, distinct penalty signals through a 
weighted linear utility formulation. In the proposed framework, the 
environment produces a vector-valued reward corresponding to main -
tenance cost, structural risk, and serviceability, while the weighted 
utility function is applied during policy optimization to derive the scalar 
decision signal used for policy improvement. This approach enables 
intelligent agents to learn effective maintenance policies while consid -
ering component interactions, and multiple criteria, including material 
properties, bridge age, traffic loading, environmental conditions, and 
long-term structural health. A multi-criteria decision-making process is 
integrated into the framework to balance cost, risk, and performance 
objectives. The main contributions and novelties of this study are as 
follows: 
1. Development of a multi-objective RL-based Decision Support System 
(RL-DSS) for bridge maintenance, designed to operate as a decision- 
making module within DT-based bridge management environments.
2. The bridge maintenance problem is formulated as a Multi-Objective 
Markov Decision Process (MOMDP), with maintenance cost, struc -
tural risk, and serviceability represented as separate reward com -
ponents. A fixed linear utility function is used for policy 
optimization, while the vector-valued representation is retained to 
preserve detailed per-objective analysis.
3. The MO-DCMAC framework is adapted from two to three objectives 
as an implementation-level contribution. A vectorized joint distri -
butional critic and a multi-dimensional Bellman projection are 
developed to ensure computationally efficient learning in the three- 
objective setting.
4. Empirical validation across multiple real-world bridge case studies 
and comparison of the proposed RL-DSS performance with the 
multiple established rule-based policies.
A series of case study evaluations were conducted using real-world 
bridge case studies to assess the effectiveness of the proposed frame -
work in long-term maintenance planning. The results demonstrate that 
RL-DSS, powered by the MO-DCMAC algorithm, can effectively balance

2. Related works and motivations
In bridge maintenance optimization, decision-making can be struc -
tured at either the network level or the individual asset level. Network- 
level strategies optimize maintenance across multiple assets, typically 
applying uniform interventions to all structures ( Jaafaru and Agbelie, 
2022 ; Lei et al., 2022 ; Xu and Guo, 2022 ). SHM data and sustainabil -
ity criteria could also be combined into a single utility-based reward to 
enable efficient life-cycle maintenance policies across multiple bridges 
at network level ( Lei et al., 2023 , 2025 ). In contrast, bridge-level opti -
mization, which is the focus of this study, targets a single structure and 
concentrates on the strategic maintenance of its internal components. 
Although the scale is smaller, bridge-level optimization introduces its 
own complexities, particularly the compounded risks that arise when 
multiple critical subsystems degrade simultaneously.
A review of existing literature reveals that many bridge-level main -
tenance strategies have been proposed. These methods are predomi -
nantly grounded in classical optimization approaches, rule-based 
systems, or supervised machine learning techniques. For example, Wang 
et al. (2024) proposed a data-driven maintenance optimization frame -
work for bridge infrastructure that integrates reliability-based deterio -
ration, intervention, and financial models. Similarly, Ghafoori et al. 
(2024) introduced a system that integrates ML techniques and binary 
linear programming to predict the condition of concrete bridge elements 
and optimize maintenance interventions. Using historical data, the 
system models deterioration and generates long-term plans under 
budget constraints. Other frameworks, such as the ontology-driven 
smart contract system for bridge preservation decision-making pro -
posed by He et al. (2025) , focus on the gap in formalizing and applying 
implicit rules derived from expert judgment. Their method integrates 
domain ontology with smart contracts to enable transparent rule 
execution and decentralized data sharing. While these approaches offer 
valuable contributions, they are limited in their capacity to adapt 
dynamically to evolving bridge conditions and uncertain operational 
environments.
In contrast, RL methods have emerged as promising tools for adap -
tive and intelligent infrastructure maintenance planning. These tech -
niques formulate maintenance as a sequential decision-making process, 
enabling agents to learn optimal policies through environmental inter -
action. For instance, Wei et al. (2020) developed a DRL-based frame -
work using the Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithm to optimize bridge 
maintenance decisions. The model considered five system states and 
four possible actions per component, with rewards based on mainte -
nance costs and structural risks. In another research, Xin et al. (2023)
proposed a dynamic programming-based intelligent agent to optimize 
component level bridge maintenance decisions using regular inspection 
data. A two-parameter Weibull distribution predicts the duration of 
bridge elements in various states, enhancing cost estimation and 
tailoring decisions to specific defects.
However, a critical observation from the literature shows that Multi- 
Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL) methods tailored for bridge 
maintenance planning remain relatively scarce. While most existing RL 
applications in bridge management focus on optimizing single-objective 
reward structures, real-world maintenance scenarios inherently involve 
multiple, often conflicting objectives. Only a few studies have explicitly 
formulated the problem within a MORL framework. For example, Du 
and Ghavidel (2022) proposed a parameterized RL framework that 
generates a unified maintenance policy for diverse highway bridges. The 
framework simultaneously optimizes multiple objectives by integrating 
a time- and policy-dependent life-cycle risk assessment, balancing 
maintenance cost and structural risk over time. In another study, van 
Remmerden et al. (2025) presented a Multi-Objective Deep Reinforce -
ment Learning (MODRL) framework for infrastructure maintenance that 
directly optimizes policies under nonlinear utility functions. The method 
is tested using threshold-based and FMECA-based utility functions 
across multiple maintenance environments, including historical quay 
walls in Amsterdam. Results show MO-DCMAC significantly out -
performs conventional heuristic rule-based policies, demonstrating its 
effectiveness in balancing cost and structural safety.
Beyond the MORL, limited research integrates intelligent DSS 
methods within DTs frameworks for predictive and data-driven bridge 
management. For example, Heng et al. (2024) proposed a DT-based 
maintenance framework for ageing steel bridge hangers, integrating 
monitoring data, inspections, and predictive modelling. Their method 
integrates Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), 
Asynchronous Advantage Actor Critic, and Bayesian Dynamic Linear 
Model algorithms to guide proactive interventions such as early repairs 
and strategic replacements, thereby improving maintenance efficiency 
through coordinated, condition-based, and cost-effective decision- -
making. Table 1 presents the comparison of solutions reported in recent 
literature for bridge maintenance optimization problem. The gaps in 
these methods include the limited application of MORL tailored for 
bridge infrastructure maintenance, the lack of application of RL-based 
approaches within DT technologies, and the insufficient ability of 
existing models to dynamically adapt to real-time structural conditions 
and evolving operational demands. This study aims to address these key 
challenges through the development of a novel RL-DSS framework that 
can be seamlessly integrated with bridge digital twins to provide a 
robust and adaptive decision-making tool.
3. Theoretical background
This section provides a brief overview of the fundamental concepts 
and background related to the DL algorithms employed in the proposed 
RL-DSS. It begins with an introduction to RL, followed by a brief 
explanation of MORL and DCMAC.
3.1. Brief overview on reinforcement learning
RL involves an autonomous agent that learns optimal strategies for 
achieving predefined objectives through continuous interaction with its 
environment. RL does not require prior data for training; instead, the 
agent iteratively explores its environment, evaluates the outcomes of its 
actions through a reward-based feedback mechanism, and refines its 
decision-making policy. The primary objective in RL is to maximize the 
cumulative reward over time, enabling the agent to identify and execute 
actions that yield the most beneficial long-term outcomes. This problem 
can be systematically modelled using a Markov Decision Process (MDP) 
( Sutton and Barto, 1998 ), which provides a mathematical framework for 
decision-making under uncertainty and over extended time horizons.
In an MDP, the state transitions and rewards are defined by a tuple ( S ,
A , P , R , γ ) , where S is the state space, A is the action space, P defines 
transition probabilities, R is the reward function, and γ ∈ [0,1] is the 
discount factor that determines the importance of future rewards. The 
Bellman expectation equation provides a recursive definition for the 
state-action value function Q under a policy π :

RL methods are classified as model-based, which uses a known model 
of the environment to plan actions efficiently but can be sensitive to 
model inaccuracies, and model-free approaches, which learn optimal 
behaviour through trial and error without a predefined model.
RL has experienced significant advancements due to the emergence 
of novel algorithms capable of tackling increasingly complex problems. 
In this context, complexity is primarily associated with high- 
dimensional state and action spaces, which often pose limitations for 
traditional RL methods. DL techniques have effectively mitigated these 
challenges through Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) that leverages 
deep neural networks to manage and learn from complex, high- 
dimensional environments.
In DRL, neural networks are used to approximate value functions, 
such as Q ( s , a ; θ ) where θ are the parameters of the Q-network. One 
example is the DQN, where the temporal difference error is minimized 
by adjusting θ through backpropagation, enabling generalization across 
large, continuous state spaces.
3.2. Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL)
Traditional RL approaches are inherently designed to optimize pol -
icies for single-objective tasks. However, many real-world problems 
involve the simultaneous consideration of multiple objectives, which 
often differ fundamentally in nature and cannot be readily combined 
into a single scalar reward. These objectives represent diverse di -
mensions of system performance that are neither directly comparable 
nor easily reducible to a unified metric. Consequently, addressing the 
complex and multi-dimensional nature of infrastructure maintenance 
demands more advanced and flexible learning frameworks.
To address this, MORL extends the RL framework to accommodate 
multiple goals. MORL models are formalized through a multi-objective 
MDP, where the key distinction from a conventional MDP lies in the 
reward function which yields a vector of rewards across multiple ob -
jectives rather than a single scalar.
Unlike conventional RL, assessing policy performance in MORL 
presents inherent complexity. This arises from the presence of multiple, 
often conflicting, objectives. For instance, one policy may improve one 
objective while another improves a different one, making comparisons 
ambiguous without understanding of the relative importance of objec -
tives. This challenge is particularly evident in bridge maintenance 
planning, where decision-makers must weigh trade-offs.
In such cases, a key concept is Pareto optimality. A policy is 
considered Pareto-optimal if no other policy can improve one objective 
without worsening another. The set of all such non-dominated policies 
forms the Pareto front, providing a visual and analytical tool for eval -
uating trade-offs.
To address this, utility-based approaches are commonly employed to 
aggregate the vector of cumulative rewards into a single scalar utility 
value using a predefined function. This scalarization enables consistent 
evaluation and comparison of policies within the multi-objective 
framework. A common form of scalarization is the linear scalarization 
method, where:

execute local actor policies, enabling coordinated learning alongside 
independent action.

4. Proposed RL-DSS framework
The application of deep RL and DSS within digital twinning enhances 
bridge optimal maintenance planning by enabling informed and pre -
dictive decision-making. To fully exploit these advantages, a RL-based 
DSS reference framework is essential.
The proposed RL-DSS is designed to be integrated into data-driven 
DT environments, where data from diverse sensors are processed using 
advanced techniques to provide a real-time representation of the 
bridge's condition. In this framework, the DT utilizes its internal state- 
estimation and data assimilation algorithms to process, filter, and fuse 
real-time structural health monitoring data, thereby generating an 
updated and reliable system-state representation that is subsequently 
supplied to the RL-DSS for decision making. This continuously updated 
bridge state information serves as the input for decision-making within 
the proposed RL-DSS framework in this study. As the condition of bridge 
elements changes based on real-time data streams, the decision-making 
process within the RL-DSS dynamically selects actions according to the 
updated system states using a pre-trained policy, without online 
retraining or parameter updating. The primary focus of this research is 
on the RL-based decision-making process, designed to be integrated into 
data-driven DTs, while detailed explanations of damage detection stra -
tegies within DTs are beyond the scope of this study and can be found in 
recent related works such as Mousavi et al. (2025b) .
The proposed framework, shown in Fig. 1 , presents a simulation- 
based methodology to optimize bridge maintenance strategies using a 
Multi-Objective Deep Reinforcement Learning (MODRL) framework. 
This approach addresses the complexity of infrastructure management 
decisions where multiple conflicting objectives must be balanced 
simultaneously. The primary objectives include minimizing mainte -
nance costs, managing expected failure consequences, and maintaining 
structural serviceability (penalizing poor DS). The framework employs a 
MA architecture where individual agents manage distinct bridge com -
ponents, enabling distributed decision-making within a unified learning 
structure. This design captures interdependencies between structural 
elements while maintaining computational efficiency across complex 
bridge systems.
As illustrated in Fig. 1 , the methodology follows an iterative process 
beginning with bridge condition initialization, incorporating damage 
indices, material properties, environmental factors, and component 
ages. The system operates within a dynamic simulation environment 
that continuously reflects evolving infrastructure states, accounting for 
both maintenance interventions and natural deterioration processes.
The decision-making process involves systematic observation 
collection where agents gather state information representing current 
bridge conditions. Based on these observations and learned policies, 
agents select maintenance actions from a predefined set ranging from no 
action to complete component replacement. Selected actions are applied 
within the simulation environment, triggering state updates that reflect 
immediate maintenance effects and ongoing deterioration.
A reward calculation mechanism evaluates outcomes across the 
three primary objectives. This multi-objective penalty-based system 
quantifies trade-offs between direct cost expenditure, risk thresholds, 
and serviceability penalties. The framework incorporates a distribu -
tional network that estimates long-term consequences by predicting 
probability distributions over future returns, providing comprehensive 
evaluation of policy decisions.
Policy optimization integrates calculated rewards and network 
evaluations through a utility function that enables multi-objective 
outcome integration. This approach allows the system to learn mainte -
nance strategies aligned with long-term infrastructure management 
goals while adapting to the complex, time-dependent nature of struc -
tural deterioration.
The iterative framework enables continuous learning and adaptation 
where agents progressively refine decision-making capabilities through 
repeated simulation interactions. This methodology specifically ad -
dresses long-term infrastructure planning challenges where current de -
cisions have consequences extending over decades of operational life. 
The details of the proposed framework will be discussed in the next 
sections.

5.1. Bridge condition assessment
As the initial step of the proposed framework, the current bridge 
condition state is required. The bridge condition or health index is a 
critical metric for evaluating the structural and functional performance 
of bridge infrastructure. These general qualitative condition indices are 
commonly derived from the condition assessment of bridge elements 
considering the level of service the bridge provides. It plays a pivotal 
role in identifying deteriorated components and informing the prioriti -
zation of maintenance and rehabilitation activities ( Chase et al., 2016 ). 
The overall condition of each structural element is evaluated using a 
five-level Damage State (DS) rating scale during the inspection process, 
as outlined in Table 2 .
Traditionally, DS is determined through on-site inspections con -
ducted by qualified inspectors. However, for bridges that are equipped 
with actively managed DTs, condition assessments can also be derived 
from these digital models through continuous monitoring and data 
evaluations. In cases where neither DT systems nor recent inspection 
reports are available, expert judgment can serve as a valuable alterna -
tive for estimating the current condition of the bridge elements. This 
flexible approach ensures that bridge condition data can still be lever -
aged to support informed decision-making in asset management, even 
under data-limited circumstances.
Beyond the assessment of element-level damage states, this study 
also incorporates two additional technical factors into the evaluation 
framework, following methodologies established in previous research 
( Mohammadi et al., 2023 ; Rashidi et al., 2016a ). These include material 
vulnerability, element importance factor and associated causal factors. 
Integrating these variables allows for a more comprehensive and reliable 
bridge maintenance strategy by addressing technical uncertainties and 
reflecting the differential significance and susceptibility of various 
components within the overall structural system. The following sub -
sections provide a detailed explanation of each of these factors.
5.1.1. Material vulnerability (MV)
The MV quantifies the susceptibility of different construction mate -
rials to environmental degradation and aging. Material type plays a 
crucial role in determining the durability and long-term performance of 
bridge components, particularly under harsh service conditions.
While a detailed vulnerability model of bridge elements across all 
hazard types requires extensive investigations, existing literature and 
expert assessments indicate that steel components are generally more 
prone to deterioration than precast reinforced concrete elements under 
harsh environmental conditions, whereas timber components are 
particularly vulnerable to biological degradation and moisture-related 
damage ( Rashidi and Gibson, 2011 ; Valenzuela et al., 2010 ). Table 3
presents the vulnerability classification of commonly used bridge con -
struction materials.
5.1.2. Causal factors (CF)
Bridge components deteriorate progressively over time and the rate 
of deterioration is influenced by multiple interacting parameters, 
including pre-existing conditions, such as original design specifications 
and construction quality, and post-existing variables related to opera -
tional environments, usage patterns, and quality of inspection practices. 
To systematically capture the impact of these variables on structural 
degradation, CF criterion is utilized, which is composed of four primary 
dimensions including environmental exposure, structural age, road 
type/loading, and inspection quality. 
• Environmental Aggression (EA) : Environmental factors signifi -
cantly affect material durability, especially in concrete structures 
through both chemical and physical processes induced by climatic 
variables, pollutants, and human activity. These factors can trigger 
or accelerate deterioration like carbonation, chloride corrosion, and 
alkali – silica reaction, with early construction defects further facili -
tating harmful ingress ( Rashidi et al., 2016a ).
• Structural Age (A): The age of a bridge indicates its integrity and 
fatigue performance. While many bridges have a nominal 50-year 
design life, some continue to operate up to 100 years, though ele -
ments like bearings may need replacement in 25 years. Extended 
service necessitates re-evaluating safety and durability against 
original design and current performance standards ( Rashidi and 
Gibson, 2012 ).
• Road Type and Loading (R) : The intensity and nature of traffic 
loading vary according to a bridge's functional role within the 
transportation network. In this study, traffic load intensity is 
approximated using the Average Annual Daily Traffic (AADT) 
metric. Higher AADT values, characteristic of arterial or highway 
bridges, are associated with increased structural demand and more 
rapid wear, whereas bridges on local roads typically experience 
lower traffic volumes and consequently slower deterioration.
• Inspection Level (I) : The quality and consistency of condition as -
sessments are influenced by human factors and inspection practices, 
including inspector expertise, environment, components accessi -
bility, and adequacy of inspection tools ( Ellenberg et al., 2016 ). In 
this regard, four distinct levels of bridge inspections have been 
considered. The initial inspection is conducted after construction. 
Routine inspections monitor the general condition of the bridge by 
regular visual evaluations. Detailed inspections use non-destructive 
testing for potential structural issues, and special inspections are 
performed following incidents or observed anomalies to support 
structural analysis for interventions guide ( Mohammadi et al., 2023 ).
Each of the above factors is categorized into four discrete levels, as 
presented in Table 4 .

5.1.3. Importance factor (IF)
The IF is used to represent the relative criticality of each bridge 
element to overall structural safety and service. IF expresses how 
strongly a component's failure would influence system performance. In 
practice, IF values are assigned per component based on engineering 
judgment about structural role, redundancy, and consequence of failure. 
Within the risk formulation, IF is used as a weight to combine 
component-level failure probabilities into the probability of system 
collapse. After each action, updated component failure probabilities are 
aggregated using normalized IF values, ensuring that failures of more 
critical elements have a stronger impact on the system-level risk signal.
5.2. Bridge maintenance simulation environment
The bridge maintenance problem is formulated as a MOMDP in this 
study to provide a structured foundation for implementing sequential 
decision-making under uncertainties, where multiple conflicting objec -
tives must be optimized over time.
However, the implementation of such frameworks in real-world 
bridge maintenance encounters data paradox challenge. RL agents 
require large volumes of diverse data that represent a wide range of 
system states and the outcomes of various actions in order to learn 
effective policies. In practice, however, comprehensive and longitudinal 
real-world data for bridge maintenance is exceptionally scarce and 
inherently unsuitable for exploratory learning. The reasons for this are 
threefold: 
• Missing Alternative Outcomes : Historical records only show what 
maintenance actions were actually taken, not what would have 
happened with different choices. Since RL systems learn by 
comparing different possible actions, this creates a fundamental gap 
in the available data.
• Impractical Learning Requirements : Bridges operate over de -
cades, but RL systems need millions of trial-and-error experiments to 
learn effectively. Testing different maintenance strategies on real, 
operational bridges would be both impossible to complete within 
reasonable timeframes and dangerously risky.
• Poor Data Quality : Existing maintenance records are often incom -
plete, inconsistent, or stored in incompatible formats. This makes 
them unsuitable for training RL systems, which require reliable and 
standardized information about system conditions, actions taken, 
and outcomes achieved.
To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a high-fidelity 
simulation-based training environment to enable safe, repeatable, and 
scalable learning for long-term bridge maintenance planning. This dig -
ital environment enables the RL agent to interact with a virtual repre -
sentation of a bridge over countless simulated life cycles, allowing it to 
develop and refine policies without the risks and constraints associated 
with real-world testing. The validity of this simulation-based approach 
hinges on the quality of its underlying models and parameters. The 
environment for this study is not built on arbitrary rules but is param -
eterized based on established engineering principles, empirical models, 
and data reported in existing literature.
Within this simulated framework, the MOMDP is implemented by 
explicitly defining its fundamental components including the state 
space, action space, stochastic transition functions and reward structure. 
Therefore, MOMDP can be defined by a tuple ( S , A , P , R , γ ) , where S is a set 
of states representing the condition of the bridge. A is set of actions 
corresponding to maintenance interventions. P is a transition function 
defining the probability of moving from one state to another given an 
action. R is a multi-objective reward function that provides feedback on 
the action's outcome. γ is discount factor for future rewards. In following 
subsections, the detailed definitions of each component in the proposed 
framework implementation are presented.
5.2.1. State representation
The primary state variables for each bridge component include a 
discrete DS, as described in Section 5.1 and Table 2 , which quantifies the 
level of deterioration from Healthy to Critical , and the component's age, 
defined as the time elapsed since its last major repair or replacement. 
These variables are fundamental for assessing the current structural 
condition and projecting future maintenance requirements. DS values 
are typically derived from systematic assessments of individual struc -
tural elements based on standardized inspection criteria, such as the 
five-level DS rating scale, which reflects the severity of material defects 
and the extent of cross-sectional or surface area loss. Together, these 
state indicators provide a robust foundation for modelling bridge con -
dition dynamics and informing effective maintenance decision-making.
5.2.2. Maintenance action space
The decision-making process for maintenance interventions was 
structured through a discrete action space designed to enable systematic 
and interpretable strategies. For each bridge component, the agent can 
choose from a standardized set of four distinct maintenance actions. 
When the agent selects an action, the environment applies deterministic 
changes to the corresponding component's state: 
1. No Action signifies deferring active maintenance, allowing compo -
nents to continue operating under current conditions subject to 
ongoing deterioration and the component's state is only affected by 
the stochastic deterioration model for that time step. No Action 
signifies deferring active maintenance. This option enables cost- 
effective strategies by avoiding unnecessary interventions when 
structural performance remains acceptable.
2. Minor Repair actions address moderate deterioration levels through 
limited restoration. They reduce the component's DS by one level 
without significantly altering its age or long-term service life tra -
jectory. These interventions represent routine, low-cost maintenance 
activities managing localized damage or slowing deterioration 
progression.
3. Major Repair actions provide substantial restoration capabilities. 
They improve the component's condition by reducing its DS by two 
levels while also rejuvenating the component by reducing its effec -
tive age by a predefined period which is considered fixed across all 
components for simplicity. This action extends service life through 
more comprehensive intervention.
4. Replacement is the most intensive intervention, completely renew -
ing a component by restoring its DS to " Healthy " (DS = 0) and reset -
ting its age to zero. Unlike traditional rule-based models that 
explicitly mask or restrict this action to advanced DS, the proposed 
framework allows the agent unrestricted access to all actions. 
Instead, the agent learns to avoid premature replacements through 
strict economic "overkill" penalties, fostering a robust and naturally 
learned decision-making process.
