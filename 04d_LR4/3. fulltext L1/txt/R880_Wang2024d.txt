Construction of disaster-resilient cities has attracted considerable attention. However, traditional methods of 
studying urban disaster resilience through experimental approaches are often constrained by various limitations, 
such as testing sites, costs and ethical considerations. To address these constraints, this paper proposes incor- 
porating digital twin concepts into urban disaster resilience research. By establishing a connection between the 
physical realm of the city and its virtual counterpart, this approach utilizes digital simulations to overcome the 
limitations of experimental methods and enables dynamic deduction and control of the disaster process. This pa- 
per delves into three key aspects encompassing the acquisition of data from reality to the virtual space, disaster 
simulation within the virtual space, and translation of virtual insights into eﬀective disaster prevention strate- 
gies in reality. It provides a comprehensive summary of relevant research endeavors from the authors’ research 
group and showcases the eﬀectiveness and potential of the proposed techniques. These ﬁndings serve as refer- 
ences for pre-disaster planning, real-time emergency assessments, post-disaster rescue operations, and accident 
investigations for buildings and cities. 
1. Introduction 
“Sustainable Cities and Communities ”(also known as SDG 11) is at 
the heart of achieving all 17 of the United Nations Sustainable Develop- 
ment Goals (SDGs) [ 1 , 2 ], aiming to ensure inclusive, safe, resilient, and 
sustainable cities and communities by providing aﬀordable transporta- 
tion solutions, curbing urban sprawl, promoting active participation in 
urban governance, safeguarding cultural assets, and addressing climate 
change issues. Earthquake, ﬁre, and wind disasters are three of the most 
devastating disasters, resulting in immediate loss of life and long-term 
economic damage [ 3–5 ]. Taking China as an example, according to data 
released by the National Bureau of Statistics, the direct economic losses 
caused by earthquake, ﬁre, and wind disasters in 2021 were 10.6, 6.7, 
and 13.2 billion, respectively. Therefore, enhancing the resilience of 
cities and communities against these disasters is of paramount impor- 
tance for advancing SDG 11. 
In previous studies, physical-object-based experimental methods 
have been widely used to test the performance and resilience levels of 
infrastructures, such as buildings and bridges, at an individual object 
scale [ 6–8 ]. Owing to limitations resulting from testing sites and costs, 
this method faces signiﬁcant diﬃculties when applied at an urban scale.

Hence, there is an urgent need for a more feasible, eﬃcient, and accurate 
method for studying the disaster resilience of buildings and cities. 
Digital twins have become popular research topics in recent years 
[ 9–11 ]. The earliest concept of digital twins was proposed by Grieves 
[ 12 ] in 2003. In 2011, the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Admin- 
istration (NASA) ﬁrst introduced the concept of “twins ”i n the Apollo 
program [ 13 ] and provided a detailed deﬁnition of digital twins [ 14 ]. 
It refers to mapping real-world entities into the virtual space, synchro- 
nizing the virtual model with the real state of the entities, and achiev- 
ing interaction between the virtual and real worlds [ 15 ]. Digital twin 
technology can be applied to guide decision-making in the real world 
through simulations in the virtual space. Hence, in the last 10 years, re- 
searchers from diﬀerent ﬁelds have explored the applications of digital 
twins in various areas, such as product design [ 16–19 ], manufacturing 
[ 19–21 ], and operation condition monitoring [ 22–24 ]. 
In civil engineering, digital twin technology can not only be used 
for three-dimensional(3D) reconstruction and visualization of individ- 
ual infrastructure objects[25–28] or cities[29–30], but also can be ap- 
plied to disaster risk assessment and other ﬁelds[31–38].Regarding in- 
dividual infrastructure objects, Ariyachandra and Brilakis [ 25 ] used a 
car-mounted laser scanner to obtain the coordinate positions of rail-

government introduced the “Virtual Singapore ”project, which utilized 
Dassault Systemes’ 3D EXPERIENCE platform to digitally model Singa- 
pore’s buildings, infrastructure, trees, etc. It incorporates images and 
data collected by sensors for analysis, and provides urban environment 
simulation, operational analysis, planning, and management decision- 
making for residents, businesses, government agencies, and research 
communities. The government of New South Wales in Australia initiated 
a virtual four-dimensional model of the Western Sydney region, encom- 
passing buildings, subsurface plans, terrain, property boundaries, and 
utilities such as electricity, water, and sewage. Taking the West Cam- 
bridge area of the University of Cambridge as an example, Lu et al. [ 29 ] 
proposed a digital twin system architecture for building clusters and 
city scales by integrating heterogeneous data sources to support histori- 
cal data queries and analysis. In addition, they discussed the challenges 
of constructing a digital twin city from a technical perspective, based 
on the case of the University of Cambridge. White et al. [ 30 ] released a 
digital twin model of Dublin’s port area, allowing citizens to report and 
mark city issues through interactive devices and enabling simulations of 
ﬂood propagation, among other functionalities. 
Moreover, digital twin technology is being investigated to assess the 
damage to buildings and the resilience of virtual communities. Enderami 
et al. [ 31 , 32 ] proposed a novel computational model and quantitative 
assessment framework that can validate and conﬁrm the impacts, dam- 
ages, and recovery processes of community-level disasters. Ellingwood 
et al. [ 33 ] established a virtual testbed to develop measurement science 
for community resilience assessment. Deierlein et al. [ 34 , 35 ] developed 
a computational workﬂow for regional hazard simulation, addressing 
the characterization and simulation of the destructive impacts of natural 
hazards on buildings and civil infrastructure. They also quantiﬁed the 
resulting economic losses, disruptions, and other societal consequences. 
Braik & Koliou [ 36 ] introduced a novel digital twin framework for elec- 
tric power networks, which is capable of modeling and estimating faults 
and performance states of various components within electric power net- 
works. Wang et al. [ 37 ] established an urban building inventory using 
deep learning techniques, providing the necessary data for disaster and 
risk management planning and simulations. Attary et al. [ 38 ] proposed 
a combination of electric power networks and building damage proba- 
bilities under wind disasters using techniques such as weighted cellular 
automata. 
Although digital twin technology has been extensively applied in 
civil engineering, previous studies have primarily focused on the daily 
operation and maintenance of infrastructure objects and cities. In ad- 
dition, there are also studies on urban resilience through digital twins, 
but this is still its infancy early stages. Further research is needed to in- 
vestigate the application of digital twin technology in enhancing urban 
disaster resilience. 
Motivated by the encouraging advancements in digital twins, in- 
troducing the concept of digital twins into research on city disaster 
prevention can provide quantitative guidance for promoting city re- 
silience by predicting or reproducing the impact of disasters in the vir- 
tual space. As shown in Fig. 1 , three important issues must be addressed 
in the digital twin-driven analysis of disaster resilience for buildings and 
cities. 
(1) How can building and city information be eﬃciently obtained from 
the real world and mapped from reality to virtuality? 
(2) How can disaster simulation models be established for buildings and 
cities in the virtual space to predict or simulate disaster processes 
accurately? 
(3) How can simulation results from the virtual space be used to guide 
disaster prevention and mitigation strategies in the real world and 
achieve a feedback loop from virtuality to reality? 
A digital twin cycle can be eﬀectively established by addressing these 
three issues, providing a solid framework for the formation of rational 
disaster prevention and mitigation strategies. Based on the authors’ re- 
cent research outcomes, this paper discusses these three issues. Speciﬁ- 
cally, three aspects, namely disaster simulation models for buildings and 
cities in the virtual space, artiﬁcial intelligence (AI)-powered building 
and city data acquisition from reality to virtuality, and disaster preven- 
tion and mitigation implementations based on the feedback loop from 
virtuality to reality, are introduced in the following sections. The re- 
search outcomes highlight the potential of digital twins in facilitating 
the improvement of city resilience and the achievement of SDG 11. 
2. Disaster simulation models for buildings and cities in the 
virtual space 
Owing to the low-frequency nature of disasters and the rapid devel- 
opment of cities, there is a lack of disaster prevention and mitigation

sequently, the reliability and applicability of the data-driven disaster 
simulation models generated by ﬁtting historical disaster data require 
further evaluation. In comparison, simulation models established based 
on the physical laws of the disaster process exhibit favorable rationality 
and universality and can be applied to diﬀerent regions and disaster sce-

Therefore, to ensure the rationality and reliability of disaster simulation 
results, it is necessary to begin with the disaster mechanism and estab- 
lish a model that can truly reveal the physical laws of disaster evolution. 
Several physics-based damage simulation models have been developed 
at both the individual building and city scales by the authors, as intro- 
duced below. 
2.1. Individual-building-level damage simulation models 
(1) Models for earthquake 
Super high-rise buildings have been rapidly built in China, with lit- 
tle experience of seismic damage. To address their seismic vulnerabil- 
ity issues, Lu et al. [ 39 ] proposed a novel seismic collapse simulation 
model powered by a ﬁber beam, layered shell, and elemental deactiva- 
tion technique, which can capture strong nonlinear structural dynamic 
features during the entire process of earthquake-induced building col- 
lapse. This model has been applied to many landmark buildings, includ- 
ing the tallest Chinese building (i.e., the Shanghai Center with a height 
of 632 m) and Beijing’s tallest building (i.e., the CITIC Tower with a 
height of 528 m, Fig. 2 a). 
(2) Models for ﬁre 
Fire simulation in buildings is a sophisticated problem that interacts 
with both thermodynamics and ﬂuid dynamics. Lu et al. [ 40 ] and Xu 
et al. [ 41 ] proposed a high-ﬁdelity scenario construction method for ﬁre 
spread and a volume-rendering-based smoke visualization method that 
can predict or reproduce a ﬁre and smoke spread scene. These methods 
have been applied to ﬁre simulations of several important facilities such 
as schools, hospitals, and subway stations ( Fig. 2 b). 
(3) Models for wind 
To achieve a high-eﬃciency establishment of wind simulation mod- 
els, Lu et al. [ 42 ] developed an eﬃcient mapping method from a BIM 
to a computational ﬂuid dynamics (CFD) model ( Fig. 2 c). This method 
automatically extracts building façade data, based on which the estab- 
lished CFD model can subsequently be used to predict the wind ﬁeld 
around the building. 
2.2. City-scale building damage simulation models 
(1) Models for earthquake 
The ﬁnite element method has been widely used in previous stud- 
ies to predict the seismic performance of individual buildings [ 39 , 43 ]. 
Nevertheless, this method cannot be applied at the city scale because of 
the signiﬁcant amount of manpower and computing resources required 
to perform a detailed ﬁnite element analysis of all the buildings in an 
urban area. To address this issue, Lu and Guan [ 44 ] developed a city- 
scale time history analysis (THA) method, realizing the rational predic- 
tion of the story-level seismic dynamic response of building clusters in 
a large urban area and achieving eﬃcient and accurate seismic damage 
prediction and high-ﬁdelity visualization of earthquake scenarios. This 
method, based on the basic equations of structural dynamics theory, 
uses multiple-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) models to simulate the struc- 
tural behaviors of diﬀerent types of buildings. Speciﬁcally, the MDOF 
shear model is used to simulate a structure whose deformation mode is 
dominated by shear deformation (for example, a multistory frame struc- 
ture), whereas the MDOF ﬂexural–shear model is adopted for structures 
with large height-to-width ratios and ﬂexural–shear coupling deforma- 
tion modes (for example, a high-rise frame–shear wall structure). In ad- 
dition, a GPU/CPU collaborative computing technique is used to facil- 
itate computational eﬃciency. In the absence of design drawings, an 
automated model generation algorithm was proposed to estimate the 
parameters based on building inventory data [ 44 ]. The proposed pa- 
rameter determination method, based on a simulated design procedure 
and statistics obtained from extensive experimental and analytical stud- 
ies, can be easily applied to diﬀerent regions with diﬀerent design re- 
quirements. Given the above-mentioned advantages, this method has 
been adopted by well-known international projects, including the US- 
NSF NHERI SimCenter, and has been applied to disaster analyses of 
more than 50 cities worldwide, including the San Francisco Bay Area 
(1.84 million buildings). By dynamically visualizing the simulation re- 
sults, the displacement response of buildings is obtained ( Fig. 3 ). 
(2) Models for ﬁre 
To realistically reproduce the ﬁre-spread scene among building clus- 
ters, Xu et al. [ 45 ] and Lu et al. [ 46 ] proposed a ﬁre-spread simulation 
method based on thermal radiation and plume mechanisms and devel- 
oped a smoke diﬀusion simulation method based on ﬂuid dynamics. 
This method can not only clearly illustrate the dynamic evolution of 
ﬁre among building clusters, but can also be combined with the city- 
scale THA method to simulate ﬁre following earthquakes ( Fig. 4 b). The 
model considers the impact of various ground motions and the seismic 
resilience of diﬀerent buildings on the ﬁre spread process. The proposed 
method has been successfully applied in simulating ﬁres in two villages,

To accurately predict wind damage to building clusters, Gu et al. [ 47 ]

concept was introduced to facilitate the computational eﬃciency of CFD 
simulations. In addition, the inﬂuence of trees on wind pressures acting 
on building façade components was considered in the proposed method 
by modifying the Navier–Stokes equations within the tree domains. The 
method features an obvious advantage over regression-based empirical 
models in terms of accuracy and universality, and the prediction results 
are in good agreement with actual wind damage records. 
2.3. AI-powered damage simulation model 
Physics-based models generally need to address a large number of 
physical equations, which presents a bottleneck in terms of computa- 
tional eﬃciency. The introduction of AI may compensate for this deﬁ- 
ciency. Taking a regional earthquake simulation as an example, Xu et al. 
[ 48 ] and Lu et al. [ 49 ] combined deep learning with city-scale THA 
and proposed a real-time seismic damage simulation method. Speciﬁ- 
cally, as shown in Fig. 5 , the model comprises four main parts: histor- 
ical ground motion gathering, region-speciﬁc database generation via 
city-scale THA, long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network train- 
ing and veriﬁcation, and real-time damage prediction, which establishes 
high-performance mapping rules between ground motion and structural 
damage. The computational accuracy and eﬃciency of this model have 
been validated based on a case study of a typical large community. The 
results indicate that the average error can be decreased by 61 % com- 
pared with the traditional vulnerability method, and the exhausted com- 
putation time can be reduced from 12 min to 0.52 s compared with the 
physics-based model (i.e., city-scale THA), highlighting the great poten- 
tial of AI in damage simulation models.

3.1. Before disasters: building and city data acquisition based on oblique 
photography and street view 
Drones can be used to obtain city-scale building information quickly, 
exhibiting the advantage of cost eﬃciency compared with ﬁeld inves- 
tigation methods. Sun et al. [ 50 ] and Gu et al. [ 51 ] proposed a novel 
method to acquire building and city information using drones and deep 
learning. The method applies drones to obtain oblique photography data 
of the target area and realizes automatic information extraction of build- 
ings, façade components (such as windows), and other objects (such 
as trees) through deep learning algorithms. Two-dimensional (2D) data 
extracted from 2D images can be transformed into 3D data in the real 
world based on the proposed mapping method and the drone position 
data recorded during ﬂight. Consequently, the automatic establishment

3.2. During disasters: structural deformation data acquisition driven by 
computer vision 
Monitoring the deformation or displacement response of buildings 
during disasters is critical for ensuring structural safety. The recent de- 
velopment of computer vision has led to extensive research on the ap- 
plication of vision-based measurements in structural monitoring. Urban 
surveillance video cameras, which are widely installed in cities, can pro- 
duce numerous images and videos of urban scenes to measure struc- 
tural displacement. Provided that the building response can be mon- 
itored in real-time with urban surveillance video cameras, the city- 
scale structural safety issue can be addressed in a cost-eﬃcient man- 
ner without installing sensors on a large number of buildings in the 
city. 
However, structural displacement measurements may be inaccurate 
owing to the limited hardware resolution of surveillance video cameras 
or the long distance from the cameras to the monitored targets. To this 
end, Sun et al. [ 52 ] proposed a novel method to improve displacement 
measurement accuracy using a deep learning super-resolution model 
based on generative adversarial networks ( Fig. 7 ). The proposed method 
enhances the texture detail of low-resolution images or videos by supple- 
menting high-resolution photographs of the target, thereby improving 
the accuracy of vision-based displacement measurements. The proposed 
method showed good accuracy and stability in both static and dynamic 
experimental validations compared with the original low-resolution im- 
ages/video and interpolation-based super-resolution images/video. In 
conclusion, the proposed method can support displacement measure- 
ment of buildings and infrastructure based on urban surveillance video 
cameras. 
Furthermore, the 3D displacement of structures is important for 
structural analysis. It is challenging for existing vision-based measure- 
ment methods to obtain all the 3D displacement components because 
they require either multi-view camera systems or additional specially 
designed targets, making it diﬃcult to meet the requirements of mea- 
surement applications based on urban surveillance cameras. Sun et al. 
[ 53 ] proposed a 3D structural displacement measurement method using 
monocular vision and deep-learning-based pose estimation. This method 
uses virtual rendering to synthesize the training set based on 3D mod- 
els of the target objects and then trains the deep learning model Dense 
Pose Object Detector (DPOD) to estimate the poses of the target object. 
Finally, it measures the 3D translation of the structures based on the 
original and destination poses or the original pose and keypoint match- 
ing ( Fig. 8 ). The eﬀectiveness of the proposed method was validated 
through static and dynamic experiments, indicating that the method 
meets the requirements for obtaining 3D structural displacement and 
has good accuracy in identifying the principal frequencies of the dy- 
namic responses. The proposed method can support 3D displacement 
measurements of buildings and infrastructure based on urban surveil- 
lance cameras. 
3.3. After disaster: building seismic damage data acquisition based on 
drones 
Rapid assessment of seismic damage to buildings can facilitate im- 
proved emergency responses and timely relief in earthquake-prone ar- 
eas. With the rapid development of remote sensing technology, satel- 
lite or aerial images of disaster areas have become available within 
24 h after an earthquake. Collapsed buildings can be easily identiﬁed 
from these images. Hence, taking advantage of the identiﬁed build- 
ing collapse scene in a disaster area, Xiong et al. [ 54 ] and Lu et al. 
[ 55 ] proposed a near real-time seismic loss estimation method for 
regional buildings, improving the accuracy of nonlinear THA-based 
loss estimations ( Fig. 9 ). Speciﬁcally, a building image segmentation 
method was proposed using a 3D building model of the target area 
as a georeference, through which multiview-segmented building im- 
ages can be obtained. Subsequently, the VGGNet convolutional neu- 
ral network, which is ﬁne-tuned based on manually tagged building 
images obtained from the Internet, is adopted to identify the col- 
lapse distribution of buildings. Next, a series of THA is performed 
for the target regional buildings, thereby generating numerous simula- 
tion results. Through a machine-learning-powered similarity measure- 
ment method, the simulation results that bear strong similarities to the 
identiﬁed collapse scene are identiﬁed as optimal solutions, which are 
used to estimate the seismic loss. The simulation results of real-world 
case studies indicate that the use of the identiﬁed building collapse 
scene leads to considerably closer estimations of the actual economic 
losses.

4. Disaster prevention and mitigation implementation based on 
the feedback loop from virtuality to reality 
4.1. Pre-disaster planning and personnel evacuation drills 
Rational pre-disaster planning is of great importance for reducing 
disaster losses. The above disaster simulations based on digital twins can 
provide detailed damage predictions for buildings and cities, as well as 
high-ﬁdelity disaster scenarios. These outcomes can help managers iden- 
tify high-risk areas in cities more clearly, enabling more scientiﬁc and 
quantitative decision-making for reinforcement and renovation plans to 
enhance disaster resistance ( Fig. 10 ) [ 56 ]. In addition, based on disaster 
simulations using digital twins, Xu et al. [ 41 ] utilized a virtual reality 
(VR) platform to conduct virtual drills for personnel rescue, providing a 
reference for facilitating evacuation and rescue eﬃciency during disas- 
ters ( Fig. 11 ).

post-earthquake recovery. Therefore, taking advantage of the dense 
strong earthquake monitoring network constructed in countries such

accident investigations to develop rational accident impact mitigation 
solutions. For instance, by combining a ﬁnite element model with a 
physics engine, Lu et al. [ 58 ] reproduced the collapse process and ruin 
distribution of a real condominium building collapse accident in Florida. 
On June 24, 2021, a 40-year-old reinforced concrete ﬂat-plate structure 
building in Florida suﬀered a sudden partial collapse. The simulated col- 
lapse process and debris distribution agreed well with the actual con- 
ditions, indicating that the collapse initiated from the column removal 
zone and propagated to the east side of the building owing to the colli-

5. Case study 
5.1. Digital twin-based investigation of the SEG tower 
A super high-rise building in southern China, the SEG Tower, ex- 
perienced an unusual vibration in May 2021, necessitating an urgent 
investigation into its cause and the provision of viable solutions. Con- 
sequently, the authors’ research group employed a digital twin system 
framework to address this issue. A computer vision-based method was 
used to acquire the structural deformation information, enabling the 
capture of precise time-varying data pertaining to the vibrations occur- 
ring at the top of the building’s mast. Subsequently, a comprehensive 
digital twin model was established ( Fig. 14 ), encompassing the overall 
ﬁnite element model of the building, a reﬁned ﬁnite element model of 
the mast, a model representing the surrounding wind ﬁeld, and a ﬂuid–
solid coupling model for the mast. These numerical models facilitated 
ﬂuid mechanics and structural dynamics analyses, leading to the iden- 
tiﬁcation of the root causes of the abnormal building vibrations, that is, 
vortex-induced mast vibration due to wind and the resonance of higher- 
order vibration modes within the primary structure of the building. By 
evaluating various remedial scenarios within the virtual model, it was 
determined that the removal of the mast would mitigate the occurrence 
of wind-induced vibrations in the building. These ﬁndings provide valu- 
able guidelines for resolving such incidents.

6. Conclusion 
The construction of disaster-resilient cities has attracted consider- 
able attention. This paper focuses on three key aspects pertaining to the 
integration of the digital twin concept in research of building and city 
resilience. These aspects include the acquisition of data from reality to 
the virtual space, disaster simulation within the virtual space, and the 
translation of virtual insights into eﬀective disaster prevention strategies 
in reality. This paper provides a comprehensive summary of the rele- 
vant research endeavors of the authors’ research group and showcases 
their application eﬀectiveness and potential for development across var- 
ious stages, including pre-disaster planning, real-time emergency assess- 
ments, post-disaster rescue operations, and accident investigation. This 
paper oﬀers references for related research by presenting typical case 
studies. 
Moreover, digital twinning methodologies rely signiﬁcantly on com- 
prehensive city inventory datasets. Additionally, the intricate nature of 
interdependencies among disasters and interactions within urban areas 
presents considerable challenges when simulating disaster scenarios in 
densely populated regions. These factors impose limitations on the prac- 
tical implementation of digital twinning. Therefore, future research en- 
deavors should prioritize the exploration of eﬃcient and accurate meth- 
ods for acquiring urban information data. This endeavor necessitates 
considering the interdependencies among diﬀerent disasters to eﬀec- 
tively conduct disaster scenario simulations for buildings and cities that 
face multiple hazards. Furthermore, there is a critical need to enhance 
the utilization of digital twinning in disaster prevention and mitigation 
strategies and to employ it as a tool for identifying potential additional 
disaster types. 
Relevance to resilience 
This work addresses the critical applications of digital twin in re- 
search on building and urban resilience. The use of digital twin di- 
rectly contributes to an increase in the resilience of buildings and cities, 
demonstrating signiﬁcant eﬀectiveness in pre-disaster planning, real- 
time emergency assessments, and other aspects.
