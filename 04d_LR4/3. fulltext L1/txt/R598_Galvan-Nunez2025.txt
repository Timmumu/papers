Digital Twin Vision for Freight Railroads: A Case
Study in North America
Silvia Galván-Núñez
 , Ryan Alishio, and Christopher Johnson
Abstract— In recent years, the concept of “digital twins” (DTs)
has gained increasing attention in the railroad industry. This
complex concept allows the creation of digital representations
of physical assets or processes and can support a railroad’s
transportation system throughout its lifecycle. For decades,
MxV Rail has used the Facility for Accelerated Service Testing
(FAST (Registered trademark.) to enable research and testing
designed to improve the safety, reliability, and efficiency of North
American railroads. At FAST, data streams generated by 1)
operations, 2) maintenance, 3) wayside and onboard detector
technologies, and 4) individual testing are employed to better
understand the effects of heavy axle loads (HAL) as high tonnage
accumulates on track components at various stages of wear. The
development and implementation of DTs have strong potential
to support decision-making for infrastructure and mechanical
systems with regard to monitoring, predictive maintenance, and
knowledge transfer to conditions where data is unavailable. This
paper seeks to open the conversation by reviewing the concept of
DTs in the North American railroad industry and showing the
vision of the implementation layers for making a DT for FAST
and the current efforts to achieve this vision.
Index Terms— Digital engineering, digital twins, railroad
freight, FAST.
I. I NTRODUCTION
W
ITH
the growing interest and rapid advancements in
digital transformation, along with the drive to create
more value from both large and small data streams, the concept
of digital twins (DTs) has emerged as a focal point across
various industries.
Before diving into the concept of DTs, it is necessary
to introduce the concept of digital engineering (DE), which
is defined as a comprehensive approach that consolidates
information throughout the lifecycle of a megaproject. This
approach leverages digital information management systems
for designing, engineering, constructing, and maintaining
complex projects [1]. When implemented properly in the
aerospace, automotive, energy, and other industries, DE can
significantly improve the quality of services and achieve
notable cost reductions in these industries [1].
In addition to DE, DTs represent a concept with diverse
interpretations. With origins at the National Aeronautics and

Space Administration (NASA), a DT is defined by [2] as “a
set of virtual information constructs that fully describes a
potential or actual physical manufactured product from the
micro atomic level to the macro geometrical level. At its
optimum, any information that could be obtained from inspect-
ing a physical manufactured product can be obtained from
its digital twin.” Both DE and DT can be considered the
synthesis of established methods and processes, forming a
unified framework to improve system management across
various sectors. Whether the systems exist or are hypothetical,
this framework may integrate techniques such as physics-based
modeling, machine learning, and analytics to create cohesive
digital system replicas. While some researchers emphasize
the real-time visualization capabilities of DTs to distinguish
them from traditional modeling and simulation methods, other
researchers point to the creation of a virtual duplicate of a
physical object as the key innovation for a DT [3]. The concept
of DTs has been implemented across a variety of fields and
industries, including manufacturing [3], [4], automotive [5],
aerospace [6], [7], and energy [8], [9], among others.
Based on various applications and definitions found in
literature, the conceptualization of DTs has expanded from
representing only industrial objects or products to represent-
ing almost any physical or intangible object as well. Most
published work suggests that the definition of a DT may
vary depending on the application area. For instance, in the
initial product design phase, a physical object may not be
required for the creation of a DT, but it may be essential
for asset management. This perspective reinforces the need
for a clearly defined DT and invites the establishment of
common ground for different industries and application areas.
As presented later in this paper, the railroad industry has made
multiple efforts to define and apply the concept of DTs in the
context of structural health monitoring of track components,
asset management, and mechanical systems. However, as in
other sectors, there is still no unified concept, indicating the
possibility of ongoing opportunities for further exploration and
definition within the industry. The Facility for Accelerated
Service Testing (FAST ®) provides continuous monitoring,
research, and testing of track and mechanical systems for the
North American railroads and offers excellent opportunities to
explore the DT concept.

sation regarding exploration of DTs in the North American
railroad industry with the goal of defining DTs and 2) present
an initial attempt to create a DT for the FAST loop. The

II. L ITERATURE REVIEW
Recently, there has been a growing interest in applying DTs
within the railroad industry. This section provides a summary
of the literature on DTs in railroads. The review compiles peer-
reviewed articles, conference papers, and master’s theses that
include terms related to DTs, DE, and railroads in their titles,
abstracts, and keywords.
On a high level, [10] discusses how DTs can improve the
railroad industry by optimizing maintenance planning, design,
and operations. In addition, to improve risk analyses, real-
time asset health visualizations designed to decrease the time
needed to complete technical checks display accurate train
makeup to improve efficiency in transshipment preparation,
etc. Reference [11] presents a definition for DTs along with
an execution plan and the applications of the plan based on
stakeholders’ questions regarding railcar information. Refer-
ence [12] presents an overview of the current state of DT
in railroads, highlighting the fact that the emphasis has been
placed in the infrastructure side with limited applications in
the rolling stock side. Reference [12] also mentions that the
status of DT implementation is in the early stages, suggesting a
future focus on predictive analytics, lifecycle management, and
real-time performance monitoring. Reference [13] provides a
detailed literature review and the state of DTs and the railroad
industry.
On the mechanical side, [14] considers a real-time freight
car braking system designed to identify faults. This study uses
both physical and model-based information, employing com-
plex equations to replicate specific parts and integrating them
with specialized sensors to enhance accuracy. On the infras-
tructure side, [15] discusses opportunities for and challenges
in automatically generating geometric DTs for track struc-
tures. In terms of structural health monitoring, [16] provides
applications in bridge health, employing machine learning,
the Internet of Things, and cloud technology to enhance
monitoring, analysis, and decision-making for bridge main-
tenance. Another bridge-related study uses machine learning
and three-dimensional (3D) simulation to conduct continu-
ous online damage detection on bridges using train-induced
responses [17]. For rail yard monitoring, a DT was developed
to establish 1) optimal train paths, 2) train make-up, and 3) task
orders, with the authors using feedback and corrections from
controllers to fine-tune their models [18]. A 3D DT to monitor
the structure of railroad tunnels was also created [19]. Addi-
tionally, DT applications in track maintenance are explored in
a case study on the Passenger Rail Agency of South Africa
by [20], demonstrating the practicality and potential of DTs
in the railroad industry. Reference [21] depicts the application
of a DT to predict rail surface defects, such as rolling contact
fatigue in heavy haul operations, by integrating longitudinal
train simulation, multibody simulation, and traction modeling.
For grade crossing safety, there is an application for using a
DT focused on improving grade crossing safety, concentrating
on reducing waiting times for pedestrians and increasing train
speeds [22]. Additionally, applications in special trackwork
include the creation of a DT to monitor the temperature in
turnouts to improve defect detection and maintenance work
under different weather conditions [23]. Reference [24] covers
the development of a DT to monitor switch machine opera-
tions.
Reference [25] presents a DT prototype of a consist to aid
in the understanding of the derailment risks for autonomous
train operations (ATO) [25], and [26] presents the application
of a DT for the European Train Control System for traffic
management. For sustainability applications, [27] used the DT
concept to assess the sustainability metrics for train station
buildings, with a focus on energy efficiency and environmental
impact.
Based on the literature reviewed, it is apparent that the
usage of the term “digital twin” focuses on rail infrastruc-
ture, particularly the structural health monitoring of special
trackwork and bridges, with fewer mentions in the mechanical
and operational sectors. Most studies using DTs are centered
around case studies or remain in the hypothetical and prototype
stages, leaving the scalability of DTs not fully explored.
The reviewed literature also highlighted implementation chal-
lenges, such as data accuracy and automation. Despite these
challenges, the increasing interest within the railroad industry
is promising, opening up further opportunities for exploration
and application.
III. C ASE STUDY: THE FACILITY FOR ACCELERATED
SERVICE TESTING
A. F AST Overview
MxV Rail, a wholly owned subsidiary of the Associa-
tion of American Railroads (AAR), operates FAST as part
of the AAR Strategic Research Initiatives (SRI) program.
Since operations began in 1976, FAST has evolved into an
industry-leading facility for testing railroad infrastructure and
mechanical components, maintenance practices, and inspection
technologies [28], [29]. FAST focuses on understanding the
effects of high tonnage and heavy axle loads on accelerated
track degradation and mitigation strategies, as well as inves-
tigating the root causes of events to ultimately help improve
track and mechanical component performance to reduce risk
and increase safety.
At the FAST loop, testing is conducted with an 18,000-
ton train of cars loaded to 315,000 pounds, operating in a
highly controlled environment while accumulating tonnage
of approximately 140 million gross tons (MGT) annually,
exceeding that of all but the busiest Class 1 main lines (Fig.1).
In 2023, the new FAST loop was built at PuebloPlex in Pueblo,
Colorado, United States, and it has been fully operational
since November 2023. Spanning nearly 16,000 acres, Pueblo-
Plex is a planned redevelopment of the soon-to-be-former
Army Depot. This project will incorporate industries including
manufacturing, warehousing, and distribution operations, and
rail-related business, among others [30]. The new FAST loop

B. F AST Data Management
The in-house FAST Internal Records for Statistics and
Testing (FIRST) system was designed and developed as part
of the initiative to enhance the data collection, storage, and
reporting of infrastructure and mechanical system inspections,
maintenance, and tests at FAST. This system was modeled
using a framework similar to what is typically used for the
North American railroads’ data management systems and
tailored to meet the specific needs of FAST, enabling robust
research and testing for North American railroads. Data is
gathered from various sources, including wayside detectors,
such as hot bearing detectors (HBD), hot wheel detectors
(HWD), dragging equipment detectors (DED), truck perfor-
mance detectors (TPD), automatic equipment identification
(AEI) detectors; train operations; tests and measurements
scheduling; and rail temperature, etc.
The data collection cycle within the FIRST system begins
with test planning before progressing through train operations
and the inspection and maintenance of infrastructure and
mechanical components. Throughout this process, the tonnage
and mileage data are accumulated for both the track and
mechanical components. After the process is complete, the
system generates reports tailored for various organizational
levels.
IV. D IGITAL TWINS VISION FOR FAST
As mentioned in Section II, FAST provides substantial
research and testing support for North American railroads.
Fig. 2. Digital twin implementation levels and layers for FAST.
Given the diversity of the available data stream sources and
the continuous monitoring of the data stream, it is logical
to establish a vision for developing DTs for FAST. This
initiative is motivated by the attainment of various benefits,
such as long-term improvements in train operations, safety,
and sustainability. Additionally, DTs can enhance research
capabilities through data integration and modeling and aid
in the lifecycle management of infrastructure and mechanical
components. Ultimately, this effort can also enhance data-
driven decision-making.
There are different ways to present the DT implementation
pathway. In this paper, the authors present simple, yet com-
plex, implementation levels for DTs. Level 1, mirroring, dupli-
cates a physical object or process as a DT. Level 2, monitoring,
is the process of observing and controlling the physical object
based on the DT. Level 3, modeling and simulation, involves
improving the physical object using 3D modeling, machine-
learning models, and other models. Level 4, federation, refers
to connecting or merging multiple DTs. For example, in com-
plex systems, such as railroads, multiple DTs are needed to
represent part of the system. Level 5, autonomous, refers to
the full automation of the DT. Achieving all levels depends
on the specific application, e.g., a DT can make it to the
modeling and simulation level (level 3) and may not need to
get to the federation and/or autonomous levels (levels 4 and
5). To achieve the DT vision for FAST, the authors propose
the following implementation levels and layers (Fig. 2).
A. Level 1: Mirroring
There is an interest in building DTs to enhance FAST
inspection, maintenance, and research. The physical layer,

could benefit from DTs include special trackwork components,
such as insulated joints and OWLS crossings and switches,
as well as bridges, ties, fasteners, and rails, etc. On the
mechanical side, the physical objects might be comprised of
wheelsets, bearings, and more. These components will be mon-
itored and inspected using data collection systems that may
include wayside and onboard detectors, visual inspections,
maintenance activities, and testing.
B. Level 2: Monitoring
The monitoring level refers to processing, fusing, storing,
and visualizing the data streams associated with the physical
objects mirrored by DTs. This step is crucial and challenging,
as data sets gathered from multiple sources must be integrated
and stored in a way that allows them to be used collectively
for analysis. Additionally, the data must enable the generation
of visualizations and reports that are actionable at various
organizational levels, such as the operations, research and
development, and engineering teams that support FAST.
C. Level 3: Modeling & Simulation
The modeling and simulation level involves developing
models to 1) enhance the understanding of physical object
behavior, 2) support research capabilities, and 3) ensure the
safety of train operations. Depending on the specific questions
that need to be addressed regarding the physical object,
these models may incorporate machine-learning algorithms,
finite element analysis, multi-body dynamic simulations, and
traditional statistics.
D. Level 4: Federation
Railroads are complex systems, and creating a DT to repre-
sent entire railroad networks is not feasible. This complexity
also applies to FAST. Therefore, to achieve comprehensive
coverage of all operations around the loop, it is necessary
to create and interconnect multiple DTs while enforcing
cybersecurity protocols to ensure the privacy and security of
the collected data. Although the Security and Privacy layer
is introduced in Level 4, it is crucial to emphasize that
cybersecurity must be considered at every stage of the DTs’
design, development, and deployment.
V. P RELIMINARY RESULTS
Regarding the current implementation stage of a DT for
FAST, the focus has been on improving the quality and
accuracy of the models to ensure they reflect the physical
reality of the track and mechanical components tested. This
effort has involved refining the algorithms that process opera-
tional/maintenance data and improving the system’s real-time
monitoring capabilities. These improvements have been instru-
mental in advancing the development of the DT, particularly
in terms of mirroring and monitoring, Level 1 and Level 2,
respectively, of the DT implementation.

Continuous monitoring for track and mechanical systems
is operational alongside the data collection systems, such as
the FIRST system. As described in Section IV, the physical
layer exists from the FAST loop, the locomotives and cars,
wayside detection systems, etc. Track side inspection data
includes non-destructive evaluation (NDE) for rail and welds,
visual track inspection, rail break detector, and rail profile
measurement data, among others. As an example of the
track side inspection systems, the NDE for rail and welds
allows the identification of internal defects, and, depending
on the condition, the defect can be removed from the track
immediately or monitored over time for growth and possible
removal in the future. Depending on the type of defect, the
growth rate is calculated and plotted against the tonnage of
that defect in track, measured in MGT. Fig. 3 provides an
example of using rail wear data for inspections at FAST[31]—
the image on the left presents the typical wear pattern on the
low rail in a 5-degree unlubricated curve, and the image on the
right shows the wear pattern on the high rail. The blue lines
on the rail profile illustrate multiple wear conditions between
0 and 654 MGT.
The data to support the rail wear analysis above come from
multiple sources, including, but not limited to, the recording
of track maintenance work done through the FIRST system.
Fig. 4 presents an example for some of the maintenance
summary screens at a tie level. Train operations data is also as
important for this analysis to calculate the MGT accumulation,
and this data is also tracked in the FIRST system.
On the mechanical side, inspection data includes TPD,
HBD, HWD, DED, NDE for wheelsets, and visual inspection
of cars and locomotives, among others. For example, TPD
data provides near real-time information about the vertical (V)
and lateral wheel/rail contact forces (L), L/V ratio, alongside
HBD data that can provide insights regarding the condition of
the wheelset and bearings so maintenance can be prioritized.

of the FAST operation and the value the component/object
adds to ongoing research and testing.
VI. C ONCLUSION
The increasing interest in DTs within the railroad indus-
try presents numerous opportunities for further research and
practical applications, especially through initiatives like FAST.
While challenges to achieving the full predictive capabilities
of the DT remain, the groundwork laid in Level 1 and
Level 2 provides a solid foundation for future improvements.
Continued efforts to enhance the accuracy and functionality of
DTs will be critical in supporting more advanced applications,
such as predictive maintenance, and will ultimately contribute
to the resilience and efficiency of railroad operations. However,
for these technologies to be effectively integrated and used,
it will be crucial to establish a unified definition for DTs in the
railroad sector or at least within specific areas of application in
the railroad industry. Additionally, addressing implementation
challenges, including security and interoperability issues, will
be essential to realizing the full potential of DTs in enhancing
railroad infrastructure and operations.
