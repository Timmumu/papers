Analyzing Safety Risk Imposed by Jobsite Debris to
Nearby Built Environments Using Geometric Digital
Twins and Vision-Based Deep Learning
Mirsalar Kamari, S.M.ASCE 1; Jaeyoon Kim, S.M.ASCE 2; and Youngjib Ham, Ph.D., A.M.ASCE 3
Abstract: Extreme wind events can pick up loose and small objects on the ground, and once the objects become airborne, they negatively
impact surrounding communities due to the collision impact. In this regard, jobsites and laydown yards that involve construction materials
such as gravel piles and crushed rocks could be the main sources of potential windborne debris during extreme wind events. To analyze safety
risk and predict the damage imposed by jobsite debris to nearby built environments, a new computer vision-based risk assessment based on
geometric digital twins of jobsite debris is proposed for the reliability analysis on glazing systems of dwellings located on nearby jobsites.
The impact of a gravel pile in a railroad jobsite on nearby buildings and residential environments was studied based on extreme wind event
scenarios, and the failure risk of the building glazing system was computed. The risk associated with jobsite debris during extreme wind
events and their impact on neighboring communities are analyzed through three computing modules: (1) satellite imagery-based terrain
modeling to study 3D characteristics of the at-risk built environment; (2) analyzing visual data from Google Street View to assess the risk
associated with glazing panels of dwellings in the communities; and (3) analyzing visual data from a jobsite to quantify the impact of jobsite
debris, to associate its safety risk to neighboring communities. The proposed method can provide an immediate heads up for those who reside
nearby jobsites, allowing to take required preemptive actions to protect their habitation against potential windborne debris. Practitioners
will also be informed of such jobsite debris-related risk before extreme wind events to better secure their jobsites for the risk mitigation.

Introduction
Natural disasters such as hurricanes impose a great risk to the built
environment, causing billions of dollars worth in damage each year.
The primary victims are the communities and neighborhoods that
are woefully unprepared for the disasters. For example, hurricane
damages can range from structural damages ( Pinelli et al. 2004 )t o
flooding and inundation due to heavy rain ( Alizadeh and Behzadan
2021). Particularly, the majority of damage in extreme wind events
are imposed due to the collision impact of potential windborne
debris (PWDs) ( Beason et al. 1984 ; Doddipatla and Kopp 2019 ;
Vickery et al. 2006 ). As a poignant example, the aftermath of
Hurricane Alicia imposed substantial window glass damage in the
downtown Houston area (Beason et al. 1984 ). On-site investigation
identified that windborne missiles (e.g., gravel) substantially con-
tributed to the amount of glazing damage and window breakage,
especially to high-rise towers ( Beason et al. 1984 ). The gravels in
Hurricane Alicia were sourced from roofs of nearby buildings, and
caused damage to more than 5,700 glass panes that were eventually
replaced ( Beason et al. 1984 ). To minimize the risk of such air-
borne PWDs during extreme wind events, the Federal Emergency
Management Agency (FEMA) of the United States Department of
Homeland Security has leveraged a physical-based risk model to
quantify the risk of glazing damage for buildings and residential
environments, and the model has been used to carry out the reli-
ability analysis for glazing systems that are impacted by PWDs
(FEMA 2021 ).
Jobsites generally involve incomplete and unstructured resour-
ces that are susceptible to extreme wind events. In this regard, prior
works such as Chavez ( 2016), Ham et al. ( 2017), Kamari and Ham
(2021a, b), Kareem ( 1985), and Li and Ellingwood ( 2006) classi-
fied construction sites among the most vulnerable environments to
extreme wind events such as hurricanes. Jobsites and laydown
yards are used to temporarily store unsecured materials such as
gravel pits, crushed rocks, and metal piping rods, that may become
airborne during severe wind events, which may lead to substantial
damage to neighboring environments. According to a report by the
Department of Homeland Security ( SunSentinel 2017 ), the after-
math of Hurricane Irma left more than 200,000 cubic yards of
debris, which were primarily classified into construction-related
materials and vegetation. The overall cost of debris removal was
estimated as 1.4 billion dollars because the debris hauling process
typically takes over several months to complete. To mitigate the
impact of extreme wind events, state governments located in
hurricane-prone areas have enacted ordinances that mandate super-
intendents, contractors, and safety directors to take proactive mea-
sures regarding hurricane preparedness in their jobsites ( Bohm
2020). Disobedient contractors failing to implement proper mea-
sures to identify PWDs and secure them, are subject to fines and
imprisonment of up to 60 days ( Bohm 2020 ). For implementing
such policies, hazard recognition in large-scale jobsites still depend
on field observation by workers to recognize relevant hazards
amidst irrelevant distractors ( Jeelani et al. 2017 ). In this regard,
despite substantial safety training programs, it has been known that

about the jobsite debris-related risk is limited, and there is need for
the reliability analysis of buildings and residential environments
threatened by PWDs sourced from nearby jobsites.
In this paper, a novel computing method is proposed to assess
the risk of PWDs in jobsites with respect to adjacent buildings in
neighboring communities. In the proposed method, visual data ana-
lytics are performed based on virtual representation of communities
and jobsite debris, and the following computing modules are pro-
posed: (1) satellite imagery-based terrain modeling to study 3D
characteristics of the at-risk built environment; (2) analyzing visual
data from Google Street View to assess the risk associated with
glazing panels of dwellings in the communities; and (3) analyzing
visual data from a jobsite to quantify the amount of PWDs such as
gravel, to associate its risk to neighboring communities. In this re-
gard, by using images collected from jobsites, a 3D point cloud
model is reconstructed, and PWDs (gravels in this paper) are auto-
matically detected from the point cloud. Building on the semantic
segmentation, vulnerable openings such as windows are analyzed
in Google Street View images collected from built environment
nearby jobsites, and the failure probability of glazing systems are
computed building on the physical-based risk model by ( FEMA
2021). Cases studies were performed to demonstrate the risk
assessment of communities located nearby a railroad jobsite. The
proposed framework has the potential to provide a heads-up for
residents and communities that will be exposed to jobsite debris
during extreme wind events. The residents impacted by PWDs from
jobsites could take preemptive measures to mitigate the loss, which
support establishing community resilience during extreme wind
events.
Background
Visual Data-Driven Disaster Management
In past years, the world has experienced a record number of natural
disasters, including hurricanes that caused billions of dollars in
damage. In this regard, studies have been carried out toward pro-
active disaster management measures to reduce the cost and mit-
igate the loss imposed by such disasters. Generally, it has been
known that every two or three dollars investment in disaster man-
agement could lead to up to one hundred dollars saved due to the
disaster loss reductions (Pheng et al. 2006). The focus of prior works
on disaster management are divided into pre- and postdisaster
responses. The focus of predisaster management is primarily placed
on adopting proactive measures to mitigate the impact of disasters,
and the essence of postdisaster activities is set to implement the
damage assessment to better plan recovery measures ( Alizadeh
et al. 2021). To rapidly assess the severity of damage after disasters
such as hurricanes, remote sensing solutions, including ground/
aerial images and 3D laser scanners, would be one of time- and
cost-effective practices. In this regard, research efforts implement
remote sensing techniques to perform postdisaster damage assess-
ment ( Guo 2010 ; Kashani et al. 2015 ; Olsen et al. 2013 ; Pesaresi
et al. 2007 ; Tralli et al. 2005 ; Wang et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2002 ).
For instance, Li et al. ( 2008) and Ural et al. ( 2011) applied the
LiDAR to measure landmark variances among pre- and posthurri-
cane and tornadoes. In addition, aerial imagery captured in high
(Vijayaraj et al. 2008 ), medium ( Womble et al. 2006 ), and low
(Wang and Xu 2010 ) spatial resolutions has been utilized for
the damage assessment. Contextual information obtained from
imagery was used to measure the normalized difference in vegeta-
tion index (NDVI) and assess damaged areas ( Ill et al. 1997 ; Liou
et al. 2010 ). To implement mitigation measures to reduce disaster
risk in jobsites, it is required that practitioners such as safety man-
agers accurately identify the hidden risk in a timely manner ( Carter
and Smith 2006 ). In this regard, eye-tracking technology has been
proposed to study the hazard cognition in jobsites ( Asadi et al.
2017; Jeelani et al. 2019 ). The focus of these works is placed on
assessing the quality of hazard perception by practitioners and bet-
ter training them to more effectively flag and analyze the extent of
potential hazards and the associated threats. Due to the complexity
of hazard analysis related to PWDs at different severity of wind
events, they would not translate into sensible outcomes because
factors such as physical and geometrical shape, unit-weight and
wind speed can influence the debris behaviors and contribute to the
complexity of the associated risk assessment ( Wills et al. 2002 ).
Recently, Kamari and Ham ( 2022) proposed the kinetic energy-
driven risk assessment and visual data analytics using a deep learn-
ing technique to quantify the debris risk with respect to extreme
wind events. Despite the potential in the context of disaster prepar-
edness, the direction and the scope of the prior study was limited to
given jobsites, and thus the vulnerability of nearby built environ-
ments including buildings and residential environments impacted
by jobsite debris could not be encoded into the model.
Physical-Based Risk Assessment Models
Once objects (e.g., gravel) become airborne, they are transported in
the three-dimensional wind field. The airborne grains will eventu-
ally collide with the ground or building façades and potentially
cause a breach in the glazing systems. Modeling the behavior of
such PWDs and predicting their potential impact have been studied
for past years. In this regard, Kind and Wardlaw ( 1984) performed
a wind tunnel experiment to investigate gravel scour patterns from
rooftops that are due to spiral vortex flow. Later, Wills et al. ( 2002)
classified the debris behavior, based on their physical shapes,
into the three categories of compact-, rod-, and plate-type debris,
and possible trajectories of the rod- and plate-type debris during
severe wind events were studied by Holmes (
2004) and Holmes
et al. ( 2006). As such, there have been efforts to document the ex-
tent of damage to the glazing system, which was later used as a
basis for examining windborne debris trajectory and damage mod-
els. For instance, during Hurricane Andrew, a high-rise complex
building located in Kendall, in the state of Florida, suffered severe
damage imposed by airborne gravel, and Behr and Minor ( 1994),
Minor ( 1994), and T. Smith, personal communication, (1999)
recorded photographs of glazing system damage. Later, these pho-
tos were used as a benchmark to assess the fidelity of the HAZUS
debris model to estimate loss and damage to the glazing system
(FEMA 2021 ). The HAZUS model on airborne gravel risk to

Methodology
The computer vision-based reliability analysis in this paper builds
upon the HAZUS model ( FEMA 2021 ) by taking account of:
(1) the amount of the source (i.e., windborne debris such as grav-
els), (2) the distance between the source and target buildings,
(3) the windows of the target buildings, and (4) the wind speed.
To carry out the risk analysis on the glazing system of buildings
with respect to debris in nearby jobsites, the proposed framework
consists of three computing modules, each with a particular type
of visual data input (Fig. 1). First, building on satellite imagery,
the terrain roughness is computed for the at-risk built environment,
and the wind intensity in the given area is adjusted based on the
terrain roughness. Second, images from façades of target buildings
are collected from Google Street View, and the portion of the build-
ings occupied by the glazing is computed through the deep
learning-based approach. Third, visual data from a jobsite is proc-
essed to quantify the volume of potential windborne debris (PWDs)
in the jobsite (gravels in this paper). Given the debris volume, the
number of expected debris impacts that are initiated from the job-
site is computed for each building, and building upon the fragility
properties of the glazing systems, the associated reliability informa-
tion (i.e., the probability of no damage ) is computed as follows
(FEMA 2021 ):

Processing

at the immediate vicinity of the ground surface, but the intensity at
the upper level remains almost the same. This is because vertical
barriers (e.g., buildings, trees) are expected to dissipate the wind
intensity in rough terrains. For example, the wind intensity expe-
rienced by two-story dwellings located in the middle of the forest
might be as low as half of the wind intensity encountered by the
same dwellings located in an open environment ( FEMA 2021). As
such, the density of trees and buildings needs to be taken into ac-
count for assessing the terrain roughness. Building upon Lettau ’s
equation ( Lettau 1969 ), the terrain roughness can be quantified as

Analyzing Building Façades with Respect to Glazing
Panels
The glazing system is among the most fragile component of dwell-
ings becauses they can break due to the collision impact of PWDs.
In this sense, buildings with larger openings and façades that are
built from tempered glasses are more susceptible to break down
during extreme wind events. In this regard, one of the key param-
eters in assessing the risk of glazing damage during extreme wind
events is to quantify the portion of given buildings that are occu-
pied by glass openings. In this research, we leverage publicly avail-
able large-scale visual data collected from Google Street View. To
retrieve the image of building façades in given regions, an API
request is established through an HTTP request in Google Street
View (Balali et al. 2015 ; Chen et al. 2019 ; Kim et al. 2021 ). Multi-
ple images from different perspectives are retrieved for target build-
ings [Fig. 4(a)]. The semantic segmentation is then performed, and
the images with the best line of sight of the buildings are chosen
for further processing. The outcome of semantic segmentation is
studied among collected images, and the image containing the
greater number of pixels classified under building and windows
categories is selected to represent the best line of sight [Fig. 4(a)].
The semantic segmentation is carried out by a residual deep neural
network, Resnet 50 ( He et al. 2016 ; Thorpe and van Gennip 2018 ).
The Resnet architectures demonstrate high performance in terms
of accuracy and computation cost. Unlike other deep neural net-
work architectures, the residual networks are robust for optimizing
weights and generally gain accuracy upon increasing network
layers. The size of residual networks is generally determined com-
mensurate with the accessible computation power ( He et al. 2016 ;
Thorpe and van Gennip 2018 ). To train the Resnet network, we
build upon the following four classes: building, windows, possible
occlusion, and others. The class of possible occlusion consists of
trees and cars that might occlude the line of sight to building fa-
çades. The other category involves pixels associated with sky,

Fragility Curves of Glazing Systems of Buildings
Exploring the characteristics of the glazing system is essential in
damage prediction in extreme wind events. A dwelling with fragile
glass is subjected to a higher failure risk than the one with tougher
properties. In this research, building on the experimental outcomes
of Dharani et al. ( 2004), the fragility of glass is analyzed based on
laminated glass at different impact momentum. Laminated glass is a
durable glazing system because it holds together once shattered. The
profile of the specimen consists of outer glass ply, polyvinyl butyral
interlayer, and inner glass ply. The glass specimen is subjected to
2 (g) steel balls at the speed of 20–50 m=s. Here, it is intended
the small steel ball represents the gravel projectiles ( Dharani
et al. 2004). For each launch speed, the cumulative probability of the
breakage is analyzed to obtain the fragility curve of the specimen
(Fig. 7). The prior work ( Dharani et al. 2004 ) experimentally found
that when it comes to 2 g projectiles (gravel grain), wind speeds such
as 25.9, 33.5, and 42.7 m=s generate 0.052, 0.067, and 0.085 kg ·
m=s impact momentum. Building on the experimental outcomes
from Dharani et al. (2004), failure probabilities at these wind speeds
are 0.11, 0.47, and 0.94, respectively. In this research, given a wind
speed and the weight of the gravel grain, the momentum of impact is
computed, and the failure probability of the glazing window system
Pðξ < ξdÞ is obtained from this failure probability information at
different impact momentum. The parameter ξ is the impact energy
or momentum of the projectile, and ξd denotes the damage momen-
tum threshold value.

to as the HAZUS model, to predict the risk of the glazing system

damage during extreme wind events ( FEMA 2021 ). The HAZUS
model provides a reliable and robust framework to estimate the se-
verity of loss in the glazing system with respect to the wind speed
(FEMA 2021). There are assumptions in the HAZUS risk model to
assess glazing system damage. For example, (1) the wind direction
is persistent and is not changed during the wind event, (2) the prop-
erty of the windows is considered to be uniform among housing,
and (3) airborne gravels are uniform in size and mass. In this re-
search, visual analytics was integrated with the HAZUS model to
quantify the risk imposed by jobsite debris in glazing systems of the
nearby housing. Despite its benefits in providing estimates of the
potential wind-induced damage, the HAZUS model in the proposed
work needs to be further discussed as follows.
Surface Roughness Estimation
An accurate estimate of the surface roughness of a landscape
substantially contributes to effectively predicting wind-induced
damage and loss. The Lettau ’s equation offers a means to quanti-
tatively predict surface roughness from satellite images. However,
in accordance with the FEMA recommendation, the use of Lettau ’s
equation is subjected to a thorough engineering judgment in
approximating the height of buildings and trees ( FEMA 2021 ).
In this regard, significant uncertainties might exist in estimating
surface roughness in terrains where trees are the major obstacles.
In this research, we assume the number of floors of buildings in the
case study area to obtain the common building height. Accordingly,
the height of the trees was estimated relative to the building heights
that are presented in the case study area. In practice, similar inves-
tigations need to be conducted to satisfy the FEMA ’s recommen-
dation to quantify the height and frontal areas of the buildings
and trees.
Challenges in Vision-Based Building Façade Analysis
Although visual data collection from all sides of building façades
has an indisputable advantage in the context of the completeness of
the risk analysis, it is not trivial to leverage such data for the given
regions and neighborhoods at scale. To broaden the impact and
applicability of the proposed vision-based risk analysis at scale,
visual analytics of buildings in the proposed method is limited
to the part of the façade that is facing the street due to the nature
of visual data collection building on Google Street View, similar to
the prior work for assessing the seismic vulnerability of masonry
walls using images collected from the street nearby buildings
(Wang et al. 2021 ). It is assumed that the frontal façade facing
the street involves a large portion occupied by the glazing system,
which is likely vulnerable to extreme wind events that needs to be
analyzed in this module.
Challenges in Evaluating the Risk Model
Although the proposed probabilistic approach can provide situa-
tional awareness of how jobsite debris could affect nearby built
environments in extreme wind events, further research to validate
the fragility analysis with real disaster cases will help compare the
analytical results and real data. Although the proposed framework
based on the FEMA’s risk model is expected to reliably predict the
imposed damage to the glazing system, there are still challenges
in evaluating FEMA ’s risk model in real-world scenarios. For in-
stance, factors such as the volume of jobsite windborne debris and
distance between debris and target buildings are closely relevant to
the potential damage to the glazing system. However, the locations
of specific debris are not typically identified, and their volumes are
not proactively quantified in real-world wind events. Lack of such
information (e.g., volume, type, and location of debris) poses an
immediate challenge to properly associate the observed damage
with the volume/location of specific debris, and compare real dam-
ages with prediction outcomes. In addition, FEMA ’s probabilistic
risk model may require large-scale observations for validation and
potential mathematical refinement. In this regard, the challenge re-
garding the size of data to effectively evaluate the FEMA model
based on real-world scenarios has been presented, featured, and
highlighted by prior studies such as Johnson and Mahoney ( 2011).
Further research to validate the findings with real disaster cases
will discuss: (1) what are the required data to investigate the cause
of failures of glazing systems of buildings nearby jobsites in ex-
treme wind events?; and (2) how can we robustly collect such data
at-scale?
Conclusion
The aftermath of extreme wind events such as hurricanes leaves
behind billions of dollars in damage every year. Whereas structural
damage and inundations are common occurrences, the majority of
the damage is imposed by the collision impact of windborne debris.
To this end, jobsites having gravel piles and aggregates are classi-
fied among the most hazardous environments to neighboring com-
munities. During extreme wind events, windborne debris from
jobsites can impose a devastating effect on nearby buildings and
their associated glazing systems such as windows and glass open-
ings. For the associated reliability analysis, a novel vision-based
computing method is presented in this paper. The proposed method
builds upon visual data at three fronts: (1) satellite images collected
through Google Maps from the at-risk built environment threatened
by jobsite debris, (2) ground-level images of building windows and
glazing systems that are collected through Google Street View, and
(3) visual data collected using UA Vs from jobsites for geometric
digital twinning to quantify the volume and impacts of jobsite
debris. Being able to identify the hidden risk of construction ma-
terials imposed on nearby communities would allow practitioners
to better secure their jobsites before extreme wind events and thus
help mitigate the damage and loss during extreme wind events.
Despite the benefits of the proposed method in the context of dis-
aster preparedness, there are still open research challenges as fol-
lows: (1) ground-level images collected from building facades
could be occluded by cars and trees, which may limit properly
study the condition of building glazing systems; and (2) the digital
twin framework to assess jobsite debris through aerial imagery
might demonstrate poor performance in suboptimal weather con-
ditions such as rain ( Porav et al. 2019 ) or fog ( Sakaridis et al.
2018). The aforementioned challenges are currently being explored
as part of our ongoing research.
