1. Introduction
Dam projects have created huge economic and social bene-
fits for human beings throughout history, especially in water
supply, flood control, and renewable energy production
\(Huang & Yan,
\ However, most as-built
dams suffer from safety-related issues \(e.g., excessive
deformation \(Chen, Gu, Bao, Wu, & Su,
\ adverse
seepage \(Jiang et al.,
\ slope instability \(Martt,
etc.\ due to the combined influence of many reasons such
as increasing natural hazard risks, aging of existing infra-
structure, lack of experience in dam safety management and
operations, among others. These emerging safety issues may
lead to potential dam accidents and further cause adverse
impacts on the life, property, and environment of down-
stream areas, if they are not detected and resolved in time.
A comprehensive evaluation of dam service behaviour is the
most important and scientific way to monitor dam oper-
ation condition and diagnose potential problems \(Wu, Xu,
Gu, & Li,
Recently, with the increasing awareness of risk prevention
and control in the field of dam engineering, some research
has demonstrated the necessity of the transformation of
comprehensive dam evaluation mode from the traditional
static and passive one to a dynamic and proactive one
\(Jeon, Lee, Shin, & Park,
; Supakchukul et al.,
Wu & Su,
\ Its initiative aims to maintain dynamic
information, support continuous condition monitoring of
dam structures, achieve efficient decision-making, and
finally minimise the risk of dam failure. The new mode of
dam safety evaluation is of vital significance for diagnosing
potential problems and taking reinforcement measures
timely especially when some unpredicted catastrophic floods
are coming or a united safety management of dam groups
in the same river basin is required \(Huang, Li, Hu, Zhang,
& Zhao,
; Li, Bao, & Fang,
However, in the traditional static mode, measured data
that contains diagnostic information is read from different
kinds of instruments or sensors and stored in one or several
databases \(Li & Wang,
\ while detailed information
\(e.g., monitoring precision and frequency, correction param-
eters and methods\ of instruments are hidden in the
instruction manuals and location information are presented
in the monitoring instrument layout \(usually a set of design
drawings\ The information related to dam safety monitor-
ing is usually saved in different formats and separately
stored in different management systems. In view of this,
dam engineers may require significant time and effort to
query and coordinate all necessary information for evalu-
ation purposes. Hence the current distributed and informal
information management status in dam engineering cannot

2.2. Review of comprehensive evaluation of
dam behaviour
The evaluation methods of dam behaviour can be classified
into two categories: physical simulation analysis and data-
driven methods. Physical simulation analysis mainly lever-
ages finite element method \(FEM\ to calculate structural
responses, with model parameters being calibrated using
monitoring data \(Colombo, Domaneschi, & Ghisi,
Data-driven methods refer to the data fusion, modelling,
and analysis of massive information to mine useful informa-
tion \(Su, Wen, Chen, & Tian,
\ Among them, compre-
hensive evaluation of dam behaviour using multiple
monitoring-related information is always a popular topic
because of its significance to ensure dam safety during the
O&M stage.
As shown in
Figure 1
, a complete evaluation index sys-
tem is a multi-index and multi-layer system that includes
well-organised and preprocessed monitoring data \(mainly
the monitored dam behaviour, such as stress, deformation,
seepage, etc.\ The comprehensive dam evaluation should be
implemented from left to right. Hence, the reasonable and
effective evaluation of each measuring point is the basis to
ensure accuracy of the entire comprehensive evaluation pro-
gram. After deriving the baseline of each monitoring point,
the remaining challenge is to reason from a quantitative
value to a qualitative index. For each monitoring point, the
quantitative value is the difference between the baseline and
monitored effect variable, and the qualitative index is an
evaluation grade that indicates the safety status of this mon-
itoring point, such as normal, abnormal, and so on.
Most existing research utilises the concept of fuzzy sets
to evaluate safety status of monitoring points \(Shu, Bao, Li,
Zhang, & Wu,
; W u e t al.,
\ In these solutions,
evaluation grades are quantified by fixed boundary condi-
tions based on human experience, and functions of
membership degree are curves with fixed parameters.
Hence, the way to obtain safety status of monitoring points
is deterministic. However, the truth is, ambiguity inevitably
exists in the evaluation grades \(Jiang & Fan,
\ and
monitoring data collected from monitoring instruments is
always discrete and random because of some unpredictable
factors \(Zhang & Li,
\ Thus, these existing methods
cannot simultaneously characterise the ambiguity and ran-
domness in the evaluation process of monitoring points.
Recently, several studies have adopted the cloud model
concept to solve the above issue. For example, Jiang and
Zhang \(
\ established nine cloud models to describe the
quantitative judgement matrix in the AHP index system. Jia
et al. \(
\ adopt cloud model to determine the basic prob-
ability assignment of the Dempster-Shafer theory. In these
studies, evaluation grades are characterised using cloud
models. But the uncertainties in the reasoning process from
difference values to evaluation grades are ignored.
Meanwhile, weight values of evaluation indices are usually
determined using some subjective methods \(Luo et al.,
\ There is no denying that these methods have advan-
tages in the aspect of rich human experience, but they are
both labour-intensive and time-consuming when it comes to
continuous condition evaluation of whole dam structures.
Hence, an automated evaluation model that can fully con-
sider these uncertainties requires further study.
3. The IFC-based data integration for
comprehensive dam evaluation
3.1. Information requirements for comprehensive
evaluation of dam behaviour
Dam safety monitoring through instrumentation is the most
important way to indicate dam service condition because
monitoring data collected from a comprehensive monitoring
system contains adequate diagnostic information which can
be utilised to detect and identify abnormalities of dam
behaviour. Hence, a standardised description of DSM sys-
tems is of vital importance to ensure correct classification
and utilisation of various monitoring-related information.
Currently, due to insufficient understanding of the
impact of multiple factors on dam safety, such as natural
forces, structural mechanism, material properties, construc-
tion controls, and human behaviour, various types of dam
incidents like leakage, landslide, overtopping, etc. are taking
place now and then. Also, a dam is an integrated structure
including the main hydraulic structure \(dam body\ dam
foundation and abutments, blank slopes, reservoirs, and so
on, incidents happened in any structure may cause dam fail-
ure. With these considerations, a systematic and complete
DSM system demands comprehensive monitoring of all pos-
sible hazards in all related structures or areas.
Hence, to describe essential characteristics of monitored
physical quantities, interpret dam structural behaviour, and
implement monitoring-related tasks, scientific and reason-
able classification of monitoring items is necessary. For
example, the key monitoring items of concrete dams, pre-
scribed in the Design Specification for Safety Monitoring in

ing items in DSM systems are mainly divided into four cate-
gories, and each monitoring category implements specific
monitoring task, which is completely different from others
in this system. For instance, deformation monitoring is
designed to detect shape changes of dam structures, while
seepage monitoring focuses on observing the impact of
groundwater movement in dams.
On the other hand, a monitoring category is composed
of several monitoring items, because changes caused by
external factors cannot be reflected in a single measurable
variable. For instance, deformation is a comprehensive con-
cept to describe shape changes of dams, but there is no vari-
able that can be directly measured to characterise the extent
of dam deformation. In view of this, deformation monitor-
ing items, such as horizontal displacement, vertical displace-
ment monitoring, and inclination, are designed to measure
specific physical quantities pertaining to dam deformation
from several different aspects. Using this intuitive and clear
classification, instrumental monitoring-related information
in the DSM system can be described in a meaningful and
systematical manner.
As the most direct carrier for various monitoring data,
monitoring instruments also contain a lot of useful informa-
tion for preprocessing raw measured data. In current prac-
tice, monitoring instruments installed in a DSM system can
be generally divided into two types: embedded monitoring
instruments and external-mounted monitoring instruments
\(Shen & Mao,
\ Embedded monitoring instruments
refer to sensors embedded in hydraulic structures, which are
mainly used to monitor physical quantities related to dam
structures, such as strain, crack openings, seepage pressure,
and so on. These instruments \(e.g., strain gauge, osmometer,
flow meter, and thermometer\ are single-type monitoring
instruments, that means they can be used to measure the
relevant physical quantities independently without the aid of
other facilities.
On the other hand, external-mounted monitoring instru-
ments are usually installed on the surface of hydraulic struc-
tures, observation galleries, or bank slope to measure
structural deformation, seepage, and environmental varia-
bles. This type of monitoring instruments, such as tensile-
wire instruments, hydrostatic level instruments, and plumb-
line instruments, are usually combinations of measuring
sensors and some auxiliary facilities. Taking the method of
tension wire alignment as an example, it is the most import-
ant and common method currently used to measure hori-
zontal displacements of dams, and it is implemented by
measuring the distances between measuring points and the
datum line.

depicts the main components of a tensile-wire
instrument: \(1\ endpoint devices, \(2\ measuring point devi-
ces, \(3\ measuring line, \(4\ protective tube, and \(5\ displace-
ment sensors, among which all are auxiliary facilities except
displacement sensors. Generally, auxiliary facilities only play
the role of providing monitoring conditions, while these dis-
placement sensors perform actual measurement tasks. As
shown in
Figure 2
, a set of tensile-wire instrument is
equipped with several displacement sensors, which allows to
measure the horizontal displacements at multiple measuring
points simultaneously. The raw measured data collected
from displacement sensors which are assembled on the
same set of auxiliary facilities needs to be error-corrected
using the same instrument parameters and data process-
ing method.

To facilitate a standardised description of monitoring-
related information in DSM systems, a semantic data struc-
ture is proposed based on general regulations of dam safety
monitoring, which was introduced in the Subsection 3.1. As

various categories according to different monitoring tasks,
such as deformation monitoring, seepage monitoring, envir-

be identified through specific measured physical quantity
\(e.g., horizontal displacement, vertical displacement, uplift

index systems for comprehensive dam evaluation.
Furthermore, a monitoring item is a sensory network
designed to measure, collect, and send the same variable attri-
bute. Each monitoring item comprises sensors, sensor assem-
blies, and programmable logic controllers \(PLCs\ Sensor
assemblies are defined for representing external-mounted
monitoring instruments, while sensors are used to character-
ise embedded monitoring instruments. Generally, for differ-
ent monitoring items, sensors or sensor assembly are selected
according to the attribute of the measured physical quantity.
However, at least one sensor or sensor assembly is required in
a monitoring item to detect dam behaviour, also at least one
PLC is attached to receive, decode, and then send the meas-
ured data. Moreover, considering the characteristic of exter-
nal-mounted monitoring instruments, monitoring facilities
and sensors are necessary components of a sensor assembly.
Here, the monitoring facility can be regarded as a combin-
ation of all the auxiliary facilities in an external-mounted
monitoring instrument. Finally, semantic references from
sensors to structural models of dams are defined because the
positions of measuring sensors in dam structures are essential
to ensure the meaningfulness of the measured data.

4. Cloud model-based comprehensive evaluation of
dam safety status
4.1. Construction of evaluation index system
Data collection and processing for comprehensive evaluation
of dam behaviour is both labour-intensive and time-
consuming. Till now, all the related information is well-
defined and integrated into dam DTs based on the extended
IFC schema, the time required for information query, as
well as the possibility of errors are reduced \(Ding et al.,
\ However, it is still insufficient to fully support the
evaluation of dam behaviour because diagnostic information
needs to be classified according to the used evaluation index
system. As shown in
Figure 1
, the result of dam safety
evaluation does not depend on the observed characteristic
from a single monitoring item or a single observation point,
but on the comprehensive safety condition derived from all
evaluation indices \(with different weight values\ Usually the
first-level index \(highest level\ is the overall safety status of
the dam being evaluated, other evaluation indices shown in
the second level are determined by possible failure modes of
this dam. To clarity the impact of each failure mode on
dam safety, dam engineers need to categorise various moni-
toring points according to these indices.
The classification process in the traditional mode is per-
formed as follows. First, monitoring data is collected from
databases, as well as the corresponding sensor number,
monitoring type, monitoring physical quantity, etc.
Secondly, position information of this sensor is obtained by
checking the monitoring instrument layout. Thirdly, based
on above information, the index to which this monitoring
point belongs can be derived. During this process, human
intervention is necessary to collect and judge the related
information \(e.g., monitoring type, position, etc.\ of each

4.3.4. The proposed evaluation framework
The proposed framework for comprehensive evaluation of
dam safety is shown in
Figure 8
. I n this solution, data avail-
ability is provided and enhanced by DT-based data integra-
tion. The whole evaluation process is divided into three
parts. First, the first-level assessment is to evaluate the safety
status of each monitoring item, that is, the related monitor-
ing points. For each monitoring point, the raw measuring
data is corrected using correction method and the evalu-
ation criterion or baseline used to indicate the standard
dam response is deduced from the corresponding statistical
model. The input of a statistical model is the consistent
long-term monitoring data, more specifically, the historical
structural response \(e.g., displacement, stress and strain,
joint, etc.\ and corresponding working conditions \(e.g.,
water level, temperature, time effect, etc.\ The outputs are
regression coefficients of a quantitative interpretation func-
tion which represents the real relationship between struc-
tural response and the loads or environment, and a multi-
correlation coefficient which represents the fitting degree of
this statistical model.
Using this model, dam response under certain conditions
can be predicted. According to the difference between the
measured and predicted structural behaviour, the health sta-
tus of a measuring point can be inferred by a multi-rule
cloud reasoning model. The evaluation rules are necessary
to establish a multi-rule cloud reasoning model. For
example, the qualitative rules could be
if the difference is
very small, then the health status is normal
if the differ-
ence is a little large, then the health status is little abnormal
The three digital characteristics
of conditions
and results in rules can be determined by specific dams.
The input of the multi-rule cloud reasoning model is the
difference between the measured and predicted structural
response, the output is an effect score which represents the
safety status of the monitoring point. The inputs of the
improved CRITIC method are the effect scores and multi-
correlation coefficients of all the related monitoring points
in a monitoring item. The outputs are corresponding weight
values. Then, the safety status of the monitoring item is the
weighted average of all effect scores.
Secondly, the second-level evaluation indices are assessed.
In this step, safety status of related monitoring items can be
regarded as influencing factors. The weight values of these
factors are calculated by the entropy weight method
\(EWM\ Then, a multidimensional cloud evaluation model
is established, and the evaluation grades for describing dam
safety status which are expressed as three digital characteris-
are determined by specific dams in advance.
The inputs are safety status of all related monitoring items
and corresponding weight values. The output is a matrix of
membership degree of the safety status for each evaluation
grade. Thirdly, the third-level assessment is to evaluate the
safety status of a whole dam. The weight values of these

6. Conclusions
To achieve the dynamic and proactive mode of dam safety
evaluation, a DT-enabled solution is proposed in this paper.
The results of the case study verify the ability of the pro-
posed approach to provide dynamic information and per-
form continuous dam safety evaluation by taking full
advantage of the standardised monitoring-related informa-
tion in a dam DT. It is beneficial for indicating the overall
safety status, supporting decision-making, and performing
preventive maintenance before severe and even catastrophic
consequences happen during the O&M stage of dam proj-
ects. The main innovations of this paper are the following:
1. With the help of the standardised description of moni-
toring-related information in dam DT environment, the
availability and interoperability of required information
for complex evaluation tasks are enhanced. Compared
with traditional methods of dam management, this
approach can provide reliable and dynamic data to sup-
port continuous safety evaluation.
2. Based on available information and the cloud model
concept, an automated evaluation approach that can
consider uncertainties in the evaluation process, is pro-
posed. This approach can present the general trend of
dam safety status in a certain period. Hence, compared
with the traditional static evaluation mode, it is of vital
significance for diagnosing potential problems and tak-
ing reinforcement measures timely.
Although the proposed comprehensive dam evaluation
approach can realise continuous monitoring of dam
behaviour, as verified in the case study, there are still some
shortcomings existing. For example, IFC entities related
to dam structures like
, and
are not included in the current IFC
schema, which still need further investigation. Meanwhile, the
measurement of evaluation indices is only performed based
on the historical monitoring data, but the FEM-based simula-
tion results are also useful especially when some monitoring
data is missing or contaminated \(Salazar et al.,
However, to realise the complementation of these two sources
of information, further study such as the integration of simu-
lation results into dam DTs and the ingenious combination of
these two completely different kinds of information, is
still required.
