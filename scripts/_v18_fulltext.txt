<-> Abstract
<-> The Digital Twin (DT) concept has attracted popularity as a strategic and new paradigm for the built environment industry seeking to improve efficiency. Yet its application in phases prior to operation stage remains fragmented and whether existing systems can learn and improve their own predictions over time has not been clearly addressed. This review therefore has objectives twofold. First, to map the bibliometric landscape of DT research addressing design and construction phases. Second, to determine the extent to which current DT systems exhibit self-improving and cross-phase capability. In this study, a scientometric analysis was conducted for 152 bibliometrics-searched articles of DT in built environment industry to provide a critical understanding of current research trends and gaps to address. The review found that despite the publication growth in the field, the current studies remain confined to the one-way or static DT and highly concentrated in operations phase. None of current study attain a cognitive self-improving or convergence capability. Research is overwhelmingly single-phase and methods to support autonomy such as reinforcement learning, graph neural networks and large language models, are almost underexplored. These results provide quantified evidence that a self-improving cognitive digital twin (CDT) does not yet exist in built environment industry. In response, this study proposes a CDT framework of which the innovations are threefold: a cross-phase forward predictor linking design geometry to downstream safety and quality risks, leading-indicator-driven learning that improves without waiting for actual accidents, and a cross-project convergence loop that unifies design prediction.
<-> Keywords: Cognitive Digital Twin; Design-for-Safety; Design-for-Quality; Built Environment; Systematic Literature Review; Bibliometric Analysis
<a0> Introduction
<a0> The AECO Industry's Phase-Fragmented Delivery Problem
<-> Digital Twin concept was originally proposed by Grieves and Vickers [1] as a mechanism for mitigating unpredictable emergent behavior in complex systems. It has rapidly evolved into a strategic paradigm for the digital transformation of infrastructure sectors. Within the Architecture, Engineering, Construction, and Operations (AECO) industry specifically, Boje, et al. [2] proposed a semantic construction digital twin framework and explicitly identified lack of cyber-physical integration and cognitive adaptation as critical unaddressed challenges, while Opoku, et al. [3] mapped the diverse but fragmented application landscape across various phases of construction projects. The previous study and discussions on digital twin are enormous and broadly distributed into various phases of life cycle and application fields with dispersed purposes. Building upon these foundational contributions, this study aims to review the current state of DT application and the barriers preventing its further development towards high intelligence.
<a0> Design-for-X as cross-phase analytical lens
<-> Design-for-X (DfX) refers to an approach by which a specified downstream requirement of safety and quality are considered in upstream design decisions. Rather than treating these considerations as corrections to be made after design, Design-for-X seeks to anticipate them when design alternatives can still be evaluated and modified to achieve maximized total benefits in full life cycle [4].
<-> In the AECO industry, performance of design decisions may not become observable until materialized in construction or operation. Permanent features such as openings, shafts, façade interfaces, access constraints and complex component connections can subsequently generate temporary-work requirements, work-at-height exposure, installation difficulties, defects or rework. Previous studies have accordingly examined Digital Twins for construction safety-risk management and quality management, although these applications remain dispersed across project phases [5, 6]. Existing Construction Digital Twin research has also identified lifecycle integration and the return of downstream information to earlier decision stages as unresolved requirements [2, 3, 7].
<-> This review deliberately adopts safety and quality as analytical lenses and observable outcome domains through which cross-phase DT capability can be examined. Safety consequences can be traced through hazard assessments, near-miss records, protection requirements and incident records [8, 9], while quality consequences can be observed through dimensional deviations, inspection records, non-conformance and rework [10, 11]. As such, it is now available two traceable pathways for investigating forward prediction from design to construction and operation, and backward learning from realised outcomes. Within the proposed CDT, Design for Safety (DfS) and Design for Quality (DfQ) initially serve as prediction targets, while the underlying cross-phase learning architecture remains extensible to other Design-for-X objectives.
<a0> Preliminary Research Gaps
<-> An evident conceptual-implementation gap exists in widely cited application frameworks. First, existing cognitive DT frameworks propose self-improvement conceptually but do not formalise convergence. For examples, Tao, et al. [7] proposes a conceptual function is proposed to describe the digital twin MDT= f (Physical Entity, Virtual Entity, services; DT Data, connections) but it lacks convergence. Zheng, et al. [12] introduces iterative optimisation concept yet does not demonstrate self-updating mechanism nor convergence criterion. A contrasting variant of the same gap is that Schooling, et al. [13] in their DT-enabled anomaly-detection system for building O&M, adopt Bayesian on-line change-point detection deliberately because it does not require "prior knowledge of pre-change or post-change operation processes". It contains no mechanism for the digital twin to update its own model from operational data, leaving the self-improving convergence unaddressed. Second, existing closed-loop BIM by Pärn, et al. [14] feeds operation data back episodically without iteration. Knowledge-based feedback loop is proposed that return operational performance data from the O&M phase to designers and contractors but at one time without iteration, self-learning and is not instantiated. The mechanism by which such feedback would be continuously converged toward improved cross-phase decisions is left entirely unaddressed. Based on the above preliminary findings, it is initially observed that cross-phase DT with forward prediction and convergence and backward self-improvement is underexplored.

<-> Figure 2
<af8> Figure 3 Publication Trends in themes (Scopus)
<-> Figure 3 presents the overlay visualization of the keyword co-occurrence network, with node colour representing the average publication year of each keyword (ranging from 2023.0 to 2025.0). This temporal overlay reveals three distinct thematic phases in the recent evolution of DT-in-AECO research.
<-> Phase 1 Foundational Consolidation (2023, purple nodes)
<-> The earliest thematic cluster visible in the corpus is anchored by cyber-physical system, embedded systems, virtual models, computer architecture, mixed reality, 3d modeling, neural network, condition monitoring, and occupational risks. These keywords represent the mature foundational infrastructure of DT research, upon which more recent developments have built.
<-> Phase 2 — Current Mainstream (2024, green nodes)
<-> The central cluster of the network is dominated by digital twin, BIM, safety, machine learning, artificial intelligence, IoT, real-time, deep learning, risk assessment, infrastructure, construction industry, and lifecycle management. This phase represents the active mainstream of contemporary research, characterized by BIM–DT–AI integration for construction management and safety applications.
<-> Phase 3 — Emerging Frontier (2025, yellow nodes)
<-> The most recent thematic cluster features reinforcement learning, defect detection, damage detection, structural safety, structural health monitoring, predictive maintenance, intelligent construction, construction operations, image segmentation, point-clouds, blockchain, and data privacy. These nodes represent the current research frontier, characterized by the integration of reinforcement learning with defect and damage detection.
<-> The temporal overlay reveals two critical structural observations relevant to the research gap identified in this review. First, the keywords most closely aligned with the present dissertation's objectives—reinforcement learning (Objective 2's core methodology) and defect detection (Objective 1's primary prediction target)—both exhibit the highest average publication years (>2024), positioning the proposed research directly within the emerging research frontier. Second, the notable absence from the visualization of keywords explicitly denoting cognitive digital twin, self-improving, or cross-phase convergence, all below the analysis threshold of 5 occurrences, empirically confirms that these specific formulations remain new even within the 2025 frontier. This dual observation is that the emergence of adjacent enabling technologies alongside the absence of integrated cognitive-convergence frameworks defines the precise theoretical gaps that the proposed research objective seeks to address.
<a0> Keyword Co-Occurrence Map by Scopus
<af8> Figure 4 Keyword Co-occurrence Network (Scopus)
<-> The keyword co-occurrence network is constructed using VOSviewer with the full-counting method, applying a minimum occurrence threshold of 5 to 3,786 unique keywords after thesaurus-based normalisation, resulting in 109 keywords retained. The analysis identified four thematic clusters, revealing the intellectual structure of digital twin research in the AECO industry.
<-> Cluster 1 Digital Twin Core, AI, and Automated Sensing (red):
<-> Anchored by digital twin (257 occurrences, the largest node) and integrating safety (52 occurrences), machine learning, deep learning, real-time, and reinforcement learning, this cluster represents the intellectual core of the field. It integrates AI-driven sensing technologies (3D modelling, point cloud, photogrammetry, object detection, image segmentation) with construction safety applications (safety monitoring, defect detection, damage detection, structural safety) and specific engineering contexts (construction equipment, intelligent construction, railway, excavation).
<-> Cluster 2 Risk Assessment, SHM, and Accident Prevention (blue):
<-> Featuring risk assessment, risk management, risk analysis, risk perception, hazard, disaster, resilience, and accident prevention, this cluster represents the DfS-oriented risk analytics stream. Structural monitoring aspects are represented by structural health monitoring, structural analysis, bridge, and tunnel. Methodological support is provided by Bayesian networks, finite element method, mixed reality, and embedded systems. This cluster reflects the field's transition from descriptive safety monitoring toward predictive, model-based risk analytics.
<-> Cluster 3 — Infrastructure DT and Cybersecurity (yellow):
<-> Centred on infrastructure, predictive maintenance, asset management, and smart city, this cluster addresses DT deployment at the infrastructure scale. Emerging operational concerns include cybersecurity, interoperability, edge computing, and data privacy. Transportation infrastructure sub-topics (motor transportation, roads and streets) form a distinct peripheral community within this cluster, reflecting DT applications in intelligent transport systems.
<-> Cluster 4 — BIM, AI Decision Support, and Smart Building (green):
<-> Comprising BIM (75 occurrences, the second-largest node), construction industry, information management, decision making, artificial intelligence, IoT, big data, and lifecycle management, this cluster represents the BIM-anchored DT paradigm. It extends into immersive technologies (augmented reality, virtual reality, virtual models), emerging technologies (blockchain, electronic data interchange), and sustainability applications (smart building, sustainability, built environment, energy efficiency, quality control). This cluster reflects the traditional information-management view of DT as a BIM extension supporting whole life-cycle decision-making.
<-> The network reveals three important structural observations. First, digital twin occupies the geometric centre of the network with the highest total link strength, indicating that DT-in-AECO research is thematically well-integrated rather than fragmented across sub-fields. Second, the two largest secondary nodes of BIM and safety sit at the boundary of Clusters 1, 2, and 4, confirming BIM and safety as the dual foundational concepts organising the field. Third, the network reveals a notable absence of dominant nodes of self-improving, cognitive, or convergence-aware DT capabilities. Therefore, research gap is empirically confirmed that despite widespread DT adoption in AECO, self-improving cross-phase DT frameworks with convergence nature remain underexplored.

Xu, et al. [42] (a review of DT about concepts, technologies and industrial applications.), RASHEED, et al. This cluster functions as the field's meta-theoretical foundation, integrating the original DT formulation with methodological references and comprehensive review works.
<-> Three observations can emerge from the co-citation network. First, the distributed multi-centric structure across six clusters reflects the ongoing theoretical consolidation of DT-in-AECO. Second, several references, notably Boje, et al. [2], [30], and Grieves and Vickers [1] appear in multiple clusters, indicating their role as integrative works that bridge distinct theoretical fields. Third, Cluster 6 simultaneously contains the field's conceptual origin Grieves [25], Grieves and Vickers [1] (conceptual origin of DT + emergent-behavior mitigation framework), and adjacent methodological of reinforcement learning (RL) Sutton and Barto [26]. The presence of an RL reference in the field's foundational position confirms that RL is already recognized as an adjacent methodological tradition for DT-in-AECO applications. Concurrently, the size of Cluster 6 is much smaller application-oriented Clusters 1 to 3, which confirms that the cognitive and RL-based DT remains a substantially underdeveloped frontier.

<a0> Cross database robustness alignment
<-> The Scopus and WoS corpora are analysed independently and their keyword co-occurrence networks are compared as robustness check. Both of the two networks, generated from non-overlapping bibliographic sources, exhibit convergent structure comprising of an identical central hub of digital twin, the same dual secondary nodes of BIM and safety, and mutually consistent thematic clusters spanning AI-driven sensing, structural health monitoring, risk-oriented safety analytics and BIM decision support. This cross-database consistency indicates that the observed intellectual structure is a stable property of the field rather than an artefact of database coverage. Most consequentially, the absence of cognitive, self-improving, and convergence-related keywords is confirmed, substantially strengthens the identified research gap.

<a0> Discussion
<a0> Summary of main findings
<af2> This review combined bibliometric mapping with full-text thematic analysis to examine the development, current capabilities and methodological orientation of Digital Twin research addressing safety and quality in the Architecture, Engineering, Construction and Operations industry. The results reveal a rapidly expanding but still theoretically immature field. Publication output increased sharply after 2019, while the temporal keyword maps show that reinforcement learning, object detection, damage detection and predictive analytics have emerged only recently. The Scopus and Web of Science analyses produced broadly consistent thematic structures centred on Digital Twins, Building Information Modelling and safety, with related clusters covering automated sensing, structural health monitoring, risk assessment and decision support. However, terminology associated with cognitive capability, self-improvement and convergence remained absent or below the respective occurrence thresholds in both databases. This cross-database consistency indicates that the observed gaps are not attributable solely to the coverage of a particular database.
<af2> The full-text analysis further demonstrates a substantial difference between the claimed potential of Digital Twins and their realised capability. Among the 152 included studies, 31 were classified as Tier 1 Digital Models, 92 as Tier 2 Digital Shadows and 21 as Tier 3 bidirectional Digital Twins, while none reached Tier 4 Cognitive or Self-improving capability. The dominance of Tier 2 indicates that most existing systems support automated data transfer from physical assets to virtual representations but do not close the loop through continuous model adaptation. This interpretation is reinforced by the self-improvement assessment: 138 studies included no self-improvement mechanism, 10 mentioned the concept without implementation, only four implemented a limited updating mechanism, and none explicitly characterised convergence. The current research base therefore supports monitoring, visualisation and project-specific prediction more strongly than sustained learning across successive projects.
<af2> Safety constituted the principal application domain, with 134 studies addressing Design for Safety, compared with 10 addressing Design for Quality and eight addressing both. Nevertheless, this emphasis did not translate into strong design-stage integration. Only five studies covered the design phase, whereas 82 addressed construction and 72 addressed operation; 147 studies remained confined to a single lifecycle phase, and only five spanned multiple phases. Consequently, downstream safety incidents, defects and operational anomalies are commonly detected after they have materialised, while their relationships with originating design features remain insufficiently represented. Validation was similarly concentrated at the project level: 104 studies relied on a single case study, while only eight were evaluated across multiple real projects.
<af2> The findings also distinguish multimodal data availability from cognitive learning capability. Although 136 studies used two or more data types, multimodal integration was generally applied to improve sensing, representation or prediction within an individual application. It rarely supports cross-phase reasoning, transferable knowledge or iterative model improvement. Advanced approaches with potential to support these capabilities, including graph neural networks, large language models, reinforcement learning and Bayesian methods, remained sparsely represented in the reviewed corpus. Taken together, the results indicate that the principal limitation is no longer simply the availability of Building Information Modelling, sensor, image, point-cloud or textual data. The more fundamental limitation is the absence of an architecture that connects design features with downstream outcomes, retains learning across projects and evaluates whether successive model updates produce measurable improvement.
<af2> These findings provide the empirical basis for the unexplored areas and future directions presented in the following section. In particular, they point to the need for a Digital Twin that extends beyond real-time synchronisation by combining cross-phase prediction, multimodal reasoning, proxy-based model updating and cross-project convergence. These requirements subsequently inform the proposed Self-Improving Cognitive Digital Twin framework.

<a0> Conclusions
<-> This review set out to map the landscape of digital-twin research addressing design-for-safety and design-for-quality in the AECO sector, and to determine the extent to which existing systems exhibit self-improving, cross-phase, and convergence-aware capabilities. Four conclusions emerge. First, DT-in-AECO research has expanded exponentially since 2019 yet remains an exceptionally young field, as revealed by both keyword co-occurrence and co-citation analyses, because the knowledge graph has not yet consolidated into a mature canon. Second, the technologies are dominated by computer-vision and deep-learning techniques borrowed from adjacent domains of computer science. However, the approaches capable of supporting autonomous digital twin, including graph neural networks, large language models, reinforcement learning, agent-based modelling and their integration with DT, remain underexplored. Third, the overwhelming majority of DT implementations are limited to the digital-shadow and bidirectional-twin tiers, operate within a single lifecycle phase of O&M, and incorporate no mechanism by which the twin updates its own predictive model. Fourth, the review reveals the distinct theoretical, methodological, and empirical gap.
<-> The central conclusion of this review is that, despite the widespread adoption of digital twins across the AECO industry, a self-improving, convergence-aware cognitive digital twin capable of cross-phase Design-for-X forecasting does not yet exist. This review thereby establishes both the necessity and the design requirements for the Self-Improving Cognitive Digital Twin.
